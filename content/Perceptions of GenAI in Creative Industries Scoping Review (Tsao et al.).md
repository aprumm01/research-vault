# Perceptions of GenAI in Creative Industries: Scoping Review (Tsao et al.)

**DOI:** [10.1145/3706599.3719931](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706599.3719931)

## Summary
Tsao et al. conduct a scoping review of empirical research on how creative industry professionals — in design, advertising, film, music, and fashion — perceive and respond to generative AI tools. Synthesizing findings across 40+ studies, the review identifies consistent patterns: initial curiosity followed by concerns about professional identity, quality of AI-generated work, intellectual property, and the devaluation of human creative labor. The review maps the landscape of attitudes from enthusiastic integration to active resistance, organized by creative domain and professional role.

## Key Concepts
- Creative industry professional perceptions of GenAI
- Professional identity threat and adaptation
- Intellectual property and AI-generated creative work
- Human creative labor devaluation
- Scoping review methodology

## Theoretical Framework
Uses scoping review methods (Arksey and O'Malley); organizes findings through job crafting and professional identity frameworks; considers threat appraisal theory to explain the range of defensive and adaptive responses to GenAI.

## Methods
Systematic scoping review; database searches across multiple disciplines; inclusion/exclusion criteria focused on empirical studies of professional perceptions; thematic synthesis across creative domains.

## Main Arguments
- Creative professionals across domains share a cluster of concerns about GenAI: authenticity, skill devaluation, attribution, and displacement — though the relative salience varies by domain
- Design and advertising professionals show more pragmatic adoption than fine art or music professionals, whose identity is more deeply tied to the creative act itself
- IP and attribution concerns are nearly universal among professional creatives, even among those who actively use GenAI tools
- Resistance to GenAI is not uniform technophobia — it is often a principled response to perceived threats to craft, livelihood, and cultural value

## Limitations
- Rapidly evolving landscape; perceptions reported in studies from 2022-2024 may already be outdated as tools and industry norms evolve
- Publication bias toward English-language, Western creative industries contexts
