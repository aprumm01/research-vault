# Labor, Automation, and Ownership: What the Vault Actually Says

> *A synthesis of vault literature on AI, labor displacement, and the political economy of ownership — May 2026.*

---

## The Core Framework: Zuboff's Two Faces

[[papers/Automate Infomate Two Faces of Intelligent Technology (Zuboff 1985)|Zuboff (1985)]] is the vault's most-connected node for a reason. She identifies the fundamental fork in how intelligent technology gets deployed:

- **Automate** — replace human labor, eliminate the worker from the loop, extract value by reducing headcount
- **Infomate** — generate information *about* the process, making it legible to workers and augmenting their understanding and control

The owner's choice between these two faces isn't neutral or technical. It's a political decision about who gets power. Zuboff's argument is that management overwhelmingly chooses automate even when infomate would produce better outcomes — because infomate shifts knowledge (and therefore leverage) toward workers.

This is the 1985 version of the question. Replace "intelligent technology" with "AI" and the structure is identical. The question of whether AI tools empower workers or displace them is not a question about capability — it's a question about who controls the deployment decision.

## The Mechanism: Braverman Through Attewell

[[papers/The Deskilling Controversy (Attewell, 1987)|Attewell (1987)]] reviews Braverman's deskilling thesis carefully before critiquing it — and even in critique, the mechanism he describes is damning. Braverman identifies three interlocking drives that shape how capitalism organizes labor:

**The Babbage Principle:** Divide complex craft work into simple steps so cheaper, less-skilled labor can fill each step individually. Cost, not efficiency, drives the division.

**The Control Imperative:** Management needs to convert workers' *capacity* to work into work *actually done*. The way to ensure this is to appropriate workers' knowledge and leave them as executors of someone else's plan.

Attewell quotes Braverman directly on the result:

> "Management has grasped control over the production process for itself by gaining knowledge of production and by reducing workers to mere executors of work. By reorganizing most work into narrow, low-skilled jobs with no conceptual content, the majority of workers become dependent on management, which has 'appropriated' the intellectual skills once held by craft labor."

This separation of *conception* from *execution* is the core move. The craftsperson who once held both — who knew *why* as well as *how* — becomes a detail worker following instructions generated elsewhere. The intelligence lives in management's domain; the worker provides only physical compliance.

Applied to AI: if the model handles conception (generating plans, drafts, code, designs) and the worker executes prompts and edits outputs, the Braverman structure is reproduced at the cognitive level. The intelligence has been "appropriated" into a system owned by the company. Workers become prompt executors.

## Intellectual Assembly Lines: Perrolle 1984

[[papers/Intellectual Assembly Lines The Rationalization of Managerial, Professional, and Technical Work (Perrolle, 1984)|Perrolle (1984)]] named the trajectory with precision that reads as prophecy today:

> "An intellectual assembly line is a division of labor in which the rationality of the bureaucratic organization acquires the mechanized efficiency of the factory, and in which mental labor is subjected to both the rationalization of its knowledge and the gradual automation of its productive activity."

Her argument: the same logic that turned craft labor into factory labor in the industrial revolution was already being applied to professional, managerial, and technical work via computing. Expert systems encode judgment; decision-support systems encode deliberation; AI encodes creativity. Each step moves conception further from the worker and into a system owned by the organization.

> "Technical, professional, and managerial work all involve the exercise of expert knowledge. Also involved in professional and managerial jobs are autonomous professional judgments based upon experience... To argue these mental activities can be organized in assembly line fashion presumes that computers can perform as technical experts, can acquire a kind of judgment based upon general principles and experience, and can make managerial decisions. These are precisely the claims of the research area known as artificial intelligence."

She wrote this in 1984. The "claims" she flagged as speculative are now partially realized — and the political economy she predicted (intellectual labor subjected to Taylorist rationalization) has followed.

The implication: when designers use AI to generate initial concepts, or when writers use it to draft, or when researchers use it to synthesize — the intellectual assembly line is operational. The worker refines; the model conceives. Perrolle's framework says this is not a partnership — it's a reorganization of who owns the knowledge layer.

## The Empirical Data: Kristal 2013

[[papers/The Capitalist Machine Computerization, Workers' Power, and Labor's Share (Kristal, 2013)|Kristal (2013)]] provides what the theoretical literature can only argue: empirical evidence that computerization has functioned as class-biased technology, systematically shifting income from labor to capital.

> "Since the late 1970s, labor's share declined by 6 percent across the U.S. private sector... I argue that the main factor leading to the decline in labor's share was the erosion in workers' positional power, and this erosion was partly an outcome of class-biased technological change, namely computerization that favored employers over most employees."

The phrase **class-biased technological change** is doing significant theoretical work. It rejects the neutral-tool framing (technology is just a tool; outcomes depend on how we use it) and names the pattern: when employers control deployment decisions, technology systematically favors their interests over workers' interests.

Kristal traces this through 43 industries across nearly four decades:

> "This overall decline was due to a large decline (5 to 14 percent) in construction, manufacturing, and transportation combined with an increase, albeit small (2 to 5 percent), in labor's share within finance and services industries."

The industries where workers gained were finance and services — sectors where computerization augmented professional work rather than replacing physical labor. The industries where workers lost were those where computerization enabled direct substitution. This maps exactly onto the automate/infomate distinction Zuboff draws — and shows which face of technology capital has historically preferred.

Kristal also identifies the mechanism: **erosion of positional power**, measured by unionization rates, unemployment levels, capital concentration, and import penetration. The technology didn't just displace workers — it weakened the structural conditions under which workers could negotiate.

## The Ideology: Katz 2017

[[papers/Manufacturing an AI Revolution (Katz 2017)|Katz (2017)]] argues that the "AI revolution" is not a neutral description of technical progress — it's a manufactured narrative that serves specific corporate and political interests. His core argument:

> "Corporations have helped manufacture an 'AI revolution' in which AI stands for a confused mix of terms — such as 'big data,' 'machine learning,' or 'deep learning' — whose common denominator is the use of expensive computing power to analyze massive centralized data."

And the political function of the revolution narrative:

> "By uncritically accepting that the 'age of AI' is upon us, many discussions have basically adopted the framing set by these major corporations. The manufactured AI revolution has created the false impression that current systems have surpassed human abilities to the point where many areas of life, from scientific inquiry to the court system, might be best run by machines."

This "surpassed human abilities" framing is critical. If AI is superhuman, human labor is irrelevant — the question of who owns the AI is no longer a labor question but a governance question. Workers become bystanders to a process they couldn't participate in anyway.

The Carnegie parallel Katz draws is precise: "We're democratizing AI" / "AI will empower individuals" follows the exact structure of Carnegie's "the millionaire will be but a trustee for the poor." The owner presents as benevolent steward of collective benefit, obscuring the fact that they own the means of production and extract the surplus value.

## Technology as Political Artifact: Winner 1980

[[papers/Do Artifacts Have Politics (Winner 1980)|Winner (1980)]] provides the philosophical grounding. He distinguishes two ways technologies acquire political properties:

**First:** The design of a technical system becomes a way of resolving a political contest — encoding a particular power arrangement into material form so it no longer appears political.

**Second:** Some technologies are inherently compatible with certain social structures and incompatible with others. Winner quotes Mumford on the two persistent technological traditions: one authoritarian, system-centered, immensely powerful but inherently unstable; the other democratic, relatively weak but resourceful and durable.

Winner notes the ideological move that has always accompanied new technology:

> "Scarcely a new invention comes along that someone doesn't proclaim it as the salvation of a free society."

This pattern — each new technology heralded as democratizing — is precisely what Katz documents for AI. The proclamation functions to preempt critique. If AI is liberation, asking who owns it is ingratitude.

Applied here: AI isn't politically neutral pending its deployment context. Centralized, proprietary, opaque AI systems have structural affinities with centralized power — the same way Winner argues nuclear power has structural affinities with authoritarian governance. The political properties are partly baked in by design choices — choices made by owners.

## The Historical Pattern: Technological Unemployment and Ideology

[[papers/Inventing Ourselves Out of Jobs America's Debate over Technological Unemployment (Review, 2001)|Cross/Bix (2001)]] documents a recurring cycle that the current moment is reprising:

> "Many Americans have forgotten the debate over the impact of technology on jobs. The extraordinarily long business boom of the 1990s has suppressed a central theme of modern industrialization — business use of new technologies to reduce labor costs."

The 1930s debate: labor argued that mechanization deskilled and displaced workers; business and engineers insisted technology created new jobs. When labor demanded reduced working hours to absorb displaced workers, business responded with a "well-oiled publicity machine" creating a picture of limitless technological innovation.

The parallel for AI is precise. Labor-adjacent voices are raising displacement concerns; AI companies are running a remarkably similar publicity operation (demos, benchmarks, "the jobs it creates haven't been invented yet"). The demand for reduced working hours has historical precedent — and was historically defeated not by argument but by embedding a consumption ideology that made work identity-constitutive.

Bix's central observation, per Cross: "this debate could never be resolved for ideological and statistical reasons." The same will likely be true for AI and labor — not because the empirical question is unanswerable, but because both sides are embedded in political projects that precede the technical facts.

## The Tension: Micro-Ownership vs. Macro-Concentration

The genuinely new thing about AI (compared to prior automation waves) is the combination of:

1. **Unprecedented individual leverage** — a single person with AI tools can produce outputs that previously required a team, a budget, and specialized skills across multiple domains
2. **Unprecedented ownership concentration** — the means of producing that leverage (foundation models, compute infrastructure, RLHF pipelines) are controlled by a smaller number of entities than any prior production technology

**The micro-ownership layer** (Zuboff's infomate path in individual hands):
- A solo designer produces what once required a team → they gain productive leverage previously available only to those who could hire
- A single researcher synthesizes a literature in hours rather than months
- An individual developer ships products at a pace that once required an engineering org
- At this layer, AI genuinely democratizes *production capacity*

**The macro-ownership layer** (Braverman + Kristal's structural reality):
- The means of AI production — foundation models, training infrastructure, RLHF pipelines — are owned by a small number of companies
- [[papers/Animo Nullius AI Origin Story and Data Colonialism (Penn 2023)|Penn (2023)]] adds the data colonialism dimension: these models were trained on the collective output of human intellectual labor across the internet — centuries of writing, code, art, conversation — without compensation to the "workers" who generated that training data. This is primitive accumulation applied to the cognitive commons.
- AI company employees produce AI systems; the company owns the output; surplus value flows up. Same as any wage relation — except the product they build then competes with or replaces the labor of others.

**The parallel:**
- Factory owners gave workers access to machines no individual could own → workers gained productive leverage while surrendering surplus value to capital
- AI companies give individuals access to models no individual could train → the same structural arrangement, at a scale and speed that makes prior rounds look incremental

**The contradiction:**
- The individual using AI tools *feels* like an owner — they produce more, faster, with fewer constraints
- But they are executing on a system designed, owned, and continuously updated by someone else
- Braverman's "executor of work" applies: the conception (what the model knows, how it reasons, what it optimizes for) lives in the owner's domain; the individual provides only the prompting and editing

This is not a reason to refuse AI tools any more than it was a reason for 19th-century workers to refuse factory machines. But it is a reason to be precise about what "democratization" means in this context: **access to productive capacity is not the same as ownership of the means of production.**

## What's Missing from the Vault

The vault has the critical framework (Zuboff), the structural mechanism (Braverman through Attewell), the empirical evidence (Kristal), the ideological critique (Katz), and the philosophical grounding (Winner). What it lacks:

- **Platform cooperativism / worker ownership of AI infrastructure** — proposals for alternative ownership structures
- **Data dividend frameworks** — proposals to compensate the people whose labor trained the models
- **Penn (2023) fully developed** — data colonialism as the specific form of primitive accumulation in AI (in the vault but not deeply connected yet)
- **The AI-company-employee angle** — the workers who build AI systems are themselves in a traditional wage relation to the owners of those systems; this hasn't been theorized in the vault's literature

The synthesis of micro-ownership enabling individual leverage while macro-ownership concentrates at a scale that makes prior capitalist consolidation look modest — is well-supported by the vault's material but isn't yet present in the literature itself. That's a gap in the field, not just in the vault.
