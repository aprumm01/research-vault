# Research on Teaching Methods for Computer Operating Systems Integrating AI and Structured Seminars

## Summary
integration, and PPT generation, further promoting interaction and cooperation in the classroom As a basic course for information security majors, computer operating system helps students master the basic principles and operation skills of computer operating system the traditional teaching mode of this course is implemented by the teacher speaking and the students listening.

## Key Concepts
- Structured seminar methodology combined with AI integration for computer operating systems education
- Traditional passive learning model (teacher speaking, students listening) limiting student engagement and interaction
- Five-step teaching process: defining topics, organizing groups, conducting three-part seminars, presentations/feedback, teacher summaries
- AI applied at each stage including topic definition, group formation, content integration, and PPT generation
- Three-part seminar structure (Introduction-Exposition-Summary) scaffolding student-led learning
- Information security major curriculum positioning operating systems as foundational course
- Active learning promotion through AI-supported group work and collaborative presentations
- Teacher-student interaction enhancement through technology-mediated classroom activities

## Theoretical Framework
The methodology addresses limitations of traditional lecture-based pedagogy by synthesizing structured collaborative learning with AI augmentation. Drawing on active learning principles, the approach positions students as knowledge co-constructors rather than passive recipients. Structured seminars provide predictable format reducing cognitive load while AI tools support logistics (group formation), content development (research assistance, PPT generation), and presentation quality. The three-part seminar structure (Introduction-Exposition-Summary) mirrors effective presentation patterns enabling peer teaching. AI integration serves dual purpose: (1) reducing administrative burden allowing instructors to focus on facilitation and formative feedback, and (2) providing scaffolding for student groups tackling complex technical content. The framework assumes that increased interaction, collaborative responsibility, and structured peer teaching produce deeper understanding than traditional methods.

## Methods
literature review

## Main Arguments
- Traditional passive learning in operating systems courses produces poor teaching effects and limited student engagement
- Structured seminar methodology provides predictable format enabling students to prepare for and lead learning sessions
- AI integration at multiple teaching stages enhances both efficiency and educational quality through topic generation, group optimization, and content support
- Three-part seminar structure (Introduction-Exposition-Summary) scaffolds effective peer teaching and knowledge construction
- Group presentations with AI-generated visual aids promote both interaction and cooperation in classroom setting
- Teacher role shifts from primary knowledge transmitter to facilitator and synthesizer when AI supports student-led seminars
- Information security students benefit from active engagement with operating system principles rather than passive lecture attendance

## Limitations & Critiques
- Limited empirical evidence provided for effectiveness claims beyond general teaching effect improvement
- No comparison with alternative active learning approaches to isolate benefits of AI integration versus seminar structure
- Unclear how AI tools are pedagogically integrated versus simply automating administrative tasks
- Does not address potential over-reliance on AI-generated content reducing students' independent research and synthesis skills
- Group formation optimization through AI may obscure important teamwork challenges students need to navigate
- Three-part seminar structure may become formulaic limiting pedagogical flexibility for different learning objectives
- Lacks discussion of assessment strategies for evaluating individual versus group learning in AI-supported seminars
- May not generalize beyond information security context to other computer science or technical education domains
