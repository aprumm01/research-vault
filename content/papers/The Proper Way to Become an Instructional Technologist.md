# The Proper Way to Become an Instructional Technologist

## Summary

This reflective essay by Lloyd Rieber, originally delivered as the Peter Dean Lecture in 1998 and updated with a 2023 prologue, explores the diverse pathways into the instructional technology profession. Inspired by Robert Heinich's 1984 article "The Proper Study of Educational Technology," Rieber examines multiple metaphorical roles that instructional technologists might adopt: computer scientist, philosopher, physicist/mathematician, and graduate of an IT program. Rather than advocating for a single "proper" path, Rieber argues that the field's strength lies in its diversity of backgrounds and perspectives.

The essay draws heavily on Rieber's own unconventional journey—from engineering student to elementary school teacher in rural New Mexico to professor at the University of Georgia. He uses this personal narrative to illustrate how the interplay of varied experiences shapes one's approach to instructional design. Central to his argument is the tension between theory and practice in the field, noting that instructional design is inherently contextual and situated, making the relationship between theoretical models and actual practice "messy and very idiosyncratic."

Rieber concludes by offering two lists: one describing what instructional technologists do (help people learn, solve educational problems, use diverse tools including both technologies and design principles) and another outlining what aspiring professionals should focus on (working with people, studying design processes, understanding the interdependency of theory/research/practice, learning media tools, and embracing play). He emphasizes that there is no single correct path and encourages both students and faculty to question the purpose and relevance of their graduate curricula.

## Key Concepts

- **Instructional Technology (IT)**: A field focused on helping people learn through problem-solving, tools (both technological and conceptual), and design principles. The author notes the field has been called various names including instructional systems, educational technology, and learning, design, and technology.

- **Theory-Practice Gap**: The problematic relationship between theoretical instructional design models and actual situated practice, where practitioners often make ad hoc decisions that deviate from prescribed theories.

- **SimSchool**: A simulation prototype Rieber created to let students explore the implications of objectivist vs. constructivist philosophical orientations on educational outcomes.

- **Studio-Based Approach**: A pedagogical method pioneered at the University of Georgia where students learn instructional design by actually "doing" it, rather than solely through coursework.

- **Constructive/Reflective Research**: Research that informs the researcher through deeper conceptual understanding of the topic, beyond just contributing to the literature.

## Theoretical Framework

Rieber draws on multiple theoretical traditions including:
- Piagetian learning theory (particularly through his experience with LOGO programming)
- The objectivism vs. constructivism debate in educational philosophy
- Flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi) regarding play and engagement
- The Wright Brothers metaphor for understanding the relationship between theory, research, and practice—they were simultaneously scientists, inventors, and practitioners

The essay does not propose a new theoretical framework but rather reflects on how various theories inform (and sometimes fail to inform) practice.

## Methods

This is a reflective/philosophical essay rather than an empirical study. The methodology is autobiographical narrative combined with critical analysis of the field's assumptions about professional preparation. Rieber uses his own career trajectory and teaching experiences as primary data sources.

## Main Arguments

- There is no single "proper" pathway to becoming an instructional technologist—the diversity of backgrounds enriches the profession
- A degree alone does not make one an instructional technologist; grounding in authentic contexts and problems is essential
- The field often suffers from "physics envy" and gets caught up in philosophical debates that may not be productive uses of time
- Theory and practice have an inherently problematic relationship that will never be fully resolved because instructional design is situated activity
- Graduate programs should prepare students for a life of learning, not just specific technical skills
- Research serves multiple purposes: contributing to literature AND informing the researcher's own deeper understanding
- Instructional technologists should design "with" end users, not "for" them

## Limitations & Critiques

- The essay is highly autobiographical, drawing primarily from one person's idiosyncratic career path (Penn State Ph.D., elementary teaching, University of Georgia faculty)
- The perspectives may not generalize well to non-American educational contexts (Rieber acknowledges wanting to hear from those in non-American programs)
- Written originally in 1998, some references to technology (LOGO, 3.5" floppy disks) are dated, though the 2023 prologue partially addresses this
- The essay does not engage deeply with empirical research on professional preparation in the field
- The dismissal of philosophical debates may oversimplify genuinely important epistemological questions that inform design decisions
