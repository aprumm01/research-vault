# Manufacturing an Artificial Intelligence Revolution (Katz, 2017)

# Study Partner Note: Katz (2017) - Manufacturing an Artificial Intelligence Revolution

## Summary
Katz argues that the recent "AI revolution" is largely a corporate-manufactured rebranding of big data analysis rather than a genuine breakthrough in machine intelligence. He contends this rebranding obscures neoliberal governance agendas and allows tech companies to position themselves as architects of society while masking questions of institutional power and inequality.

## Key Concepts
- **AI rebranding**: Conflation of big data, machine learning, and deep learning under the "AI" label for corporate interests
- **Behaviorist view of intelligence**: The narrow, empiricist conception of cognition underlying modern AI systems
- **View from nowhere**: Technical systems that claim objectivity while masking race, gender, and class dimensions
- **Algorithmic accountability**: Frame that redirects institutional critiques toward technical fixes rather than power structures
- **AI winter**: Historical period (late 1980s) when AI funding/enthusiasm collapsed after overpromising

## Theoretical Framework
Katz employs a critical STS (Science and Technology Studies) perspective, drawing on the idea that scientific truth is "clothed in technical capacity and institutional power" (via Jan Sapp). He interrogates how corporate interests reshape academic fields and technical vocabularies to serve political ends.

## Methods
The paper is a critical essay/literature review that examines the historiography of AI, analyzes corporate influence on academic research, and critiques contemporary AI discourse through textual analysis of media coverage and institutional claims.

## Main Arguments
- The "AI revolution" narrative is misleading; it reflects corporate rebranding of big data rather than genuine scientific breakthroughs
- Tech companies have actively manufactured AI hype by hiring academics, dominating discourse, and creating false impressions of super-human AI capabilities
- Current AI systems rest on a reductive, behaviorist account of intelligence that cannot address human thought or social complexities
- The confusion about AI capabilities serves political interests by reframing institutional power dynamics as technical/algorithmic problems to be managed by experts

## Limitations & Critiques
The paper is primarily conceptual/polemical and lacks empirical data on corporate hiring patterns or detailed case studies of specific AI deployments. It could be challenged for occasionally treating "corporate interests" as monolithic and for underspecifying what a non-rebranded, legitimate AI advancement would entail.
