# Digital Hoarding and Personal Use Digital Data: Developing and Validating a New Measure

## Summary

This paper develops and validates the Digital Behaviors for Personal Use Questionnaire (DBPUQ), a new psychometric instrument to measure digital hoarding behaviors in personal contexts. The study (N=167) addresses the gap between clinical digital hoarding measures and everyday digital accumulation behaviors, finding that personal digital hoarding correlates with emotional attachment to files, difficulty deleting, and psychological distress. The research also highlights sustainability implications, estimating that unwanted photos alone generate 10.6 kg CO2 annually per UK adult.

## Research Overview

**Research Questions:**
1. Can digital hoarding behaviors in personal use contexts be reliably measured?
2. How do personal digital hoarding tendencies relate to emotional attachment, deletion difficulty, and distress?
3. What are the environmental implications of personal digital accumulation?

**Method:** Quantitative survey study with 167 participants (75% female, ages 18-73, M=25.34 years) recruited via Prolific. Participants completed the DHQ (Digital Hoarding Questionnaire), the newly developed DBPUQ, and measures of attachment, difficulty deleting, and distress.

**Key Findings:**
- The DBPUQ demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = .86)
- DHQ total scores correlated significantly with difficulty deleting (r = .49), attachment (r = .44), and distress (r = .32)
- Factor analysis revealed a single-factor structure for personal digital hoarding behaviors
- Digital accumulation occurs across multiple file types: photos, music, apps, videos, e-books, emails, and text/numerical files

## Theoretical Framework

The paper draws on Hoarding Disorder literature (DSM-5 criteria) and extends it to the digital domain. Key theoretical concepts include:

1. **Digital Hoarding vs. Clinical Hoarding**: Unlike physical hoarding, digital hoarding lacks visible clutter but shares psychological characteristics (difficulty discarding, emotional attachment, distress)

2. **Personal vs. Professional Contexts**: Distinguishes between workplace digital hoarding (previously studied) and personal digital behaviors (understudied)

3. **Psychological Dimensions**: Three core dimensions measured:
   - Difficulty deleting digital items
   - Emotional attachment to digital possessions
   - Distress when contemplating deletion

4. **Sustainability Connection**: Links individual digital behaviors to collective environmental impact through data storage energy consumption

## Central Arguments

1. **Measurement Gap**: Existing digital hoarding measures (like the DHQ) focus on clinical/professional contexts and miss the everyday personal digital accumulation that most people experience

2. **Behavioral Complexity**: Personal digital hoarding is a multi-faceted behavior involving different file types, organization strategies, and psychological drivers

3. **Environmental Urgency**: The "out of sight, out of mind" nature of digital storage obscures its real environmental costs - data centers consume 1-2% of global electricity

4. **Emotional Drivers**: Digital hoarding is driven by emotional attachment and anticipated regret over potential loss, not just practical considerations

5. **Normalization Concern**: Easy cloud storage has normalized excessive digital accumulation, making intervention more difficult

## Evidence

**Quantitative Findings:**
- Mean DHQ score: 25.5 (SD=7.4) out of possible 49
- Mean DBPUQ score: 73.7 (SD=15.2)
- Correlation between DHQ and difficulty deleting: r = .49, p < .001
- Correlation between DHQ and attachment: r = .44, p < .001
- Correlation between DHQ and distress: r = .32, p < .001

**Environmental Statistics Cited:**
- 10.6 kg CO2 per UK adult annually from unwanted photos
- 355,000 tonnes CO2 from unwanted photos across UK
- 1-2% of global electricity consumed by data centers
- Average smartphone user takes 2,000 photos per year

**Descriptive Statistics on Digital Accumulation:**
- Photos: Mean organization = 3.72, difficulty finding = 3.13 (on 7-point scale)
- Music files: Mean organization = 4.10, difficulty finding = 2.80
- Apps: Mean organization = 4.04, difficulty finding = 2.53

## Conclusion

The study establishes that personal digital hoarding is a measurable construct distinct from clinical digital hoarding. The DBPUQ provides a valid tool for researchers studying everyday digital accumulation. The findings suggest that personal digital hoarding involves psychological mechanisms similar to physical hoarding - emotional attachment and difficulty discarding - but occurs in a context where storage costs seem negligible to individuals while creating aggregate environmental harm.

The authors call for increased awareness of the environmental footprint of personal digital data and suggest that behavioral interventions targeting digital decluttering could have sustainability benefits.

## Discussion Questions

1. How might the normalization of cloud storage affect users' awareness of their digital accumulation and its environmental impact?

2. The study found that difficulty deleting correlated more strongly with DHQ scores than emotional attachment. What design interventions might help users overcome deletion difficulty without triggering distress?

3. How do cultural differences in attitudes toward possessions (physical and digital) affect digital hoarding behaviors across populations?

4. Should digital platforms incorporate "environmental cost" indicators showing users the carbon footprint of their stored data?

5. How might the relationship between digital hoarding and psychological well-being (distress) be addressed through user experience design?
