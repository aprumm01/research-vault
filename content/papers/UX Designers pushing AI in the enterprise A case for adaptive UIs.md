# UX Designers pushing AI in the enterprise A case for adaptive UIs

## Summary
Carnegie Mellon and Accenture collaboration proposing adaptive user interfaces (AUIs) as UX-designer-led AI innovation for enterprise applications. AUIs automate frequent, repetitive tasks by learning from user actions (e.g., email recognizing phone numbers). Enterprise adoption lags consumer apps despite measurable ROI in worker time-savings. Paper presents 6-step design process: recognize opportunity, qualify benefit, gather resources, estimate cost, construct details, communicate to developers. Three common AUI patterns: ranked lists of likely targets, highlighted actionable entities, automated form-filling. Ethics section addresses worker agency, identity, self-efficacy, unintended consequences of time-savings, and privacy concerns about productivity monitoring.

## Theoretical Framework
Collective intelligence: combining machine logging/automation with human contextual understanding. Keeps user in the loop, reducing AI risk while enabling automation benefits.

## Methods
Position paper/design process proposal. Collaboration between academic researchers (CMU) and industry practitioners (Accenture).

## Main Arguments
- AUIs provide low-risk AI innovation opportunity for UX designers in enterprise contexts
- UX designers uniquely positioned to recognize frequent, repetitive tasks suitable for automation
- Enterprise applications offer clearer ROI than consumer apps (measurable worker time-savings)
- Current design processes/tools don't scaffold AUI choreographing; no systematic approach exists
- Three design patterns enable AUI implementation: ranked choice lists, entity highlighting, automated field population
- Ethical design critical: attend to worker agency/identity/self-efficacy, avoid unhealthy dependency, watch unintended consequences, monitor privacy

## Limitations & Critiques
- Position paper without empirical validation of proposed design process
- No case studies demonstrating ROI calculations or implementation success rates
- Ethics section acknowledges concerns but provides limited guidance for resolving tensions
- Assumes telemetry data availability and quality; many enterprises lack adequate logging
- Doesn't address resistance to automation or worker concerns about job displacement
- Focus on time-savings may reinforce productivity-over-humanity Taylorism despite ethical caveats
