# Designers' Articulation and Activation of Instrumental Design Judgements in Cross-Cultural User Research

## Summary

This paper analyzes how design teams articulate and activate instrumental design judgements when conducting cross-cultural user research. Using a shared dataset from the Design Thinking Research Symposium (DTRS11), the researchers examined over 13 hours of video and audio recordings documenting a Scandinavian design team's co-creation workshops with Chinese consumers for a new product targeting the Chinese market. The study reveals how design judgements shift over time and how cultural considerations are both explicitly and implicitly embedded in the design process.

The research identifies several key patterns in how the design team referenced workshop participants, noting a substantial increase in third-person plural pronouns as the design phase progressed and a shift toward more individualized references during debrief sessions. The team's instrumental judgements moved from totalising cultural references during design to frequent translator-mediated interactions during debrief. Two primary mechanisms emerged for engaging with cultural concerns: "nuancing" (where translators added layers or gradation to cultural understandings) and "making familiar" (where team members attempted to relate cultural concerns to their own context).

The findings highlight tensions between user-centred design's egalitarian ideals and the pragmatic realities of cross-cultural engagement. Despite intentions for deep cultural engagement, the team often relied on unvalidated assumptions, stereotypes, and surface-level cultural tropes, pointing to fundamental limitations in how standard design methods address cultural complexity.

## Key Concepts

- **Instrumental Judgement**: A designer's interaction with tools involving "a process of mediation that considers not only technique and which instruments to use, but proportion and gauge as well" - encompassing design methods as tools
- **Cross-Cultural Design**: Design behavior where cultural concerns are heightened when cultural boundaries are traversed, relating to geography, ethnicity, nationality, or socio-economic status
- **Nuancing**: Translator-mediated activity where cultural points are elaborated upon with additional layers or gradation that do not contradict but enrich understanding
- **Making Familiar**: Team members' attempts to find equivalences or analogues between unfamiliar cultural concepts and their own cultural experiences
- **Design Discourse**: The tacit (normative) understandings of the world that designers bring to their work, which necessarily take on cultural character

## Theoretical Framework

The paper builds on Nelson and Stolterman's theoretical typology of design judgements, focusing particularly on instrumental judgement. It draws on scholarship in cross-cultural design (Tai, Dhadphale), participatory design critique (Irani et al., Hakken and Mate), and design cognition literature. The framework positions design methods as "instruments" that carry assumptions about cultural appropriateness that designers must navigate.

## Methods

Qualitative analysis of shared DTRS11 dataset comprising:
- 13+ hours of video and audio recordings spanning 3 months of design team activities
- Discourse analysis using word frequency of third-person plural pronouns to identify references to participants
- Open coding of 1844 pronoun instances resulting in 21 distinct referent codes
- Thematic analysis identifying cultural assumptions, understandings, and their manifestation in instrumental judgements
- Analysis of design sessions (v02-v05), debrief sessions (v07, v14), and interviews (v01, v22)

## Main Arguments

- Instrumental judgements made by design teams can be traced across the design process, revealing how cultural understanding shapes design decisions
- The role of translators shifted from utilitarian language translation to cultural translation, becoming "nuancers" who added depth to cultural understandings
- "Making familiar" can help designers move forward but may yield flawed insights if parallels are fallaciously drawn
- Standard user research methods may be "considered harmful" in cross-cultural contexts when they maintain shallow engagement with cultural dimensions
- Design teams often relied on implicit cultural views that treated behaviors across cultures as expressions of essentially identical human characteristics
- User-centred design's ethic of engaging users directly may conflict with the complexity of cross-cultural meaning-making
- Cultural sensitivity did not appear to be a recognized priority even when cultural meaning-making potential was present

## Limitations & Critiques

The study acknowledges limitations inherent to secondary data analysis: the researchers could not directly influence data collection or ask follow-up questions. The scope focused on one design team in one cross-cultural context (Scandinavian-Chinese), limiting generalizability. The team lacked Chinese members during design phases, which may explain the absence of deep cultural consideration. The paper calls for further research on how instrumental judgements may be more fully articulated in cross-cultural participatory and co-design traditions.
