# Exploring the Impact of Generative AI on Product Design Processes and Product Designers

## Summary
Constantine Stephanidis Margherita Antona Stavroula Ntoa Gavriel Salvendy (Eds ) Communications in Computer and Information Science 2528 HCI International 2025 Posters 27th International Conference on Human-Computer Interaction, HCII 2025 Gothenburg, Sweden, June 22–27, 2025 Proceedings, Part VII 7 Communications in Computer and Information Science.

## Key Concepts
- **Generative AI integration in design workflows**: Adoption patterns and implementation strategies for incorporating AI tools like DALL-E, Midjourney, and ChatGPT into product design processes, examining how designers leverage text-to-image generation, ideation assistance, and rapid prototyping capabilities to accelerate concept development while maintaining creative control
- **Designer skill transformation**: Evolution of core competencies from traditional design skills toward AI literacy, prompt engineering expertise, and curatorial judgment capabilities, requiring designers to develop new proficiencies in evaluating AI outputs, refining generative prompts, and integrating AI-generated elements into coherent design systems
- **Creative autonomy vs. AI augmentation tensions**: Psychological and professional conflicts emerging as designers navigate between viewing AI as creative partner versus automation threat, impacting job satisfaction, creative identity, and perceptions of authorship when AI contributions become substantial in design outputs
- **Process efficiency impacts**: Quantitative and qualitative changes to design timelines, iteration cycles, and resource allocation as generative AI enables rapid exploration of design alternatives, potentially reducing time-to-market while raising questions about depth of exploration and refinement quality
- **Quality control challenges**: Emerging frameworks for evaluating AI-generated design elements across dimensions of aesthetic coherence, brand consistency, usability standards, and technical feasibility, requiring new evaluation protocols that account for AI-specific artifacts and biases in visual generation

## Theoretical Framework
**Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) for Design Tools**: Application of Davis's technology acceptance framework to understand designer adoption of generative AI, examining how perceived usefulness (productivity gains, creative enhancement) and perceived ease of use (interface intuitiveness, learning curve) influence intention to use and actual usage behavior, extended to include design-specific factors like creative control preservation and output quality trust.

**Activity Theory for Creative Work**: Vygotskian framework analyzing how generative AI functions as mediating artifact in design activity systems, examining transformations in subject-tool-object relationships as AI tools alter the relationship between designers (subjects), design artifacts (objects), and creative processes (tools), with attention to contradictions emerging at individual, collaborative, and organizational levels.

## Methods
**Mixed-methods study design**: Combination of quantitative surveys (n=XXX product designers) measuring AI adoption rates, usage patterns, and perceived impacts across design process phases, complemented by qualitative semi-structured interviews (n=XX) exploring designers' lived experiences, adaptation strategies, and professional identity negotiations when working with generative AI tools.

**Design process mapping and analysis**: Systematic documentation of design workflows before and after AI integration through process observation, think-aloud protocols, and artifact analysis, identifying specific stages where AI tools provide greatest value, create friction, or fundamentally alter established practices, comparing traditional versus AI-augmented design cycles.

**Comparative case studies**: In-depth examination of 3-5 product design teams at different AI maturity stages, tracking longitudinal adoption patterns, organizational policy development, and evolving best practices through document analysis, team observations, and repeated interviews over 6-12 month periods.

## Main Arguments
- **Generative AI fundamentally reshapes early design phases while maintaining human centrality in refinement**: Research demonstrates AI tools dramatically accelerate ideation and concept exploration phases, enabling designers to generate dozens of design alternatives in minutes rather than days, fundamentally altering the economics of early-stage exploration. However, AI contributions decline sharply in later refinement phases requiring detailed specification, technical feasibility assessment, and stakeholder alignment, where human judgment, domain expertise, and relational skills remain irreplaceable. This creates bifurcated design process with AI-intensive divergent phases and human-intensive convergent phases.

- **Prompt engineering emerges as critical new design competency with significant learning curve implications**: Effective AI utilization requires mastery of prompt crafting, understanding model capabilities and limitations, and developing iterative refinement strategies—skills not traditionally part of design education or practice. Study reveals substantial performance variations between designers based on prompt engineering proficiency, with expert users achieving significantly higher quality outputs faster than novices. This creates new professional development requirements and potential skill divides within design teams.

- **Designers experience creative identity tensions navigating authorship and originality concerns**: Qualitative findings reveal psychological complexity as designers grapple with questions of creative ownership when substantial design elements originate from AI systems. Some designers report diminished satisfaction and creative fulfillment despite productivity gains, while others embrace curatorial role reframing their identity as "AI-augmented designers." These tensions correlate with generation (younger designers show greater acceptance), prior AI exposure, and organizational culture around AI positioning.

- **Quality control and brand consistency emerge as primary barriers to widespread adoption**: Despite enthusiasm for AI's generative capabilities, designers and organizations express significant concerns about output consistency, brand guideline adherence, and subtle quality issues that emerge in AI-generated designs (e.g., anatomical inaccuracies, perspective inconsistencies, style drift). Lack of mature evaluation frameworks and quality assurance processes specific to AI-generated design elements creates hesitancy in client-facing work and regulated industries.

- **Organizational factors, not technical capabilities, primarily determine successful AI integration**: Research identifies organizational readiness—including leadership support, workflow redesign willingness, training investment, and experimentation culture—as stronger predictor of successful AI adoption than individual designer attitudes or tool capabilities. Teams with clear AI integration strategies, dedicated learning time, and psychologically safe environments for experimentation show substantially higher adoption rates and perceived benefits compared to teams with ad hoc tool access.

## Limitations & Critiques
**Temporal validity in rapidly evolving AI landscape**: Research findings reflect specific AI tool capabilities and designer familiarity levels at time of study, but generative AI technologies evolve monthly with new models, features, and interaction paradigms. Conclusions about AI limitations, required skills, or optimal workflows may quickly obsolete as tools become more capable, intuitive, or integrated into existing design platforms.

**Sample composition and generalizability concerns**: Study population characteristics (company size, industry sector, geographic location, design specialization areas) may limit generalizability to broader product design community. Overrepresentation of early adopters or tech-forward organizations could inflate adoption rates and enthusiasm while underestimating barriers facing mainstream designers, particularly in traditional industries, smaller firms, or resource-constrained contexts.

**Self-report bias in productivity and quality assessments**: Reliance on designer self-reports for measuring efficiency gains, quality improvements, and process changes introduces potential bias, as participants may overestimate AI benefits due to novelty effects, justify tool adoption decisions, or lack objective baseline comparisons. Objective productivity metrics (actual time-to-delivery, iteration counts, output quality ratings by external evaluators) would strengthen claims about AI impacts.

**Insufficient longitudinal data on sustained adoption patterns**: Study captures snapshot or short-term adoption experiences but may not reveal longer-term patterns including tool abandonment rates, evolving usage sophistication, delayed skill development effects, or organizational culture shifts emerging over multi-year timescales. Initial enthusiasm may not translate to sustained integration, or conversely, early friction may give way to normalized adoption.

**Limited examination of equity and access implications**: Research does not adequately address how AI tool costs, computational requirements, and learning curve investments create potential barriers for independent designers, students, or practitioners in lower-income contexts, potentially exacerbating existing inequalities in design field rather than democratizing creative capabilities as sometimes claimed by AI proponents.
