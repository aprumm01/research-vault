# Why Are There Still So Many Jobs? (Oravec Review of Autor, 2018)

**Year:** 2018
**Venue:** Journal of Economic Perspectives—Volume 29, Number 3—Summer

## Summary
Autor's landmark 2015 Journal of Economic Perspectives article (reviewed by Oravec in 2018) argues that automation has not eliminated employment over two centuries because automation and labor are strong complements, not pure substitutes. A task-based framework distinguishes routine from non-routine tasks, explaining why automation substitutes for repetitive work while increasing demand for higher-order cognitive and interpersonal labor.

## Key Concepts
- Technological unemployment
- Task-based model of labor markets
- Routine vs. non-routine tasks
- Automation-labor complementarity
- Skill-biased technical change

## Theoretical Framework
Task-based model: jobs are bundles of tasks; automation substitutes for routine tasks (both cognitive and manual) while complementing non-routine cognitive and manual tasks; historical institutional analysis grounds the framework.

## Methods
Review of two centuries of historical and contemporary economic literature; analysis of U.S. employment statistics; occupational task-level analysis; Autor draws no new primary data but synthesizes across existing empirical studies.

## Main Arguments
- Automation historically creates as much labor demand as it displaces, through complementarity and income effects that raise overall demand
- Routine tasks are most vulnerable to automation; non-routine interpersonal and judgment-intensive tasks are most protected
- Journalists and commentators systematically overstate substitution and underestimate complementarity
- Current AI may be qualitatively different, but past patterns suggest continued labor demand even amid displacement

## Limitations & Critiques
Historical patterns may not apply to the scale and generality of AI-era automation; the framework underplays distributional and political-economic effects; focuses on aggregate employment rather than quality, security, or fairness of the jobs that remain.
