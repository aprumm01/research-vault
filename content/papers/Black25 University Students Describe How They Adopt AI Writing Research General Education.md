# University Students Describe How They Adopt AI for Writing and Research in a General Education Course

## Summary
This exploratory qualitative study examines how undergraduate students documented their use of AI tools in a large General Education course on sustainability and technology at UC Irvine in Spring 2023. Of 277 enrolled students, 39 explicitly documented AI use in their final course project. Through iterative qualitative coding, the authors identify patterns in students' AI use across higher-order writing tasks, lower-order writing tasks, and other learning activities. Students primarily used AI to improve communication of their original ideas while maintaining intellectual independence, and many expressed skepticism about AI-generated content. Published in Scientific Reports (2025).

## Key Concepts
- **Higher-order AI writing tasks**: Using AI for understanding complex topics, finding evidence, developing arguments—activities requiring conceptual engagement
- **Lower-order AI writing tasks**: Using AI for revising, editing, proofreading—mechanical improvement tasks that preserve original intellectual content
- **Voluntary AI documentation**: The methodological approach of requiring students who chose to use AI to document their usage, capturing authentic rather than experimental AI adoption patterns
- **Intellectual independence**: Students' expressed concern with maintaining their own thinking and distinguishing AI-assisted mechanics from original intellectual contribution
- **AI skepticism**: Widespread student caution about AI accuracy and reliability, leading to verification behaviors and selective reliance
- **Zone of proximal development (ZPD)**: Vygotsky's concept applied to understand AI as a "more expert other" that helps students extend their abilities and reach higher proficiency
- **Early LLM adoption snapshot**: The study captures AI use just months after ChatGPT's release, documenting an early moment in the evolution of student AI practices

## Theoretical Framework
The study applies sociocultural learning theory, specifically Vygotsky's concept of the zone of proximal development (ZPD) and tool mediation. In this framework, AI functions as a "more expert other" that supports students in extending their existing abilities and reaching higher levels of proficiency beyond what they could achieve independently. This theoretical lens frames AI not as a replacement for student thinking but as a mediating agent in the learning process—similar to how tutors, peers, and instructional materials have historically scaffolded learning.

The paper also draws on computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) theories, positioning AI as a potential learning partner rather than merely a text generation tool. The distinction between using AI for mechanical tasks versus deeper conceptual work maps onto Vygotskian distinctions between tool use for externally-imposed versus internally-motivated learning.

## Methods
Qualitative content analysis of AI use documentation from 39 students in a 277-student undergraduate General Education course at UC Irvine, Spring 2023. The assignment required students to analyze conceptual networks connecting core sustainability concepts; those who used AI were required to document the system used, how they used it, what prompts they used, and how they believed AI affected their work. Iterative qualitative coding was applied to identify patterns in AI use. The study was reviewed and approved by UCI's IRB (#6704). Note: the paper itself was written in collaboration with ChatGPT (4o) and Claude AI (3.5 Sonnet), with AI use documented in accordance with the paper's own methods.

## Main Arguments
- Students primarily use AI to improve communication of ideas they already have rather than to generate ideas, suggesting that early AI adoption in academic contexts is more supplementary than substitutive with respect to original intellectual contribution
- A meaningful distinction exists in student practice between using AI for mechanical tasks (editing, proofreading) and deeper conceptual work (finding evidence, developing arguments)—students themselves articulate and navigate this boundary
- Many students expressed skepticism about AI-generated content, actively verifying outputs and emphasizing their own intellectual independence, suggesting that early adopters were not uncritical consumers of AI
- While some students leverage AI for more complex tasks like finding evidence and developing arguments, these uses are more contested and ambiguous in terms of their relationship to genuine learning and intellectual work
- The requirement to document AI use—rather than banning or ignoring it—represents a pedagogically valuable intervention that promotes metacognitive reflection about AI use without creating adversarial relationships between students and instructors
- This early 2023 snapshot reveals patterns of AI adoption that balance efficiency gains with concerns about authenticity and intellectual ownership that may persist or intensify as AI capabilities improve

## Limitations & Critiques
The sample of 39 students is small and self-selected: only students who chose to use and document AI are included, excluding the majority (238 of 277) who either did not use AI or did not document use. This selection bias means findings may not represent typical student AI use. The study was conducted in a single discipline-crossing GE course on sustainability and technology, limiting generalizability to other course types.

The documentation method itself may have influenced how students used and reported AI: students knew their AI use would be read and assessed, potentially leading to more reflective or socially desirable reporting. The early 2023 timing means the study captures initial adoption patterns that have likely evolved substantially as students have become more experienced with AI tools and institutional policies have developed.
