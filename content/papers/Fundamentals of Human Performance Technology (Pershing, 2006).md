# Fundamentals of Human Performance Technology (Pershing, 2006)

## Summary
Pershing's foundational chapter introduces Human Performance Technology (HPT), tracing its intellectual roots from Skinner's behavioral psychology through Gilbert and Harless's performance engineering work, and defining its core principles. HPT is framed as a systematic, results-based approach to improving individual and organizational performance that goes beyond training to encompass environmental supports, feedback systems, incentive structures, and structural interventions. The chapter establishes HPT's defining commitments to systemic thinking, empirical grounding, and performance gap analysis.

## Key Concepts
- Human Performance Technology definition and field overview
- Performance gap analysis and needs assessment
- Systemic vs. systematic approaches to performance improvement
- Non-training interventions as primary HPT tools
- Results-based practice and evidence-based decision making

## Theoretical Framework
Rooted in behavioral psychology (Skinner), performance engineering (Gilbert's Human Competence framework, Harless's front-end analysis), and systems thinking; draws on fifty years of HPT scholarship consolidated in the ISPI Handbook. Pershing positions HPT as a professional discipline with its own ethical code and theoretical foundations.

## Methods
Conceptual and definitional chapter providing theoretical grounding for the ISPI Handbook of Human Performance Technology; draws on historical synthesis and review of foundational HPT literature.

## Main Arguments
- HPT emerged from the recognition that well-designed training often fails to improve performance because training is only one factor among many environmental and organizational variables
- Effective HPT practice requires a systematic process: performance analysis, cause analysis, intervention selection, implementation, and evaluation
- HPT practitioners must diagnose root causes before prescribing interventions; training without analysis wastes resources and fails performers
- The field is systemic in orientation, meaning interventions must account for the organizational system as a whole rather than isolated components

## Limitations & Critiques
The chapter is an explicitly foundational overview and does not critically engage with challenges to the HPT model, including critiques of its behaviorist roots or its difficulty accounting for informal and social learning. The U.S.-centric historical framing may not fully represent HPT's international development or application in non-corporate contexts.
