# Generative AI in UX Education: Assessing Critical Thinking, Problem Solving, and Design Iteration Speed

## Summary
This mixed-methods study examines how generative AI tools (ChatGPT, UX Pilot, Figma AI) affect UX design students' critical thinking, problem-solving ability, and design iteration speed through a controlled experiment at Mae Fah Luang University in Thailand. Students were divided into AI-assisted and non-AI-assisted groups and tasked with completing three Figma UI design tasks of increasing complexity. Results show that AI primarily enhanced design iteration speed but did not measurably improve the usability quality of outputs or fundamentally develop students' independent critical thinking and problem-solving skills.

## Key Concepts
- **Design iteration speed**: AI tools significantly accelerate the rate at which students can produce and revise design artifacts, reducing time-to-output in UX workflows
- **Critical thinking in design**: The capacity to independently analyze problems, evaluate options, and generate reasoned design decisions—which AI use does not automatically improve
- **Usability heuristics evaluation**: Designs were scored against established UX principles and usability heuristics, providing an objective quality benchmark
- **Human-AI collaboration in education**: The study positions AI as a collaborative tool in design processes rather than a replacement, while questioning the conditions under which this collaboration is educationally beneficial
- **Cognitive independence**: The risk that AI-assisted efficiency gains come at the cost of students developing their own problem-solving and creative reasoning abilities
- **Mixed-methods experimental design**: Combining quantitative performance measurement with qualitative experience surveys to assess educational outcomes
- **AI over-reliance**: 83% of respondents in related research expressed concern that AI overuse would weaken independent thinking

## Theoretical Framework
The study is grounded in cognitive and pedagogical theories about skill development in design education. The central theoretical tension is between efficiency (AI as productivity enhancer) and efficacy (AI as developmental tool). Drawing on prior research about AI in UX education, the paper positions generative AI as potentially beneficial for creative ideation and workflow acceleration while raising concerns about cognitive atrophy—the weakening of foundational design thinking when students outsource cognitive work to AI. The study also engages with human-computer interaction theories about the balance between automation assistance and human creative agency.

The framework distinguishes between AI as a tool for output generation versus AI as a scaffold for learning, arguing that effective UX education must teach students to critically evaluate AI outputs rather than accept them uncritically. Ethical considerations about bias in AI-generated content and the importance of empathy-building in UX design are also foregrounded as areas where AI assistance may be insufficient.

## Methods
A three-phase mixed-methods experimental design was employed: (1) pre-experiment survey capturing demographics, prior AI experience, creativity self-assessment, and ethical attitudes; (2) controlled experiment with students randomly divided into AI-assisted and non-AI-assisted groups completing three Figma UI design tasks of increasing complexity; (3) post-experiment survey with Likert scale and open-ended questions about student experience. Design outputs were evaluated using a scoring system based on UX principles and usability heuristics. The study was conducted during the academic year at Mae Fah Luang University's School of Applied Digital Technology in Chiang Rai, Thailand.

## Main Arguments
- AI tools primarily accelerate design iteration speed rather than improving the quality or usability of final design outputs, suggesting a speed-quality trade-off where AI enables faster but not necessarily better design
- Critical thinking and problem-solving skills are not automatically developed through AI-assisted design work; students using AI did not demonstrate superior reasoning or decision-making compared to non-AI groups
- The efficiency benefits of AI in UX education are real and significant, making AI integration appropriate for workflow tasks while careful attention must be paid to ensuring foundational thinking skills are taught alongside tool use
- AI-generated content can lack human-centered perspective and empathy-building qualities essential to UX design, potentially reinforcing stereotypes rather than developing genuine user understanding
- Ethical education must accompany AI integration: students need explicit instruction in critically evaluating AI outputs, understanding AI biases, and maintaining creative agency over the design process
- The UX education context presents particular challenges for AI integration because the field inherently requires human empathy, contextual judgment, and user advocacy that automated systems cannot reliably provide

## Limitations & Critiques
The study was conducted at a single institution in Thailand with a relatively small sample size, limiting cross-cultural generalizability. The controlled experiment setting may not capture authentic educational conditions where AI use is more organic and self-directed. The usability heuristics scoring system, while objective, may not capture all dimensions of design quality relevant to UX education. The study does not track longitudinal outcomes to determine whether AI-assisted students develop or atrophy their independent skills over time.

The binary design of AI-assisted vs. non-AI-assisted groups may also oversimplify real-world hybrid usage patterns, and the specific AI tools used (ChatGPT, UX Pilot) represent only a subset of the expanding AI design tool ecosystem.
