# The Technocratic Momentum after 1945: The Development of Teaching Machines

## Summary
Examines how Cold War technocratic ideology drove the development of programmed instruction and teaching machines in the 1950s–1970s, finding that Eastern and Western states converged on a shared behaviorist-then-cognitivist teaching ideology despite their political differences. Argues that the failure of teaching machines as devices did not kill the underlying ideology—it merely shifted to new devices serving the same assumptions, a pattern that continues with digital and AI education tools.

## Key Concepts
- Programmed instruction
- Teaching machines (Skinner)
- Cold War educationalization
- Behaviorism and cognitivism in education policy
- Technocratic ideology

## Theoretical Framework
Historical and ideological analysis; concept of "educationalization" (education mobilized to solve political and technical problems); comparative Cold War policy analysis of US and USSR education systems.

## Methods
Historical analysis of OECD, UNESCO, and national education policy documents; comparative analysis of US and Soviet educational reforms; intellectual history of behaviorism and cognitivism in education.

## Main Arguments
- Sputnik shock made education a national security issue in the US and intensified the educationalization of the Cold War globally
- Teaching machine ideology was paradoxically shared across the Cold War divide, driven by technocratic efficiency logics
- The failure of teaching machines as devices did not alter the underlying ideological belief in technological solutions to learning
- This pattern—ideology persisting through device failure—explains contemporary enthusiasm for AI in education

## Limitations & Critiques
Primarily focused on Western (US, OECD) and Soviet contexts; does not examine non-OECD countries or Global South education systems; analysis ends before the internet era; may oversimplify the diversity of positions within behaviorism and cognitivism.
