# Design Thinking and Agile Design

## Summary
Svihla reviews the origins and meaning of design thinking within design research and traces how the concept has been adopted, adapted, and sometimes oversimplified in instructional design and educational technology. The chapter situates design thinking alongside agile methods and human-centered design as iterative, expertise-driven alternatives to the linear ADDIE model, arguing that design thinking is not new so much as it is a formalization of what expert designers have always done. Lessons for instructional designers center on using abductive reasoning, leveraging precedent, and shifting key insights earlier in the design process.

## Key Concepts
- Design thinking
- Abductive reasoning in design
- ADDIE and agile instructional design
- Precedent in expert design practice
- Human-centered design

## Theoretical Framework
The chapter draws on design research traditions, particularly the distinction between scientific reasoning (inductive, deductive) and design reasoning (abductive), and situates design thinking within a broader field that includes agile methods and iterative practice. It references Nigel Cross and the design cognition literature to argue that design thinking is a disciplinary epistemology, not simply a method.

## Methods
Literature review and conceptual synthesis spanning design research, instructional design journals, and practitioner accounts. Includes illustrative case examples to ground abstract concepts in practical scenarios for instructional designers.

## Main Arguments
- Design thinking is not a new invention of IDEO but an established area of design research that predates its popularization
- Expert designers reason abductively, filling gaps with precedent rather than following linear steps
- Agile and design thinking approaches reflect what accomplished designers already do; they are under-taught in instructional design programs
- Instructional designers can integrate design thinking and agile methods alongside ADDIE rather than treating them as replacements

## Limitations & Critiques
The chapter's scope is broad and primarily conceptual, and it does not provide empirical evidence that design thinking methods produce better instructional outcomes than ADDIE in practice. The framing assumes instructional designers have the autonomy and institutional support to shift their process, which is not always the case.
