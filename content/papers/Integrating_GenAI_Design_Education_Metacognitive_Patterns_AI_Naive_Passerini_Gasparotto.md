# Integrating Generative AI in Design Education: Metacognitive Patterns in AI-Naive Students

## Summary
This mid-intervention work-in-progress documents a pedagogical intervention in a Service Design course at the University of Bologna, where final-year design students with minimal prior AI exposure were introduced to LLMs within authentic public administration service design projects. The study's central contribution is the concept of "intentional friction"—the finding that technical failures and imperfect AI outputs paradoxically generate deeper understanding of LLM behavior than seamless interactions. The paper addresses the underexplored challenge of AI literacy development in "AI-naive" populations and identifies metacognitive patterns that differ fundamentally from those seen in AI-experienced users.

## Key Concepts
- **Intentional Friction**: The primary finding—when technical failures or imperfect AI outputs occurred, students engaged in deeper reflection on LLM behavior, probabilistic generation, and their own thinking processes than when AI worked smoothly; failure became a pedagogical asset
- **AI-Naive Population**: Students with little prior exposure to generative AI tools—a population typically excluded from controlled studies but increasingly common as GenAI enters non-STEM disciplines
- **Cognitive Offloading**: The tendency to reduce internal cognitive load by delegating mental processes to external tools—in educational contexts, can shift from "strategic" (conscious, controlled) to "default" (uncritical, habitual) offloading
- **Automation Bias**: The tendency to favor suggestions from automated systems even when incorrect—particularly dangerous for design students who may overestimate AI "authority" in creative and analytical domains
- **Assessment Blind Spots**: Students' limited capacity to critically evaluate AI output quality—they recognized AI could produce plausible-sounding text but struggled to assess accuracy, relevance, or appropriateness of specific outputs
- **Ethical Pragmatism**: A pattern emerging through situated practice where students developed practical ethical stances (what AI use is acceptable, when attribution is needed) through doing rather than instruction
- **Productive Struggle**: The pedagogical value of maintaining "cognitive friction" necessary for metacognitive skill development—LLMs that too smoothly generate solutions risk short-circuiting this productive struggle

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on cognitive offloading theory (the human tendency to delegate mental processes to external supports) and automation bias research (Mosier and Skitka), examining how these phenomena manifest specifically in non-STEM students working on creative design projects. Design thinking's generative and abductive nature—where the quality of the exploratory process is intrinsically valuable—creates particular vulnerability to premature convergence when AI quickly generates "good enough" outputs. The authors frame metacognitive development as the key educational outcome, arguing that students must develop not just AI literacy (how to use tools) but metacognitive AI literacy (awareness of how AI affects their own thinking processes). The study is funded through the ER2Digit European Digital Innovation Hub initiative (NextGenerationEU), situating it within European digital transformation policy.

## Methods
Action research mid-intervention report at Week 6 of a 12-week Service Design course at the Università di Bologna's Department of Architecture. Twenty final-year students in the Advanced Design Master's program worked on real public administration service design projects using LLMs introduced by the intervention. Data collected included: planning discussions, structured prompt logging (capturing students' prompting strategies and iterations), and researcher observations. The mid-point report provides "real-time" documentation of emerging pedagogical patterns before the intervention concluded—an unusual methodological choice justified by the rapidly evolving field.

## Main Arguments
- Intentional friction—technical failures and imperfect AI outputs—generated deeper metacognitive engagement with LLM behavior than seamless interactions, reversing the typical assumption that smooth tool performance is educationally optimal
- AI-naive students demonstrated "default" cognitive offloading rather than "strategic" offloading—they delegated creative planning decisions to AI without maintaining conscious control, a pattern requiring active pedagogical intervention
- Assessment blind spots represent a distinct learning challenge: students could recognize that AI produced plausible text but lacked the domain knowledge and epistemological sophistication to evaluate whether outputs were appropriate for their specific service design contexts
- Ethical pragmatism emerged through situated design practice rather than explicit ethics instruction—students developed practical stances on authorship and attribution through the friction of actual projects, suggesting ethics education should be embedded in authentic contexts
- The intervention required radical reformulation when the original plan (advanced professional AI integration) encountered students' poor familiarity with conversational interfaces, privacy implications, and probabilistic language generation—illustrating how AI literacy baseline assumptions frequently misalign with non-STEM student realities
- The paper argues for a pedagogical approach that treats AI failures as learning opportunities, incorporates structured reflection on AI limitations, and delays smooth AI fluency to preserve the metacognitive benefits of productive struggle

## Limitations & Critiques
As a mid-intervention work-in-progress, findings are preliminary and limited to Week 6 observations—the two additional dynamics (assessment blind spots and ethical pragmatism) are "noted as preliminary observations warranting post-intervention investigation" rather than confirmed findings. The small class size (N=20) and single-institution context limit generalizability. The action research methodology lacks the comparative structure needed to isolate the effects of specific pedagogical choices.

The concept of "intentional friction" is intriguing but requires more precise operationalization—what types of friction are pedagogically productive, under what conditions, and for which learning outcomes? The paper cannot yet answer these questions.
