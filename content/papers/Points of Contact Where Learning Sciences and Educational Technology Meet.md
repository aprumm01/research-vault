# Points of Contact: Where Learning Sciences and Educational Technology Meet

## Summary
Gibbons traces the historical divergence between educational technology (ET) and the learning sciences (LS) by examining the contrasting intellectual trajectories of Robert Gagné and Robert Glaser in the 1960s and 1970s. He argues that the two fields grew from shared goals but developed distinct paradigmatic commitments: ET focused on instructional categories, formulas, and systematic delivery; LS focused on learner cognition, task analysis, and responsive environment design. The chapter calls for renewed dialogue and bridge-building between the communities, identifying design as a potential point of contact.

## Key Concepts
- Historical divergence of educational technology and learning sciences
- Robert Gagné and Robert Glaser as paradigmatic figures
- Instructional events and taxonomy (ET tradition)
- Learner cognition and responsive design (LS tradition)
- Design as a bridge between ET and LS communities

## Theoretical Framework
History of educational research and instructional design; draws on the intellectual genealogy of Gagné and Glaser to trace field-level divergence; situates the ET/LS split within broader debates about instructional theory vs. learning theory; contextualized in Carr-Chellman's edited volume on classic and contemporary dialogues in instructional design.

## Methods
Historical-conceptual analysis; traces the divergence through the 1969 Annual Review of Psychology parallel chapters by Gagné/Rohwer and Glaser/Resnick; includes multiple invited response commentaries from Yip, Rowland, Zimmerman, and Reeves.

## Main Arguments
- ET and LS share foundational goals (improving learning and instruction) but have developed incompatible paradigmatic assumptions about how those goals should be pursued
- The Gagné tradition treats instructional categories and events as the primary design tool; the Glaser tradition treats learner cognition and task analysis as primary
- Neither tradition alone can support the scale and complexity demands of contemporary educational technology
- Design, particularly participatory and research-based design, offers the most promising path toward productive integration of the two communities

## Limitations & Critiques
The chapter primarily focuses on a U.S. academic lineage and may not fully represent the diversity of ET and LS internationally. The invited commentary format means the chapter is somewhat dialogic rather than presenting a single sustained argument. The call for bridge-building is aspirational; concrete mechanisms for integration are not fully specified.
