# User Experience Design Professionals' Perceptions of Generative Artificial Intelligence

## Summary
This CHI 2024 paper investigates how UX designers perceive, use, and anticipate the impact of generative AI through one-on-one in-depth interviews with 20 UX designers across companies ranging from startups to large enterprises in Europe and the United States. The study finds that experienced designers are confident in their creative, empathic, and originality skills and view GenAI as an assistive tool rather than a replacement. However, they express concern about skill degradation and job displacement for junior designers. The paper frames its analysis through responsible AI and participatory AI lenses.

## Key Concepts
- **AI as assistive tool**: The dominant framing among experienced UX designers — GenAI helps with repetitive and general tasks but requires skilled human judgment to produce meaningful, user-centered outcomes
- **Human factors of enjoyment and agency**: Two values that experienced designers identify as irreducibly human in the design process — the intrinsic motivation of creative work and the authorial agency over design decisions
- **AI alignment**: The concept that humans remain the final arbiters of whether AI outputs align with user needs, business values, and ethical constraints — a gatekeeper function that experienced designers claim for themselves
- **Junior designer vulnerability**: The asymmetric impact concern — experienced designers feel confident, but anticipate that GenAI could degrade skill development opportunities and threaten employment for early-career designers
- **Responsible AI in UX practice**: The application of responsible AI principles (transparency, fairness, accountability) to UX designers' own adoption of GenAI tools in their work
- **Participatory AI**: The argument that creative professionals, including UX designers, should be included as stakeholders in AI development decisions, not just end users of AI products

## Theoretical Framework
The paper is framed within responsible AI and participatory AI paradigms, drawing on scholarship that argues AI development must include diverse stakeholders — particularly creative professionals whose expertise is being automated. This positions UX designers as holders of valuable domain knowledge that should inform GenAI tool design, not merely as subjects of a technology adoption study.

The study also draws on human-centered AI (HCAI) frameworks, which position human oversight, control, and agency as design requirements rather than optional features of AI systems. The paper's analysis of "enjoyment" and "agency" as non-negotiable human factors connects to broader HCI debates about what aspects of creative work cannot and should not be delegated to AI.

## Methods
One-on-one in-depth semi-structured interviews with 20 UX designers recruited from companies in Europe and the United States. Participants varied in years of experience (junior to senior) and company size (startups to enterprises with over 10,000 employees). Interviews explored current UX design practices and tools, perceptions of GenAI capabilities and limitations, and expectations about future human-AI collaboration. Analysis used qualitative coding to identify themes across individual, organizational, and professional dimensions.

## Main Arguments
- **Experienced UX designers are confident in their human-specific skills**: Participants emphasized that originality, creativity in understanding users, and empathy toward end users are skills they believe GenAI cannot replicate — grounding their professional identity in capabilities they see as distinctly human.
- **GenAI is perceived as an assistive tool for productivity, not a creative replacement**: Designers see GenAI as useful for automating repetitive tasks (wireframe variations, design system documentation, competitor analysis) but maintain that the judgment, synthesis, and user-centering remain human responsibilities.
- **Enjoyment and agency are irreducible human values in design work**: A surprising finding was that participants emphasized the intrinsic value of creative work itself — the enjoyment of making design decisions — as a reason to resist full delegation to AI, independent of questions about AI quality.
- **Junior designers face asymmetric risk**: While senior designers feel secure, they consistently express concern that GenAI will erode entry-level learning opportunities, skip the formative experiences that build design competency, and depress junior hiring — creating a structural talent pipeline problem for the profession.
- **Copyright, ownership, and data privacy are significant unresolved concerns**: Participants raised concerns about using AI tools trained on others' design work, the copyright status of AI-generated artifacts in client deliverables, and the risk of confidential user research data entering AI training pipelines.
- **The UX community needs AI literacy and structured evaluation competencies**: Designers expressed a need for better understanding of how GenAI works, how to evaluate output quality critically, and how to use AI responsibly in professional contexts — skills they feel current training and tool documentation do not adequately support.

## Limitations & Critiques
The 20-participant sample is concentrated in Europe and the United States, which may not capture the perspectives of UX designers in other global regions where design practice and AI access differ. The study captures perceptions and anticipated impacts rather than observed behavioral changes, and the rapid evolution of GenAI tools means perceptions expressed in 2023 may already have shifted as tools improved. The focus on individual perceptions does not capture organizational or systemic factors that shape GenAI adoption in UX teams.

The finding that experienced designers are confident and see GenAI as assistive may reflect selection bias — practitioners who are sufficiently senior and secure in their roles to participate in research may represent a more optimistic slice of the profession than those facing more direct job pressure. The paper's responsible AI framing, while valuable, is applied somewhat selectively — it focuses on designers as potentially affected stakeholders but does not engage with the design community's own potential complicity in building AI systems that affect other workers.
