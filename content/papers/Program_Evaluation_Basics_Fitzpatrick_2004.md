# Program Evaluation: Alternative Approaches and Practical Guidelines (Chapter 1)

## Summary

This foundational chapter introduces program evaluation as a field, distinguishing it from research and articulating its purpose, types, and roles. The authors define evaluation as determining the worth or merit of an object using defensible criteria, and explore the distinctions between formative and summative evaluation, internal and external evaluators, and various uses of evaluation across sectors.

## Key Concepts

- **Program Evaluation**: Identification, clarification, and application of defensible criteria to determine value (worth or merit) of an evaluation object
- **Formative Evaluation**: Evaluation focused on improving a program during development
- **Summative Evaluation**: Evaluation focused on judging overall worth for decisions about continuation or adoption
- **Needs Assessment**: Determining whether a problem exists and what interventions might address it
- **Process Evaluation**: Examining how a program is delivered
- **Outcome Evaluation**: Examining results and impacts of a program

## Theoretical Framework

Builds on Scriven's (1967) foundational distinction between evaluation as judging merit/worth and research as seeking generalizable knowledge. Positions evaluation as applied inquiry serving stakeholder decision-making rather than primarily contributing to disciplinary theory.

## Methods

This is a conceptual/pedagogical chapter rather than empirical study. It:
- Defines key evaluation terminology
- Distinguishes evaluation from research
- Presents typologies of evaluation types and roles
- Provides illustrative examples across sectors (education, nonprofit, business)

## Main Arguments

1. **Evaluation vs. Research Distinction**:
   - Research seeks generalizable knowledge; evaluation serves specific stakeholder decisions
   - Research is theory-driven; evaluation is value-driven
   - Research generalizes across settings; evaluation is context-specific
   - Different criteria for quality (internal validity vs. utility, accuracy, feasibility, propriety)

2. **Formative-Summative Distinction**:
   - Formative: "When the cook tastes the soup" - improvement-focused, ongoing
   - Summative: "When the guest tastes the soup" - judgment-focused, final
   - Both needed throughout program lifecycle

3. **Internal vs. External Evaluators**:
   - Internal: Greater program knowledge, easier access, but potential bias
   - External: Greater objectivity, credibility, but less context knowledge
   - Choice depends on evaluation purpose and stakeholder needs

4. **Multiple Evaluation Uses**:
   - Merit and worth determination
   - Program improvement
   - Oversight and compliance
   - Knowledge development

5. **Stakeholder Centrality**: Unlike research, evaluation must serve identified stakeholders with different information needs and perspectives

## Limitations

- Introductory overview may oversimplify complex debates in evaluation theory
- Western-centric perspective on evaluation practice
- Distinctions presented (formative/summative, internal/external) are more blurred in practice
- Limited attention to participatory and empowerment evaluation approaches
- Published 2004, doesn't address recent developments in mixed methods, big data, etc.
- Focus on formal programs may not address evaluation of policies, systems, or informal interventions
