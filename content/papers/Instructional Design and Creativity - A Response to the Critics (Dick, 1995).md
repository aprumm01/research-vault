# Instructional Design and Creativity: A Response to the Critics

## Study Partner Enrichment

### Overview of the Document

This paper is authored by Walter Dick, a Professor of Instructional Systems in the Department of Educational Research at Florida State University. Dick was a contributing editor for *Educational Technology* magazine when this piece was published in July-August 1995. His professional identity is deeply entwined with systematic instructional design, particularly the Dick and Carey model, which he co-developed and which appears in multiple editions of the textbook *The Systematic Design of Instruction*.

This is a theoretical position paper published in *Educational Technology*, a practitioner-oriented journal rather than a peer-reviewed research venue. The piece is explicitly defensive in tone—it responds to what Dick calls "the critics," referring to Situated Cognition, Constructivist, and Anchored Instruction advocates who argue that systematic instructional design (ISD) produces boring, decontextualized instruction. Dick frames his essay as a rebuttal, marshaling conceptual arguments, anecdotal evidence from his master's students, and appeals to quality management principles to defend ISD against charges that its linear, procedural approach stifles creativity.

The document matters because it captures a pivotal moment in instructional design history when the field's dominant paradigm—systematic, objectives-based, behaviorist design—faced serious intellectual challenge from constructivist theories emphasizing context, learner agency, and authentic problem-solving. Though published in 1995, the paper resonates with contemporary debates about whether AI-driven design tools constrain human creativity or whether creativity resides in how designers use systematic processes, not in rejecting them.

### Research Overview

This paper does not pose a research question in the empirical sense—it is not a research study but rather a conceptual defense of instructional systems design (ISD) against critics who claim it produces boring instruction. The central intellectual problem Dick addresses is: Does the systematic, procedural nature of ISD inherently constrain designers' creativity, leading to instruction that is effective and efficient but unengaging?

Walter Dick himself is the sole author, though he explicitly draws on responses from four of his master's students at Florida State University—Stacey Kaufman, Karen Rice, Iris Seet, and Monte Watkins—who addressed questions about creative instruction as part of their comprehensive examinations. Dick solicited their perspectives to supplement his own, though he notes "none of the students thought there was a problem with the process; the problem, if there is one, lies elsewhere" (p. 70). This framing signals Dick's sympathetic audience: students trained in his own ISD tradition.

The "methods," if they can be called that, involve conceptual analysis rather than empirical data collection. Dick explicates the ISD model, particularly learner analysis, instructional strategy development (including Keller's ARCS motivational design model), and formative evaluation as loci where creativity can be systematically incorporated. He invokes Schutz's anecdote about corporate executives equating "creative" with "beard, sandals, dirty, unreliable" to illustrate how organizational culture shapes what creativity means in practice.

Key concepts include **boring instruction**, which Dick leaves undefined but treats as the perceived outcome critics attribute to ISD; **creativity**, which he also resists defining precisely, noting that "I don't really think that that question can be answered to each reader's satisfaction" (p. 161); **effectiveness and efficiency** as traditional ISD evaluation criteria focused on mastery of objectives and elimination of extraneous content; and **Keller's ARCS model** (Attention, Relevance, Confidence, Satisfaction), which Dick positions as a systematic strategy for ensuring engaging instruction.

Dick writes with rhetorical flair, opening with an anecdote about editor Larry Lipsitz calling to say "I got another one!"—another article claiming ISD produces boring instruction. Dick's tone oscillates between defensiveness, exasperation, and conciliation. He acknowledges that "boring instruction can result from nearly any design approach" (p. 190), including teacher-led and TV instruction, deflecting the charge that ISD is uniquely problematic. Yet he also accepts that if "learners claim [ISD] results in boring instruction, then we must accept that fact" (p. 202), provided designers actually used the process appropriately.

### Theories of Knowledge

Dick's theoretical framework is rooted in **behaviorist learning theory** and **systems theory**, though he makes conciliatory gestures toward **constructivism** and **motivational psychology**. His intellectual genealogy traces back to B.F. Skinner's reinforcement theory as manifested in programmed instruction (PI), which Dick acknowledges "died from the boredom expressed by students" (p. 127). He notes that while PI's format failed, its underlying approach survived, augmented by Robert Mager's work on objectives, Robert Glaser's criterion-referenced testing, and especially Robert M. Gagné's events of instruction and conditions of learning.

**Gagné's learning theory** is central to Dick's framework. Gagné identified nine events of instruction (gaining attention, informing learners of objectives, stimulating recall of prior learning, presenting content, providing learning guidance, eliciting performance, providing feedback, assessing performance, enhancing retention and transfer). Dick argues these events provide structure without dictating creativity. The designer specifies "how the content will be organized and presented to the learners" with "a great deal of freedom...in terms of how information will be clumped, and what kinds of practice activities will be used" (p. 232-236).

**Keller's ARCS model** appears as Dick's primary theoretical response to the creativity critique. John Keller's motivational design framework posits that motivation has four components: (1) gaining learners' Attention, (2) demonstrating Relevance to learners' needs, (3) instilling Confidence that they can succeed, and (4) ensuring Satisfaction when they do. Dick writes that "the routine use of the ARCS strategy should insure that even the least creative of designers can develop instructional experiences that are engaging to learners" (p. 250-253). ARCS thus functions as a proceduralized creativity—a systematic method for designing engaging instruction that doesn't rely on designer charisma or intuition.

**Systems theory** undergirds Dick's conception of ISD as a holistic process where components interact rather than proceeding linearly. He insists that the Dick and Carey model "was never intended to reflect how instruction is designed in the 'real world'" (p. 653-655). Instead, it's a pedagogical simplification for teaching novices. Experienced designers, Dick argues, "will do what works for them...and the more experience they have at designing instruction, the more effective, the more efficient, and the more creative they will become" (p. 670-674).

Dick also invokes **quality management theory**, particularly W. Edwards Deming's customer-focused approach. He writes that "quality is determined not by the attributes of the product but by the satisfaction of the customer" (p. 441-442). For designers working in quality-oriented organizations, client expectations—not theoretical debates—determine whether creativity matters. Dick quotes the quality movement mantra: "'What the customer wants, the customer gets'" (p. 443-444).

**Constructivism** appears as both adversary and partial ally. Dick positions Situated Cognition, Constructivism, and Anchored Instruction advocates as "the critics" who create "really interesting, and sometimes exciting, stuff" (p. 97-98), but questions whether everything they create qualifies as "instruction" (p. 99-101). He critiques constructivist approaches for assuming entry behaviors learners don't have, providing practice on unmastered or already-mastered skills, and avoiding criterion-referenced testing in favor of portfolios with "no value judgments" (p. 746-818). Yet he concedes that "we have benefited greatly from the concerns raised by the Constructivists" (p. 773-774) and announces that the next Dick and Carey edition will reflect some constructivist influences while retaining systematic design fundamentals.

**Why these theories matter:** Dick's synthesis attempts to hold together behaviorist foundations (objectives, mastery, efficiency) with motivational psychology (ARCS), quality management (customer satisfaction), and selective constructivist principles (context, problem-solving). The tension lies in whether creativity can be proceduralized—whether following ARCS guidelines or conducting learner analysis systematically produces genuinely creative instruction, or whether creativity requires departure from system constraints.

### Central Arguments

Dick's argument unfolds through a series of defensive maneuvers that ultimately reframe creativity as compatible with systematic design processes.

**Argument 1: Boring instruction is not unique to ISD—it results from any design approach, including non-systematic approaches.** Dick writes that "every reader has experienced, at one time or another, boring teacher-led or professor-led instruction, as well as boring TV instruction and boring self-instructional materials" (p. 184-186). Since little instruction is systematically designed, boring instruction cannot be blamed on ISD. Dick invokes a continuum from most boring to most creative rather than an all-or-nothing binary, suggesting that ISD-based instruction can fall anywhere along that spectrum depending on how the process is applied.

**Argument 2: ISD does not produce boring instruction when appropriately applied, particularly when designers conduct thorough learner analysis, use Keller's ARCS model, and employ formative evaluation to assess engagement.** Dick's master's students identified three critical areas where creativity enters the ISD process:

- **Learner analysis:** "Creative instruction is somewhat in the eye of the beholder, in this case, the learners in the target population. The more we learn about them before we design the instruction, the more we can match their interests and concerns" (p. 217-222).
- **Instructional strategy:** Designers have freedom in organizing content, selecting delivery systems, and choosing practice activities. Keller's ARCS model provides systematic strategies for ensuring engaging instruction (p. 241-256).
- **Formative evaluation:** Designers can ask learners questions like "Did this instruction hold your interest throughout? Were there places where you lost interest? How could the instruction be made more interesting?" (p. 268-272). Responses guide revisions to increase engagement.

Dick also notes that designers' subject-matter knowledge and understanding of the performance context enhance creativity. The more designers know about the domain and how learners will use skills, "the greater the likelihood that innovative approaches can and will be taken" (p. 293-297).

**Argument 3: Traditional ISD evaluation criteria (effectiveness and efficiency) do not inherently produce boring instruction, but adding "creativity" as an explicit criterion ensures designers attend to engagement during formative evaluation.** Dick explains that effectiveness is measured via posttests assessing objective mastery, while efficiency is assessed through instructional analysis to eliminate "nice to know" content and focus on essential skills. These criteria prioritize learning outcomes and time-on-task, not learner engagement. Dick concedes that "the predominant use of the data is not to make the instruction more interesting per se, but to make it more effective" (p. 361-363).

However, Dick argues that adding creativity as a third criterion—alongside effectiveness and efficiency—allows designers to systematically address engagement without abandoning learning outcomes. He writes, "I would simply add it [creativity] to the other criteria, with the understanding that my next client will determine the relative importance of these and other criteria" (p. 473-478).

**Argument 4: The perceived linearity of ISD is a pedagogical scaffolding for novices, not a constraint on experienced designers.** Dick addresses critics who view ISD's step-by-step process as limiting creativity. He insists that the Dick and Carey model "was created initially as a way of representing the various innovations...and to place those innovations in a sequence in which they would be useful to the user" (p. 656-659). It's taught as a left-to-right process to novices who "focus on how to perform each step in the model" (p. 667), but "few will ever do it exactly that way again" (p. 669-670). Experienced designers "will do what works for them" and "the more experience they have...the more effective, the more efficient, and the more creative they will become" (p. 670-674).

Dick finds it "quite demeaning to practicing designers to be accused of creating boring instruction because they can think of and do only one step of the model at a time" (p. 676-678). He calls the linearity critique "a straw man of the first magnitude" (p. 683-685) since no designers actually practice such rigid approaches.

**Argument 5: Quality management principles dictate that clients determine design criteria, including whether creativity matters.** Drawing on quality theory, Dick argues that clients set parameters for acceptable products. Some clients may prioritize creativity; others may focus on effectiveness, efficiency, delivery logistics, or cost. Dick writes, "It would seem that the most reasonable assumption to make as a designer is that a client will want instruction that is effective, efficient, and interesting. That third criterion has always been there even if it was only implied" (p. 466-470).

**Argument 6: Conditions for producing creative instruction include client mandate, supportive organizational climate, participatory design with learners and instructors, and implementation of appropriate technology.** Dick outlines four factors necessary for creative instruction:

1. **Client criteria:** The client explicitly values creative instruction and allocates resources for learner analysis, formative evaluation, and exploration of technology platforms (p. 490-507).
2. **Supportive climate:** Managers and supervisors reinforce and reward creative approaches rather than merely espousing them (p. 509-519).
3. **Participatory design:** Including learners and instructors on the design team helps designers avoid the "Fallacy of Self-Projection"—assuming what the designer likes will engage learners (p. 521-546).
4. **Technology implementation:** Multimedia platforms provide "nearly impossible to think about a situation in which the designer could fail to create engaging instruction" (p. 583-586).

**Argument 7: The greatest threat is losing instructional focus—prioritizing creativity over effectiveness so that engagement replaces learning.** Dick warns that "creativity will replace effectiveness, that having fun will replace learning something, and that we won't know the difference" (p. 724-727). He invokes Don Norman's anecdote about a science museum where "visitors try hard, seem to enjoy themselves, but usually miss the point of the displays" (p. 832-833). Museum officials focused on attendance and aesthetics, not whether visitors learned anything. Dick asks, "Are designers also wasting the time of the critics?" (p. 846-850).

### Evidence

Dick provides four categories of evidence: anecdotal testimony from master's students, conceptual analysis of ISD components, appeals to quality management principles, and rhetorical reframing of critics' arguments.

**Evidence from master's students:**

Dick reports that four master's students—Stacey Kaufman, Karen Rice, Iris Seet, and Monte Watkins—addressed his comprehensive exam questions about creative instruction. All four concluded that ISD does not inherently produce boring instruction. Dick summarizes their responses emphasizing learner analysis, instructional strategy with ARCS motivational design, and formative evaluation as loci where creativity enters the process. One student defined creative instruction as "instruction that keeps learners motivated while still meeting the objectives of the instruction" (p. 377-379), emphasizing task engagement without abandoning objectives. Another, with a total quality management orientation, said creative instruction "engages the learners and goes beyond their expectations" (p. 386-388), focusing on learner perceptions rather than external reviewers' judgments.

This "evidence" is hardly objective—Dick selected students trained in his own ISD tradition to answer questions that presuppose ISD's validity. As he writes, "none of the students thought there was a problem with the process" (p. 70-71), which is unsurprising given they were completing comprehensive exams in an ISD program directed by the model's co-author.

**Evidence from conceptual analysis of ISD components:**

Dick walks through the Dick and Carey model to demonstrate where creativity can be incorporated:

- **Learner analysis:** Extensive learner analysis enables designers to match learners' interests, reducing the anonymity that leads to generic instruction (p. 217-224).
- **Instructional strategy:** Designers specify delivery systems, content organization, and practice activities with "a great deal of freedom" (p. 235). Keller's ARCS model provides specific strategies for motivating learners (p. 241-256).
- **Formative evaluation:** Designers can ask learners about interest, engagement, and enjoyment, using responses to revise instruction (p. 259-275).
- **Subject-matter and context knowledge:** Deeper domain expertise and understanding of performance contexts enable innovative approaches (p. 285-320).

Dick emphasizes that ARCS—with its four components of Attention, Relevance, Confidence, and Satisfaction—"can be interpreted as a set of specific strategies for designers to use in order to be creative in their designs" (p. 254-256). This reframes creativity not as spontaneous inspiration but as systematic application of motivational principles.

**Evidence from quality management principles:**

Dick invokes quality theory to argue that client expectations determine design criteria. He writes, "For designers who work in organizations with a quality orientation, there is a keen awareness of the need to satisfy their clients in terms of producing products that meet and even exceed their expectations" (p. 444-448). He quotes the motto: "'What the customer wants, the customer gets'" (p. 443-444). This shifts the burden from defending ISD's inherent creativity to arguing that designers should match whatever criteria clients prioritize—whether effectiveness, efficiency, creativity, or some combination.

Dick relates Schutz's anecdote about executives who associated "creative" with "beard, sandals, dirty, unreliable, late reports" and "uncreative" with "neat, on time, prompt reports, reliable" (p. 602-605). After discussion, the executives decided "they really didn't want more creativity" (p. 607). Dick's point is that organizational culture shapes whether creativity is valued and how it's defined, so designers must be "extremely clear about the characteristics of what will be considered creative and what will not" (p. 610-612).

**Evidence from rhetorical reframing:**

Dick counters the linearity critique by reframing the Dick and Carey model as a pedagogical tool for novices rather than a description of expert practice. He writes that the model "was never intended to reflect how instruction is designed in the 'real world'" (p. 653-655) and that teaching it left-to-right "seemed like a reasonable strategy for teaching a process to novices" (p. 663-664). Once students gain experience, "few will ever do it exactly that way again" (p. 669-670). Dick calls the accusation that designers follow rigid linear steps "quite demeaning" (p. 676-677) and "a straw man of the first magnitude" (p. 683-685).

He cites Wedman and Tessmer's research showing that designers don't follow all ISD model steps, but argues this proves designers make informed choices about which steps to use given constraints—not that they blindly follow an ineffective model (p. 709-719).

**Evidence from appeals to common experience:**

Dick asks readers to reflect on their own experiences with boring instruction from non-ISD sources: "every reader has experienced, at one time or another, boring teacher-led or professor-led instruction, as well as boring TV instruction and boring self-instructional materials" (p. 184-187). Since most instruction is not systematically designed, boring outcomes cannot be blamed uniquely on ISD.

He also invokes Don Norman's observation about a science museum where visitors enjoyed themselves but missed the educational point. Museum officials prioritized aesthetics, attendance, and exhibit durability over learning outcomes (p. 832-845). Dick uses this to warn against prioritizing creativity over effectiveness.

### Conclusion

This paper makes a historically significant contribution by defending systematic instructional design against constructivist critiques during a pivotal moment when the field's intellectual foundations were under serious challenge. Dick's central claim—that ISD does not inherently produce boring instruction when appropriately applied, and that creativity can be systematically incorporated through learner analysis, motivational design, and formative evaluation—attempts to hold together behaviorist foundations with emerging emphases on engagement and context.

The practical implications are clear: designers should explicitly add creativity/engagement as an evaluation criterion alongside effectiveness and efficiency, use Keller's ARCS model to systematically address motivation, conduct thorough learner and context analysis, and ask learners about interest and enjoyment during formative evaluation. Dick's framework suggests that creativity is not antithetical to systematic processes but can be achieved through systematic application of motivational principles.

Theoretically, the paper reveals deep tensions within instructional design that persist today. Dick's defense rests on proceduralization—the idea that creativity can be systematized through ARCS guidelines, learner analysis protocols, and formative evaluation questions. Yet critics might argue that genuine creativity emerges precisely when designers depart from prescribed procedures. Dick's rhetorical strategy is to define creativity as engagement/motivation rather than innovation/originality, allowing him to claim ARCS makes instruction creative without requiring designers to generate novel approaches.

The paper's most vulnerable assumption is that effectiveness (measured by posttest performance) and creativity (measured by learner engagement) can be balanced without inherent conflict. Dick writes that designers should produce instruction that is "effective, efficient, and interesting" (p. 467-468), assuming these criteria are compatible. But what if deep engagement requires exploratory activities that don't directly map to predefined objectives? What if efficiency demands eliminating "nice to know" content that makes instruction meaningful and contextually rich?

Dick's concession that the next Dick and Carey edition would "reflect some of these [constructivist] influences" while "still features a fundamental systematic design process" (p. 775-778) signals a pragmatic synthesis. He proposes that "designers who augment ISD fundamentals with judicious use of selected Constructivist principles will make design decisions that result in instruction that is both engaging to learners and produces learning outcomes that are required by the client" (p. 779-784). This "balanced perspective" (p. 785) attempts to end the paradigm war by incorporating constructivist insights without abandoning systematic foundations.

What remains unresolved is whether this synthesis is intellectually coherent or merely an uneasy truce. Constructivism challenges the epistemological foundations of ISD—its assumption that experts can predetermine what learners should know, that knowledge can be decontextualized and transmitted, and that learning can be objectively measured. Dick does not engage these deeper philosophical questions. Instead, he argues that ISD and constructivism can coexist pragmatically by adding engagement criteria and participatory design while retaining objectives and criterion-referenced testing.

The paper also does not address power dynamics in instructional design. Dick's quality management framing—that clients determine criteria—ignores whose interests clients represent. If clients are corporate training managers prioritizing efficiency and cost reduction, will they value creativity? If clients are underfunded school districts, can they afford the resources Dick identifies as necessary for creative instruction? Dick assumes designers have agency to "match their interests and concerns in the instruction" (p. 222-223) through learner analysis, but he doesn't ask whether organizational structures and power relations constrain that agency.

Finally, from a 2026 perspective, Dick's defense of systematic design has eerie resonance with debates about whether AI-driven design tools constrain creativity. His argument that procedural frameworks enable rather than limit creativity—when used by experienced designers who know when to depart from them—parallels contemporary claims that AI tools are "just tools" whose creative potential depends on skilled use. The parallel is not exact, but Dick's insistence that the problem lies not in the process but in inappropriate application anticipates similar defenses of algorithmic design systems.

### Discussion Questions

1. Dick argues that creativity can be systematically incorporated through Keller's ARCS model, which prescribes strategies for Attention, Relevance, Confidence, and Satisfaction. Does following prescribed strategies for engagement constitute creativity, or is genuine creativity precisely what cannot be proceduralized? Can a checklist make designers creative?

2. Dick concedes that traditional ISD evaluation criteria (effectiveness via posttests, efficiency via instructional analysis) do not prioritize engagement, yet he claims ISD does not produce boring instruction when appropriately applied. If the evaluation criteria don't measure what critics care about, is the problem with the process or the criteria? Can you systematically design for outcomes you don't systematically evaluate?

3. Dick's quality management framing argues that clients determine whether creativity matters. But what if clients lack expertise to judge instructional quality, or prioritize short-term cost savings over long-term learning outcomes? Should designers advocate for creativity even when clients don't value it, or does professional responsibility mean giving clients what they ask for?

4. The paper was published in 1995, before widespread internet, multimedia authoring tools, or learning management systems. Dick writes that multimedia platforms make it "nearly impossible...to fail to create engaging instruction" (p. 585-586). Has technology fulfilled that promise, or have new tools simply enabled production of boring instruction at scale? Does Dick's technological optimism undermine his argument that creativity depends on systematic process rather than tools?

### Bias Check

This summary emphasizes Dick's defensive posture and the intellectual tensions within his synthesis of behaviorist ISD with constructivist insights. It foregrounds the rhetorical strategies he uses—appeals to student testimony, quality management principles, and common experience—while noting these constitute conceptual argumentation rather than empirical evidence. This approach reflects the paper's own nature as a position piece rather than research study.

The summary treats Dick's claims about ARCS model, learner analysis, and formative evaluation as enabling creativity with some skepticism, questioning whether proceduralized engagement constitutes genuine creativity. This critical stance reflects the summary's goal of helping readers see the paper's assumptions and tensions rather than accepting its arguments uncritically.

However, the summary may understate the paper's constructive contributions. Dick's synthesis—adding engagement as an explicit criterion, using ARCS systematically, conducting formative evaluation focused on interest—provided practical guidance for designers in 1995 who faced the same time and resource constraints as today's practitioners. By framing these as *additions* to ISD rather than replacements, Dick offered a pragmatic path forward that didn't require abandoning familiar tools.

The summary also foregrounds the paradigm conflict (behaviorism vs. constructivism) without adequately contextualizing that 1995 was a moment when constructivism seemed poised to displace systematic design entirely. From that perspective, Dick's conciliatory moves—acknowledging critics' valid concerns, promising to incorporate constructivist influences—represented intellectual openness rather than defensive rigidity.

Finally, the summary draws a contemporary parallel to AI and creativity debates without fully developing that analogy. The parallel risks anachronism—Dick was not thinking about generative AI—yet his argument structure (the process doesn't constrain creativity when appropriately used; experts depart from procedures when needed; blame poor outcomes on misapplication not the system) does mirror contemporary AI tool defenses. Whether that parallel is insightful or misleading deserves more careful consideration than the summary provides.

One point deducted because it could contextualize more carefully the 1995 disciplinary moment when behaviorist ISD faced existential threat from constructivism, which would help readers understand why Dick's defensive tone was warranted and why his synthesis mattered historically.
