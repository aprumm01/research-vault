# Fundamentals of Performance Technology: The Human Performance Technology Model

## Summary
This chapter provides a comprehensive overview of Human Performance Technology (HPT) and presents the HPT Model as developed by the International Society for Performance Improvement (ISPI). The authors define HPT as the systematic process of linking business strategy and goals with the workforce responsible for achieving those goals, applying a systems view of performance that emphasizes analysis, intervention selection, change management, and evaluation. The chapter traces the scientific foundations of HPT through contributions from key figures including Thomas Gilbert (Behavior Engineering Model), Geary Rummler (systems view), Robert Mager and Peter Pipe (performance analysis), Joe Harless (front-end analysis), and Dale Brethower (behavioral systems analysis).

The HPT Model consists of five phases: Performance Analysis (examining organizational analysis, environmental analysis, gap analysis, and cause analysis), Intervention Selection and Design (choosing from instructional and non-instructional interventions), Intervention Implementation and Change, and Evaluation (formative, summative, confirmative, and meta evaluation). The authors emphasize that HPT is empirical, using data-driven analysis rather than intuition, and that effective performance improvement requires addressing both environmental factors (information, resources, incentives) and individual factors (knowledge/skills, capacity, motivation).

## Key Concepts
- **Human Performance Technology (HPT)**: A systematic approach to improving productivity that starts from organizational goals and uses analysis, intervention, change management, and evaluation
- **Gilbert's Behavior Engineering Model (BEM)**: A six-cell matrix examining environmental supports (information, resources, incentives) and individual repertory (knowledge, capacity, motives)
- **Performance Gap**: The difference between actual state and desired state of performance
- **Exemplary Performance**: The standard against which actual performance is measured, representing what the best performers achieve

## Theoretical Framework
HPT draws from multiple disciplines including behavioral psychology, systems theory, organizational development, and cognitive science. Gilbert's foundational work established that worthy performance equals the ratio of accomplishment to behavior (P = A/B), and that environmental factors are typically more powerful determinants of performance than individual factors. The systems view emphasizes that organizations must be analyzed at multiple levels (organizational, process, job/performer) to understand and improve performance.

## Methods
HPT practitioners use a systematic methodology that includes: (1) Performance Analysis to identify gaps and their causes; (2) Intervention Selection based on cause analysis rather than jumping to training solutions; (3) Implementation with attention to change management; and (4) Multiple forms of evaluation including formative, summative, confirmative (long-term impact), and meta evaluation (evaluation of the evaluation process).

## Main Arguments
- HPT focuses on worthy performance, defined as valued accomplishments that justify their costs
- Most performance problems are caused by environmental factors, not individual deficiencies
- Training alone is rarely sufficient; interventions must address the root causes of performance gaps
- Systematic analysis must precede intervention selection to ensure solutions match problems
- HPT practitioners must be ethical, taking a systems view that considers impacts on all stakeholders
- The field has scientific foundations that distinguish it from intuition-based approaches

## Limitations & Critiques
- HPT can appear mechanistic if applied without sensitivity to human and organizational dynamics
- The comprehensive analytical process may be seen as time-consuming in fast-moving business environments
- Implementation requires significant organizational buy-in and change management expertise
- Some critics argue HPT overemphasizes measurable outcomes at the expense of harder-to-quantify human values
- The field continues to evolve, with ongoing debates about scope and boundaries
