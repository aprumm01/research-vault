# "Everyone's Using It, But No One Is Allowed to Talk About It": College Students' Experiences Navigating Higher Education in a Generative AI World

## Summary
This qualitative study examines the environmental and social factors that influence college students' use of generative AI through semi-structured interviews with 23 students at a large public university in the United States. Key findings reveal that institutional pressures (deadlines, grading, exam cycles) drive AI adoption even when students believe it undermines their learning; peer micro-communities establish de facto AI norms that override official policies; campus-wide "AI shame" pushes usage underground; and current institutional policies are perceived as generic, inconsistent, and routinely violated. The paper argues that student AI use is a situated practice requiring environmental—not just individual—intervention. Published as arXiv preprint (2026).

## Key Concepts
- **Situated AI practice**: Framing student AI use as shaped by environmental context (institutional pressures, social norms, policy structures) rather than purely individual choice or motivation
- **AI shame**: A campus-wide phenomenon where AI use is widespread but socially suppressed—"everybody uses it but nobody talks about it"—pushing usage underground and preventing honest institutional conversations
- **Intention-behavior gap**: Students' expressed values about appropriate AI use diverge from their actual behaviors under environmental pressure, particularly around deadlines and grading
- **Peer micro-communities**: Small, informal social groups that establish actual AI norms in practice regardless of official institutional policies, operating through social observation and implicit negotiation
- **Institutional AI policy perception**: Students view existing policies as generic, outdated, inconsistent across instructors, and largely disconnected from their actual practices—producing routine noncompliance
- **Value-based self-regulation**: Students develop personal boundaries about AI use based on their academic values, but environmental pressures create a gap between intentions and behaviors
- **Environmental factors**: External structural conditions—academic calendars, assignment deadlines, grading regimes, institutional rules, social norms—that shape student AI use independently of individual preferences

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on self-determination theory and situational frameworks for understanding academic behavior, particularly pre-AI research on academic misconduct showing that environmental factors (workload, assessment timing, peer influence) independently shape student behavior beyond individual motivations. The theoretical move is applying this "situated behavior" lens to AI use, arguing that existing research over-explains AI adoption through individual-level factors (motivation, beliefs, demographics) while under-explaining environmental determinants.

The study also engages with social norms research, drawing on work showing that peer influence is a powerful driver of technology adoption and academic behavior. The "AI shame" phenomenon is analyzed through the lens of norm formation and enforcement in educational contexts, where official policies and informal social norms may operate in tension.

## Methods
Semi-structured interview study with 23 students (undergraduates, master's students, and doctoral students) at a large public university in the United States, recruited through academic Slack and Discord channels and campus flyers between May–July 2025. The interview protocol covered: general AI usage patterns, motivations for using or avoiding AI, environmental influences on AI use, responses to institutional policies, and recommendations for institutional change. Saturation was reached after approximately 18 interviews; five additional pre-scheduled interviews were completed for a total of 23. Participants received $40 Amazon gift cards. The five-section protocol was pilot-tested and refined.

## Main Arguments
- Institutional pressures—deadlines, grading schemes, exam timing—drive students to use AI even when they believe it undermines their learning, creating a structural conflict between academic incentives and educational goals that individual willpower cannot resolve
- Social influences operate through peer micro-communities that establish de facto AI norms regardless of official policies, with these informal norms often more powerful in shaping behavior than institutional guidelines
- The "AI shame" culture on campuses—where use is widespread but socially unacknowledged—actively prevents honest conversations between students and instructors about AI use, creating an information void that makes effective policy impossible
- Existing AI policies are perceived as generic, inconsistent across instructors, and disconnected from actual student practices, leading to routine noncompliance that students do not experience as transgressive
- Students possess genuine values about AI use and desire to maintain authentic learning, but the environment systematically undermines their capacity to act on these values—suggesting that individual AI literacy interventions are insufficient without structural change
- Students recommend collaborative policy development (involving students), increased in-person evaluation to more accurately assess learning, and AI-literacy education that goes beyond tool instruction to include responsible integration frameworks

## Limitations & Critiques
The sample of 23 students from a single large public university in the US limits generalizability, particularly to smaller institutions, private universities, community colleges, or non-US contexts. The study captures perceptions and self-reported behaviors rather than behavioral data, introducing potential for rationalization and social desirability bias. The focus on one institution means the specific norms, pressures, and policies observed may not be representative.

The study was conducted in 2025, after AI tools had been widely available for over two years, which may mean findings reflect a particular phase of adoption rather than stable long-term patterns. The paper's policy recommendations (collaborative policy development, in-person evaluation) are reasonable but relatively conventional, and the paper does not address potential unintended consequences of these approaches.
