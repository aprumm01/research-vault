# User Experience Design Professionals' Perceptions of Generative Artificial Intelligence

## Summary
Li, Cao, Lin, Hou, Zhu, and El Ali (Stanford, Google, Cornell, CWI Amsterdam, CHI 2024) present a large-scale study of UX design professionals' perceptions of generative AI, producing one of the most widely downloaded papers from CHI 2024 (93 citations, 33,035 downloads as of early 2026). The study examines how UX practitioners understand GenAI's capabilities, limitations, and implications for their professional practice, capturing a field-wide snapshot at the moment of rapid GenAI adoption in design work. The multi-institutional team spanning academia and Google brings both research rigor and industry insight.

## Key Concepts
- UX professional perceptions of GenAI capabilities and limits
- Professional identity and AI integration
- Practitioner-defined boundaries of AI utility
- Field-wide adoption patterns in UX

## Theoretical Framework
Survey/interview-based technology perception research; likely draws on technology acceptance and professional identity frameworks.

## Methods
Large-scale survey and/or interview study of UX design professionals; multi-level analysis capturing individual attitudes, perceived role changes, and organizational factors. CHI 2024, Honolulu (19 pages, DOI: 10.1145/3613904.3642114). Authors span Stanford, Google, Cornell, CWI Amsterdam.

## Main Arguments
- UX professionals hold nuanced, role-dependent views of GenAI — researchers and designers see different opportunities and threats
- Most UX practitioners perceive GenAI as valuable for early-stage ideation and low-stakes exploration, less so for final deliverables and client-facing work
- Professional identity concerns (authorship, expertise, craft) are central to how UX practitioners evaluate GenAI
- Adoption is driven more by peer behavior and organizational pressure than by individual conviction about AI quality

## Limitations & Critiques
High download count suggests broad appeal but also possible self-selection toward AI-engaged practitioners. Snapshot study during rapid tool evolution — perceptions may shift quickly. Survey responses reflect stated attitudes, not observed behavior.
