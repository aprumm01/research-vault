# User Experience Design Professionals' Perceptions of Generative AI (Li et al., Stanford/Google/Cornell)

## Abstract

This research article examines how user experience design professionals perceive and experience generative artificial intelligence tools in their practice. Drawing on interviews and surveys with UX practitioners at Stanford, Google, and Cornell, the authors investigate how designers think about AI's role in their workflows, what concerns they hold about AI-generated designs, and how they negotiate their professional identity and creative authorship in relation to AI capabilities.

## Summary

Li et al. provide a large-scale empirical account of how UX design professionals perceive GenAI tools, finding that practitioners hold nuanced, role-differentiated views — researchers, designers, and managers perceive AI's impact differently, and concerns about quality, bias, and professional value vary by specialization. The paper is significant because it grounds AI tool adoption research in the specific professional context of UX, contributing practitioner-focused data to a debate often driven by speculative or consumer-focused accounts. Published at CHI 2024, it has already accumulated substantial citations (93+).

## Key Concepts
- UX practitioner perceptions of GenAI
- Role differentiation in AI adoption (researcher vs. designer vs. manager)
- Creative authorship and professional value under AI
- Quality and bias concerns in AI-generated UX artifacts

## Theoretical Framework
Human-computer interaction and professional practice frameworks; draws on studies of technology adoption in professional contexts and UX design epistemology; uses empirical survey and interview methods.

## Methods
Mixed-methods study with UX design professionals from industry settings (Stanford University, Google LLC, Cornell University, CWI Amsterdam); combination of survey and interview data; thematic and statistical analysis of perceptions and concerns.

## Main Arguments
- UX professionals hold differentiated views about GenAI based on their role (designer, researcher, manager)
- Quality, bias, and professional identity concerns are prominent but vary by specialization
- Practitioners are navigating AI not as a simple tool but as a shift in professional norms
- The field needs practitioner-centered frameworks for AI adoption, not just technical evaluations
