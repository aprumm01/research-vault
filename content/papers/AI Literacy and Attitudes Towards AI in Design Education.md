# AI Literacy and Attitudes Towards AI in Design Education

## Summary
Schauer et al. investigate AI literacy and attitudes among communication design (HTW Berlin, N=27) and architectural design students (University of Florence, N=35) through mixed-methods surveys combining qualitative open responses and quantitative SNAIL assessment. Students rated their AI literacy as moderate (3.22-4.5 on 5-point scale), viewing AI as helpful for productivity while expressing concerns about creativity loss, job displacement, and intellectual property issues. Findings reveal competency gaps in understanding technical foundations (e.g., natural language processing 2.48-3.06, what AI is 3.41), legal frameworks (3.22-3.33), and identifying AI-supported technologies (3.22-3.34). Students feel confident giving everyday examples (3.40-4.5), critically evaluating societal impact (3.74-3.91), and explaining data’s role (3.29-4.15), but struggle with technical depth and ethical-legal dimensions. The study argues for structured AI literacy integration across design curricula addressing these gaps.

## Key Concepts
- AI literacy dimensions: Technical Understanding (data-driven aspects, theoretical foundations), Critical Appraisal (ethical evaluation, legal concerns, outcome assessment), Practical Application (identifying AI examples, determining AI usage)
- Human-AI co-creation: Four modes—AI-enhanced human creativity, human-only "true" creativity, blind reliance leading to plagiarism, AI undermining motivation/self-concept
- Generative synesthesia: Blend of human exploration and AI exploitation shifting from human-centered to co-creativity paradigms
- SNAIL instrument: Scale for Non-Experts’ Assessment of AI Literacy measuring competencies across three domains
- Design process phases: Analysis (data gathering, pattern identification), synthesis (ideation, automation, prototyping), evaluation (testing, feedback, usability assessment)
- Literacy gaps: Areas where students lack confidence—technical architecture, legal frameworks, identifying AI-embedded technologies, NLP applications
- Attitudes toward AI: Cognitive (career intentions), affective (life relevance), behavioral (usage patterns) components shaping acceptance/resistance
- Competency integration points: Design law/ethics classes, design foundations, material/sustainability courses as potential AI curriculum anchors

## Theoretical Framework
Builds on AI literacy frameworks (Long & Magerko’s critical assessment, effective interaction, tool utilization; Ng et al.’s integration with creativity/evaluation thinking skills) and SNAIL assessment instrument (Laupichler et al.’s three-domain model). Applies design process theory (Lawson’s analysis-synthesis-evaluation interplay, Daly et al.’s experience-shaped paths) to situate AI roles. Draws on creativity definitions (Boden’s "new, surprising, valuable" criteria) and human-AI co-creation typologies (Suh et al., Ivcevic & Grandinetti’s four collaboration modes). References Verganti et al.’s generative AI integration across design lifecycle. Engages attitudes research showing AI positivity correlates with competence/connection (Bergdahl et al.) and understanding levels predict favorable views (Marrone et al.).

## Methods
Mixed-methods survey with two-part structure: (1) Qualitative open-ended questions on general AI opinions and topic interests, analyzed via grounded theory coding for recurring themes; (2) Quantitative 5-point Likert scale using 15 selected SNAIL items focusing on Critical Appraisal and Practical Application (excluded Technical Understanding questions). Communication design sample: HTW Berlin, N=27 (14 in semester 1: 4 male/10 female, ages 20-31; 13 in semester 2: 3 male/9 female/1 undisclosed, ages 19-29). Architectural design sample: University of Florence, N=35 (10 male/24 female/1 non-binary, ages 21-67). Statistical analysis: median test for group differences, Levene test for variance comparison. Qualitative coding identified themes like helpful tool, societal value, ethical risks, creativity limitations, job concerns, intellectual property issues.

## Main Arguments
- Design students recognize AI’s productivity potential (efficiency, task acceleration, process simplification) but balance enthusiasm with concerns about creativity loss, job displacement, and copyright violations
- AI literacy among design students is moderate rather than high, with significant gaps in technical foundations (how AI learns, data influences on behavior 2.86-3.29) despite strong confidence in everyday examples (3.40-4.5)
- Students feel most competent in affective/practical domains (recognizing AI in daily life, societal impact reflection) but least confident in cognitive/technical aspects (NLP applications 2.48-3.06, defining AI 3.41, legal frameworks 3.22-3.33)
- Gap between applied confidence (using AI tools, identifying applications 3.93) and foundational understanding (technical architecture, data role, legal problems) mirrors pattern found in educators (Lérias et al.’s 2.86 foundational gap despite 3.85 application proficiency)
- Design education currently lacks systematic AI integration despite identifiable touchpoints (design law, ethics, foundations, materials, sustainability courses), creating ad-hoc rather than structured learning
- Attitudes reveal dual consciousness: viewing AI as "natural progression" and "human’s future" while fearing it "limits creativity" and causes people to "forget to use own mind"
- Cross-disciplinary interest patterns: communication design emphasizes art/media generation (11 mentions), social impact/ethics (8); architecture focuses on construction applications (10), medical crossovers (5), 3D modeling (1)
- Structured curricular integration is necessary to address competency gaps, particularly in ethical considerations, specific AI technology use, legal/ownership issues, and technical understanding of how AI systems function

## Limitations & Critiques
- Small sample sizes (N=27 communication design, N=35 architecture) limit generalizability across design disciplines and educational contexts
- Single-country samples (Germany, Italy) may not capture cross-cultural variations in AI literacy and attitudes despite Bergdahl et al.’s evidence of national differences in AI positivity
- SNAIL instrument selection bias: Excluded Technical Understanding items may underestimate true knowledge gaps by focusing only on Critical Appraisal and Practical Application domains
- Self-reported confidence rather than objective assessment: Likert ratings reflect perceived competence, not demonstrated skills; students may over- or underestimate abilities
- No baseline or control group: Cannot determine if design students’ literacy differs from other non-technical disciplines or general student populations
- Cross-sectional snapshot: Cannot assess literacy development over time, track learning trajectories, or evaluate intervention effectiveness
- Age range outlier (one 67-year-old in predominantly 19-33 sample) may skew architectural design results without age-stratified analysis
- Grounded theory coding conducted by researchers without inter-rater reliability metrics or member checking to validate thematic interpretations
- Survey timing relative to curriculum exposure not reported: Unknown whether students had formal AI instruction, informal learning, or no educational intervention
- Missing links between attitudes and behavior: Positive/negative viewpoints not correlated with actual AI tool usage patterns, adoption rates, or integration in design projects
