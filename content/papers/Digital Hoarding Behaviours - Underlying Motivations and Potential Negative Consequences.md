# Digital Hoarding Behaviours: Underlying Motivations and Potential Negative Consequences

## Summary

Sweeten, Sillence, and Neave authored this 2018 paper published in Computers in Human Behavior, representing collaboration between Psychology & Communication Technologies and the Hoarding Research Group at Northumbria University, UK. This qualitative study is among the first to systematically investigate "digital hoarding"—the excessive accumulation of digital materials such as emails, photographs, and files accompanied by difficulty deleting them. The researchers surveyed 46 participants (24 females, 21 males, ages 20-52) using open-ended questions about their digital management behaviors, barriers to deletion, and perceived problems from accumulation. The paper's significance lies in extending clinical hoarding research into the digital domain, identifying five barriers to deletion (keeping data for future/just in case, keeping data as evidence, laziness/time constraints, emotional attachment, "not my server, not my problem") and four negative consequences (impaired productivity, psychological wellbeing impacts, cybersecurity threats, crossover with physical hoarding). This research connects to sustainability by illuminating the human behavioral factors underlying data accumulation, which drives storage infrastructure growth and associated environmental costs.

## Research Overview

The study addresses two core research questions: "what are the barriers to deleting digital data?" and "is accumulating excessive amounts of digital data viewed as problematic?" (Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 55). The overarching aim is "to provide a valuable starting point for understanding digital hoarding" (Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 55).

The methodology was qualitative, using an online open-ended questionnaire. Participants "were asked to respond to a series of online open ended questions which explored: current email storage and deletion behaviour; web browser behaviour, file management practices and overall attitudes towards personal digital management behaviour and its implications" (Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 55). Questions were developed from concepts within the "Hoarding Rating Scale" (Tolin, Frost, & Steketee, 2010).

Key concepts include **digital hoarding**, defined in prior literature as "'the accumulation of digital files to the point of loss of perspective, which eventually results in stress and disorganisation'" (van Bennekom et al., 2015, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 54). **Personal Information Management (PIM)** describes "the ways in which an individual collects, stores, organizes and retrieves their digital items" (Boardman & Sasse, 2004, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 55).

Data analysis employed thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke (2006): "Two authors independently coded 20% of the responses, and compared their outputs using the Jaccard index, achieving 90% agreement on inter-rater reliability" (Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 55).

## Theoretical Framework

The paper draws on **clinical hoarding disorder** research as its primary theoretical foundation. Physical hoarding "become[s] pathological, and the person acquires a large amount of seemingly 'useless' items, shows great unwillingness to discard any items, thus leading to extensive clutter in their living spaces" causing "significant distress and/or impairment of normal functioning" (Frost & Gross, 1993, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 54). The study explores whether similar patterns manifest digitally.

**Personal Information Management (PIM)** theory provides a framework for understanding digital organization behaviors. Research shows "that users typically keep half of the emails they receive and reply to about a third of them" while "few people engage in the pro-active management or 'clean-up' of that stored information, partly because they are unsure about the projected value of the information itself" (Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 55).

The paper references research on **personality and information management**, specifically the "Big Five" personality characteristics: "'filers' and 'pilers' in terms of well-known traits including conscientiousness (more conscientious = better organised) and neuroticism (more neurotic individuals responded to work pressure by keeping more information available to hand on their desktop)" (Massey, TenBrook, Tatum, and Whittaker, 2014, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 55).

**Attachment theory** is implicitly invoked through findings on emotional connections to digital possessions: "Physical hoarders share the same high level of attachment to their possessions, with such attachments interfering with the ability to discard any items" (Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 59).

## Central Arguments

The paper's central argument is that digital hoarding represents a distinct behavioral phenomenon with identifiable motivations and consequences that parallels but also differs from physical hoarding. The authors contend that "digital hoarding could clearly have negative consequences for the individual and for organizations, but information relating to digital hoarding behaviours remains sparse" (Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 55).

Supporting sub-claims include:

1. **Five distinct barriers to deletion exist**: "(1) keeping data for the future/just in case; (2) keeping data as evidence; (3) lazy/time consuming; (4) emotional attachment to data; (5) not my server-not my problem" (Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 56).

2. **Digital data has emotional value comparable to physical possessions**: "Digital photos were particularly valuable: 'No I like to keep everything. Photos are special, I wouldn't want to get rid of them. It would be difficult because I would feel that I'm deleting little bits of me and my past'" (P45, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 57).

3. **Unlimited storage enables hoarding**: "The fact that there is a sense of unlimited space acts as a barrier to deleting data at all... 'I'm not bothered about my inbox. Did not think there would be 9201 emails in my deleted though. I do not see data as a physical thing and we have unlimited data at work so I don't really care'" (P23, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 58).

4. **Four negative consequences identified**: "1) Effects on productivity; 2) Effects on psychological wellbeing; 3) Cybersecurity issues; 4) Links with physical hoarding" (Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 58).

5. **Crossover between digital and physical hoarding behaviors**: "A number of participants also drew parallels with their physical behaviour: 'I seem to hoard a lot outside of the virtual world also, perhaps there's a crossover... I knew I hoard physical items and assumed I'd hoard some aspects of digital items but not to this amount'" (P7, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 58).

## Evidence

The paper provides rich qualitative evidence through participant quotes. On **keeping data "just in case"**: "'I struggle really hard deleting folders. I always fear that what they contain will be very important to me in the future. Even though I do not use them, I still do not wish to delete them just in case'" (P4, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 56).

On **keeping data as evidence**: "'Also keep as proof that I was asked to do a certain task'" (P39, work context) and "'Proof I have passed a message on / booked a job in / expressed concern / chased something up with a supplier etc'" (P27, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 56).

On **laziness/time constraints**: "'I'm just too lazy to delete them. Life is busy and I feel that I have better things to do than to spend my life going through my backlog of e-mails and sorting them out'" (P9, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 57).

On **productivity impacts**: "'When something is too cluttered it makes it difficult to be efficient. I waste too much time looking for things when they are out of order'" (P37) and "'I think it strongly gets in the way of productivity. I used to have upwards of 10 browser tabs open at any given time, and since stripping back to only opening what I need only when I need it I have noticed a significant increase in productivity'" (P29, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 58).

On **psychological impacts**: "'Stressful. Although not actually taking up physical space, it 'feels' clutter like'" (P42) and "'I would be distraught. My entire life is saved to my computer (and backed up elsewhere)'" (P5, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 58).

On **cybersecurity concerns**: "'To elaborate, if a hacker hacked into my laptop, they could find all my pictures, all my music taste and most importantly all my interactions via email (both formal and informal)... This could then lead to a potential digital impersonation of me'" (P4, cited in Sweeten et al., 2018, p. 58).

**Limitations** include the small sample size (46 participants), reliance on self-reported behavior, and absence of clinical hoarding assessment instruments for comparison. The study did not measure actual data volumes or environmental impacts.

## Conclusion

For recall six months from now, this paper provides qualitative evidence that digital hoarding is a distinct behavioral phenomenon warranting study separate from physical hoarding. Five barriers to deletion were identified: (1) "just in case" future value uncertainty—the most common barrier, where potential future need outweighs current storage costs; (2) evidentiary value—data kept as proof of work or communication; (3) time/laziness—deletion seen as unrewarding, low-priority task; (4) emotional attachment—particularly for photos and personal communications that represent "bits of me and my past"; (5) "not my server, not my problem"—unique to workplace contexts where unlimited cloud storage removes personal incentive for curation. Four negative consequences identified: impaired productivity from cluttered systems; psychological stress and anxiety about data volume; cybersecurity vulnerabilities from accumulated sensitive information; crossover effects where digital hoarders also hoard physically. Key sustainability insight: unlimited storage capacity (especially in workplace cloud systems) removes natural constraints on accumulation, enabling behaviors that collectively drive infrastructure growth. The "not my server" finding is particularly relevant—when individuals don't perceive storage limits or costs, they have no incentive to manage data sustainably. This suggests organizational policies and interface designs that make storage costs visible might promote more sustainable data behaviors.

## Discussion Questions

1. How might the finding that unlimited storage enables digital hoarding inform the design of more sustainable cloud services or organizational policies around data retention?

2. The paper identifies emotional attachment as a barrier to deletion, particularly for photos. How should sustainability-focused interventions balance environmental concerns against the genuine psychological value people derive from digital possessions?

3. The "not my server, not my problem" barrier suggests a tragedy of the commons in organizational data storage. What governance mechanisms might align individual data behaviors with organizational sustainability goals?

4. Given the crossover between digital and physical hoarding behaviors, might clinical interventions for hoarding disorder need to address both domains, and what does this suggest about the underlying psychological mechanisms?
