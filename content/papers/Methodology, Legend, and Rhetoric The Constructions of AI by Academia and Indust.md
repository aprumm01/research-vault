## Summary
Uses Social Construction of Technology (SCOT) theory to analyze how academic researchers, commercial sector actors, and policymakers construct AI differently in the context of lifelong learning, drawing on 34 in-depth interviews and document analysis. Finds three distinct technological frames and argues that commercial sector practices currently wield the most power in shaping AI-for-learning design.

## Key Concepts
- Social Construction of Technology (SCOT)
- Technological frames
- Interpretive flexibility
- Lifelong learning and AI
- Fourth Industrial Revolution narratives

## Theoretical Framework
SCOT (Pinch & Bijker 1984); technological frames; Jasanoff's sociotechnical imaginaries; positions AI as a complex sociotechnical artifact shaped by competing social groups rather than a neutral technical tool.

## Methods
34 in-depth interviews with stakeholders from academia, commerce, and policy in the UK; document analysis of AI and lifelong learning policy and industry materials; qualitative thematic analysis.

## Main Arguments
- Academia, industry, and policy each construct AI through distinct "technological frames" with different design implications
- Commercial sector practices hold the most power in determining how AI for learning is actually designed and deployed
- Greater diversity of relevant social groups and stronger focus on desired educational outcomes are needed for democratic education
- More interaction among the three groups could reduce the dominance of commercial framings

## Limitations & Critiques
UK-focused interviews limiting international generalizability; small N (34); conducted in 2020 before the LLM era—findings may not apply to current generative AI; SCOT framework may not capture structural power asymmetries adequately.
