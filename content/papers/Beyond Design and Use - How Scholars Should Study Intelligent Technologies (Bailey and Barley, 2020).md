# Beyond Design and Use: How Scholars Should Study Intelligent Technologies

## Summary
This paper proposes a unified research agenda for studying intelligent technologies (AI, machine learning, robotics, IoT, analytics) that extends beyond current approaches focused on design and use. The authors argue that current discussions of AI's implications for work gloss four critical issues: variation (how technologies affect different contexts differently), power (whose interests shape technology development), ideology (the values embedded in design choices), and institutions (broader societal changes triggered by workplace technologies).

The authors conceptualize technology's trajectory as a timeline extending from design through implementation to use, with most current research clustering around design studies (SCOT tradition) and studies of use (technology, work, and organization tradition). They call for extending this timeline in both directions: to the left to examine power and ideology shaping technology development, and to the right to consider institutional changes (family, transportation, education) affected by workplace technologies.

The paper illustrates this unified approach through the example of self-driving commercial trucks, showing how studies could examine variation in trucker work, design decisions by manufacturers, ideology in industry discourse, power dynamics in regulatory comments, and institutional implications for family life and transportation infrastructure.

## Key Concepts
- **Variation**: How the same technology produces different outcomes in different organizational contexts due to implementation choices and local factors
- **Power**: Whose interests and agendas shape technology design, adoption, and deployment decisions
- **Ideology**: The cultural perspectives and values (e.g., artificial vs. augmented intelligence) that guide technology development
- **Institutions**: Broader societal structures (family, transportation, education) affected by workplace technology changes
- **Technology Timeline**: Framework spanning design, implementation, use, and institutional consequences

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on social construction of technology (SCOT), technology/work/organization studies, critical technology studies (Noble), organizational sociology, and institutional theory. It positions intelligent technologies as requiring integration across these traditionally separate research communities.

## Methods
Conceptual and programmatic paper synthesizing existing literature from SCOT, design studies, technology/work/organization, and critical technology studies. Uses the self-driving truck case as an illustrative example for how unified research teams might coordinate studies across the technology timeline.

## Main Arguments
- Current formulations of AI and work gloss critical issues of variation, power, ideology, and institutions
- Scholars of technology, work, and organization are well-versed in variation but have paid limited attention to power, ideology, and institutions extending beyond single organizations
- Research should extend the technology timeline leftward (examining who funds, commissions, and shapes technology design) and rightward (examining institutional consequences beyond the workplace)
- Unified approaches require interdisciplinary teams including historians, labor economists, sociologists, urban planners, and social workers
- By the time technologies are adopted and implemented, agendas of powerful actors are cemented into features; scholars must engage earlier in the design process
- Studies of ideology should examine how influential actors shape technologies and public expectations through rhetoric and framing
- Technologies employed at work have potential to transform institutions far beyond the workplace (family, transportation, cities)
- Action research using findings to shape technology design prior to implementation could help ensure more equitable outcomes

## Limitations & Critiques
- The unified approach requires unprecedented interdisciplinary coordination and funding
- Building teams spanning current divisions of labor in most universities will be challenging
- The paper is programmatic rather than demonstrating completed unified research
- The self-driving truck example remains illustrative rather than a full implementation of the approach
- Power dynamics within technology companies may limit researcher access
- Timeline between design and institutional consequences spans years; longitudinal research is difficult to fund and sustain
- Engaging with technology design prior to implementation requires new skills for scholars accustomed to post-hoc studies
