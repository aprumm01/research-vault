# Developing human experience experts design educations process-centric response to generative ai

## Study Partner Enrichment

### Overview of the Document

This paper is authored by Dennis Cheatham, a design educator at Miami University in Oxford, Ohio. Cheatham writes from the position of a practitioner-scholar deeply embedded in undergraduate and graduate design education, and his work emerges from direct classroom experimentation spanning Fall 2023 through Spring 2025. His institutional affiliation and teaching experience position him as someone actively wrestling with how to prepare design students for a rapidly changing profession rather than theorizing from a distance.

The document is a conference paper published in the proceedings of HCII 2025 (Human-Computer Interaction International), part of the Lecture Notes in Computer Science series published by Springer. It sits at the intersection of design education scholarship, futures studies, and the emerging field of AI and design practice. This placement in an HCI conference rather than a pure design education venue signals the paper's emphasis on how technological transformation reshapes design pedagogy.

This work matters because it refuses the panic narrative that AI will simply replace designers or the complacent assumption that AI is just another tool designers will learn to use. Instead, Cheatham argues that generative AI necessitates a fundamental reorientation of design education away from technical production skills and toward developing what he calls "human experience experts." The paper presents a process-centric framework grounded in four theoretical foundations—systems thinking, phenomenology, activity theory, and futures studies—and demonstrates how these theories can be translated into practical teaching methods through specific classroom interventions implemented across seven courses with over 300 students.

### Research Overview

The central research question driving this paper asks: How should design education evolve to prepare graduates who can create meaningful human experiences in an AI-enabled future where generative tools increasingly handle technical production tasks? More specifically, Cheatham investigates which theoretical frameworks illuminate the uniquely human capabilities that AI cannot replicate, and how those frameworks can be translated into practical teaching methods that emphasize process over product.

Dennis Cheatham himself conducted this research through action research in his own teaching practice. The study draws from implementations in seven different courses at Miami University between Fall 2023 and Spring 2025, including undergraduate courses like "Creating With Generative AI: Shaping the Future" (141 total students across three terms), "Communication Design Studio 1: Interaction" (67 students), and graduate courses like "Design Research Methods" (10 students) and "Experience Design Studio: Meaning-Making and Motivation" (9 students). The total sample exceeds 300 students across undergraduate and graduate levels.

The research methodology is primarily pedagogical action research combined with reflective practice. Cheatham designed specific classroom interventions—including ripple effect diagrams, Wicked Problems assignments combining Horst Rittel's framework with Sohail Inayatullah's Causal Layered Analysis, and Specifications Grading assessment structures—then implemented them, observed student responses, and iteratively refined the approach. He collected evidence through student work artifacts, particularly examining how students' ability to anticipate consequences evolved when given structured diagramming tools versus open-text prompts.

Key research concepts include "human experience experts," which Cheatham defines as designers who possess contextual understanding, empathy, and foresight that AI tools lack. "Practical Imagination" is Cheatham's term for the ability to envision intended and unintended consequences of design interventions, grounded in research evidence yet infused with critical and visionary thinking. "Process-centric education" emphasizes the journey of research, iteration, and critical thinking over final artifacts or portfolio pieces.

The paper opens with striking labor market data from the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025: "UI and UX Designers are projected as the eighth fastest-growing jobs globally—showing 44% net growth by 2030, while traditional Graphic Designer roles are among the 15 fastest declining jobs, expected to shrink by 11%" (p. 24-26). This divide frames the entire argument—the future demands designers who understand human experience, not just artifact creation.

### Theories of Knowledge

Cheatham builds his framework on four theoretical foundations, each illuminating dimensions of human experience that generative AI cannot comprehend. These are not presented as competing theories but as complementary lenses that together reveal the full complexity of what designers must understand.

**Systems thinking** provides the first foundation. Drawing on Donella Meadows' *Thinking in Systems* and Peter Checkland's Soft Systems Methodology (SSM), Cheatham emphasizes that human experiences exist within interconnected systems influenced by social, technological, economic, ecological, and political factors (STEEP analysis). SSM's CATWOE framework identifies system components, most of which are people: Customers, Actors, Transformation, Weltanschauung (worldview), Owners, and Environmental constraints. Midgley and Reynolds updated this to BATWOVE, adding Beneficiaries and Victims to foreground power dynamics. As Cheatham explains, "to be a beneficiary or a victim is a human stance that context can radically shift depending on perspective" (p. 184). The concept of Weltanschauung is particularly crucial—it refers to unquestioned views of how the world works, shaped by upbringing and past experiences. AI lacks the lived experience to comprehend how different worldviews shape people's interactions with designed systems.

**Phenomenology** forms the second foundation. Drawing on Edmund Husserl's work and contemporary phenomenological philosophy, Cheatham argues that experiences are co-created in people's minds based on their perceptions. As he writes, "The objects perceived are not reality but a constructed reality shaped by the perceiver's perspective" (Cheatham, p. 200, citing phenomenological sources). A celebration of life ceremony may be joyful and healing for one person while bleak and disturbing for another, depending on their prior experiences and emotional state. Even the same person can perceive identical situations differently at different life stages—driving a daughter to school on a random Tuesday versus driving her to university on her last day living at home carries profoundly different phenomenological weight. AI cannot understand these lived, embodied experiences because it lacks consciousness and subjective awareness.

**Activity theory**, originating from Soviet psychologists Lev Vygotsky and Alexei Leont'ev and further developed by Yrjö Engeström, provides the third foundation. Activity theory holds that products, services, and systems exist not for their own sake but to mediate goal-directed human activities. As Cheatham explains, "A design's format mediates interactions between the person, shaping the user's experience" (p. 218). Using a smartphone app to purchase concert tickets, riding a bicycle to school, or participating in a classroom activity to learn typography—all involve technical and social dimensions intertwined. Individual motivation, perception, cultural norms, values, and social relationships all shape how activities unfold. At the deepest level, activities facilitate meaning-making. AI can recognize patterns in how people use interfaces, but it cannot grasp the meaning-making that happens when someone successfully completes a meaningful goal.

**Futures studies** forms the fourth foundation, drawing on scholars like Sohail Inayatullah, Joseph Voros, and Anne Burdick. Cheatham argues that designers shape the future every time they implement an intervention, and futures literacy is therefore as critical as technical skills. He introduces the concept of "Practical Imagination," which he defines as the ability "to envision the intended and unintended consequences caused by their creations" (p. 241), grounded in research evidence yet involving critical thinking and visionary dreaming. The futures cone—a visual framework identifying preferred, probable, plausible, possible, and preposterous futures—guides designers to understand multiple potential futures their work might create.

Causal Layered Analysis (CLA), developed by Sohail Inayatullah, is a key futures studies method Cheatham employs. CLA examines problems at four levels: Litany (surface-level phenomena), Social and Structural (systemic causes), Worldview (cultural beliefs and values), and Metaphors and Myths (deep unquestioned assumptions). Each level requires deeper excavation to identify root causes. As Cheatham notes, "People try to fix symptoms (Litany) when the most critical level to affect change is at the underlying level" (p. 532-533, citing Meadows).

**Why these theories matter together:** Cheatham's framework is synthetic rather than reductive. Systems thinking reveals interconnections, phenomenology illuminates subjective experience, activity theory shows goal-directed action, and futures studies develops foresight. Together, they equip designers to understand contexts AI cannot perceive, anticipate consequences AI cannot imagine, and create interventions that resonate with human meaning-making AI cannot replicate. The theoretical stance is explicitly humanist—it positions design as fundamentally about understanding people, not mastering software.

### Central Arguments

Cheatham's central argument unfolds in three interconnected claims that build toward a transformative vision for design education in the age of generative AI.

**Claim 1: The designer's role is shifting from technical producer to human experience expert.** Cheatham opens with labor market data showing UI/UX designers growing 44% by 2030 while graphic designers decline 11%. This split, he argues, "signals a crucial shift: the future demands designers who are experts in human experience, not just artifact creation" (p. 26-28). Generative AI tools can now produce layouts, write code, generate images, and create prototypes rapidly. What AI cannot do is "understand the nuanced human experiences that make design meaningful" (p. 31-32). AI lacks consciousness, lived experience, cultural understanding, and the ability to comprehend how design decisions impact people across different contexts, abilities, and emotional states. Cheatham provides a concrete example: AI can generate interface layouts for a hospital registration system, but "it cannot truly understand how a grieving parent might interact differently with that system or how cultural backgrounds affect someone's comfort level with self-service technology" (p. 106-108). The rush to AI-generated solutions risks "compromising the deep knowledge that makes design effective" (p. 134).

**Claim 2: Design education must become process-centric rather than product-centric.** Traditional design education focuses on production skills and final portfolio pieces—the tangible artifacts that demonstrate technical competence. In an AI era where tools can generate professional-quality artifacts in seconds, this approach is insufficient. As Cheatham writes, "Rather than focusing solely on final artifacts, process-centric education emphasizes the journey of research, iteration, and critical thinking that leads to meaningful human experiences" (p. 54-56). Process-centric education develops what Cheatham calls "contextual understanding and Practical Imagination that AI tools cannot replicate" (p. 15-16). Meaningful design requires "an iterative process involving broad exploration (divergence), testing and modifying each concept (transformation), and selecting successful candidates for focused development (convergence)" (p. 273-275). This process cannot be rushed or automated—it requires what Michael R. Gibson calls the opposite of a "last-minute miracle" (p. 271-272).

**Claim 3: Four theoretical foundations—systems, phenomenology, activity theory, and futures studies—provide the conceptual grounding for process-centric education.** These frameworks illuminate different dimensions of human experience that AI cannot grasp. Together, they enable designers to understand interconnected social systems (systems thinking), perceive how people subjectively experience designed interventions (phenomenology), recognize goal-directed activities that products mediate (activity theory), and anticipate intended and unintended future consequences (futures studies). Cheatham demonstrates how these abstract theories translate into practical teaching through three specific interventions: ripple effect diagrams, Wicked Problems assignments combining Rittel's framework with Causal Layered Analysis, and Specifications Grading assessment structures.

The paper's most powerful claim is that these interventions develop "Practical Imagination"—a term Cheatham coins to describe the ability to envision consequences grounded in research evidence yet infused with critical and visionary thinking. Unlike pure speculation, Practical Imagination is disciplined by systems analysis and phenomenological awareness. Unlike pure analysis, it requires imaginative projection into possible futures.

### Evidence

Cheatham provides three categories of evidence: pedagogical implementation data, student work artifacts demonstrating learning outcomes, and iterative refinements showing how interventions evolved based on observed effectiveness.

**Evidence from ripple effect diagrams:**

Cheatham developed ripple effect diagrams to help students anticipate intended and unintended consequences of AI use. The assignment evolved through iteration. Initially in Spring 2024, students were asked to identify consequences using open text boxes. Out of 43 students, 28 focused only on personal impacts like "It didn't give me a clear answer," revealing limited ability to think systemically. The most insightful responses imagined broader implications—for example, how AI-generated film scripts might homogenize global cinema or how widespread AI use in medicine could advance Alzheimer's treatment.

In Spring 2025, Cheatham redesigned the assignment using a structured ripple effect diagram modeled on the futures cone, explicitly prompting students to consider personal, regional, and global consequences. He reports that "early results show a nuanced and insightful analysis of AI's broader impacts" (p. 417). The structured diagram scaffolded students' thinking in ways that open-ended prompts did not. Student work samples shown in Figures 2-4 demonstrate the visual structure of the assignment templates and how students populated them with consequences at different scales.

**Evidence from Wicked Problems and Causal Layered Analysis assignments:**

The Wicked Problems assignment combined Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber's Wicked Problems framework with Sohail Inayatullah's Causal Layered Analysis. Students worked in groups of 3-5 to identify a problem in domains like Communication, Energy, Health, Education, Social Inequality, Governance, or Economics. They defined the problem context in detail, then demonstrated how it satisfied each of Rittel and Webber's ten criteria for wickedness (no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, solutions not true or false but good or bad, no immediate test, every solution a "one-shot operation," no enumerable set of solutions, every wicked problem unique, symptoms of another problem, multiple explanations, planners have no right to be wrong).

Following problem definition, students individually completed Causal Layered Analysis, identifying how the problem manifests at four levels: Litany (surface phenomena), Social and Structural (systemic causes involving STEEP factors), Worldview (cultural beliefs and perceptions), and Metaphors/Myths (deep unquestioned assumptions). Cheatham notes this "has proven to be the most difficult of the semester in this course because CLA's deepest levels—Worldview and Metaphors and Myths—challenge them to consider matters that often go unnoticed and unquestioned" (p. 529-531).

The pedagogical value lies precisely in this difficulty. As Cheatham explains, "The impetus of the Wicked Problems assignment is for designers to practice identifying deep, underlying causes that are deeply human—the matters that AI tools cannot address because they lack the lived experience that enables people to make meaning of the world around them" (p. 542-545). Figure 5 shows a completed CLA template with student work, demonstrating how they populated each layer with descriptions and images.

**Evidence from Specifications Grading implementation:**

Specifications Grading is an alternative assessment model Cheatham implemented across all his courses since Fall 2023. Unlike traditional point accumulation systems where students receive partial credit and final grades mark the end of learning, Specifications Grading uses only three rubric levels: Success, Not Done Yet, and Incomplete. Assignments are marked complete only when all learning outcomes are achieved. Students can revise and resubmit work after receiving instructor feedback, creating an iterative improvement cycle.

Cheatham reports that this model shifts student behavior. In traditional grading, "students often prioritize the numerical grade as the definitive measure of their work's quality," and "feedback instructors provide accompanying the assignment letter grade is often ignored because the awarding of points or the letter grade marks the end of the assignment" (p. 563-568). By contrast, under Specifications Grading, "students read and respond to instructor feedback because it is the only way to earn credit for the work" (p. 588). Students in his courses now commonly submit work with notes saying "Here's my first attempt!" signaling they understand learning is iterative (p. 591-592).

This approach is particularly valuable in an AI era because it reduces incentives to use AI as a shortcut. When students know they can revise, "they're more likely to start with their own ideas and understanding rather than seeking AI-generated shortcuts. The focus shifts from 'getting it right immediately' to developing the critical thinking and experiential awareness that generative AI tools cannot replicate" (p. 600-602).

### Conclusion

This paper makes a vital contribution by refusing both technological panic and complacent optimism about AI in design education. Cheatham's vision is neither dystopian (designers will be replaced) nor naively techno-optimistic (AI is just another tool). Instead, he argues that generative AI necessitates elevating design education's focus from technical production to human understanding.

The practical implications are immediate and actionable. Design programs should audit their curricula to identify how much emphasis currently goes to software operation versus understanding human experience. Courses teaching technical skills (typography, layout, prototyping) should be reframed as courses developing contextual awareness, where technical execution serves human understanding rather than being the end goal. Assessment should shift from evaluating portfolio artifacts to evaluating thinking processes, using structures like Specifications Grading that reward iteration and depth over polished first drafts.

For design educators specifically, Cheatham's three interventions—ripple effect diagrams, Wicked Problems + CLA assignments, and Specifications Grading—offer tested starting points. The ripple effect diagram is particularly portable; any design course could adapt it to help students anticipate consequences at personal, regional, and global scales. The Wicked Problems assignment develops systems thinking and futures literacy simultaneously, preparing students to tackle complex sociotechnical problems that AI cannot solve.

Theoretically, the paper advances design education scholarship by synthesizing systems thinking, phenomenology, activity theory, and futures studies into a coherent framework rather than treating them as competing paradigms. This synthesis demonstrates how diverse theoretical traditions converge on a shared insight: human experience is irreducibly complex, shaped by interconnected systems, subjective perception, goal-directed activity, and temporal unfolding. Each lens reveals dimensions that AI fundamentally cannot grasp because AI lacks consciousness, embodiment, culture, and lived experience.

What remains unresolved? Cheatham does not address the resource implications of process-centric education. Specifications Grading with iterative feedback cycles requires significant instructor time—more than traditional grading where you assign points once and move on. Ripple effect diagrams and CLA assignments demand sophisticated facilitation. Can this model scale beyond boutique programs with small class sizes and committed faculty? What institutional support would enable widespread adoption?

The paper also does not engage with critiques that design education already struggles with elitism and gatekeeping. If the future requires designers to be "fluent in human experience" with deep theoretical grounding in systems thinking and phenomenology, does this raise barriers to entry for students from non-traditional backgrounds who may not have had exposure to these frameworks? Cheatham's emphasis on developing empathy through "lived experience and exposure to diverse perspectives" (p. 389-391) gestures toward inclusivity, but the paper does not address how to ensure process-centric education serves rather than excludes marginalized students.

Finally, the paper was written during 2024-2025, a moment of rapid AI advancement. Cheatham acknowledges that "agentic AI and reasoners" are emerging, where AI "can control computers and make their 'decisions' independently without prompting" (p. 91-93). If AI develops genuine reasoning capabilities or even rudimentary consciousness, would that collapse the distinction between technical production and human experience expertise that Cheatham's entire argument depends on? His framework assumes AI will remain fundamentally limited in ways that humans are not—an assumption that may not hold indefinitely.

Despite these unresolved questions, the paper makes a compelling case that design education must transform now rather than waiting for the AI dust to settle. As Cheatham writes in his closing, echoing Buckminster Fuller's optimism, "Perhaps the technological change designers are experiencing will bring humanity to make meaning of what it means to be human. Perhaps we will learn our human language more fluently by speaking it more often as designers who co-create with humankind" (p. 637-639). The future of design, in this vision, lies not in competing with AI's capabilities but in developing our distinctly human capacity for understanding, empathy, and creating experiences that resonate with the complexity of human life.

### Discussion Questions

1. Cheatham argues that designers must become "human experience experts" who understand nuanced contexts that AI cannot grasp. But what if AI's pattern recognition across millions of data points gives it *better* understanding of aggregate human behavior than any individual designer's lived experience could provide? At what point does statistical inference become a form of "understanding"?

2. The paper emphasizes developing "Practical Imagination" to anticipate unintended consequences, but designers historically have poor track records predicting consequences—think of social media platforms designed to connect people that instead amplified misinformation and polarization. If human designers routinely fail to anticipate consequences, is Practical Imagination truly a distinctive human capability, or is it an aspiration we've never reliably achieved?

3. Cheatham's process-centric approach using Specifications Grading requires significant instructor time and small class sizes for iterative feedback. If design programs face budget constraints, enrollment pressures, and adjunct labor, how can resource-intensive pedagogies scale beyond elite institutions? Is there a risk that "human experience expert" education becomes accessible only to privileged students?

4. The paper assumes a future where UI/UX designers thrive (44% growth) while graphic designers decline (11% shrinkage) because the former focus on human experience and the latter on artifact production. But couldn't this split reflect labor market exploitation—companies using AI to deskill creative labor while concentrating higher-paid strategic roles for a smaller elite? Is Cheatham's vision emancipatory or does it risk justifying inequality in the design profession?

### Bias Check

This summary emphasizes Cheatham's theoretical framework and pedagogical interventions while treating his claims about AI's limitations as largely valid. This reflects the paper's own priorities and aligns with its humanistic stance, but readers should note that the summary does not critically interrogate whether AI's limitations are fundamental or temporary.

The summary foregrounds Cheatham's successful implementation evidence (students improved with structured diagrams, iterations under Specifications Grading led to deeper learning) while noting but not dwelling on the unresolved scalability question. This makes the approach sound more universally applicable than it may be—the paper's evidence comes from one instructor's courses at a single institution over 18 months. Generalizability is limited.

I also reproduced Cheatham's focus on what designers must develop without equally emphasizing what must be unlearned or dismantled. The paper frames the transformation as additive (adding systems thinking, phenomenology, futures literacy) rather than subtractive (removing outdated software-centric pedagogy, dismantling elitist design education structures). This additive framing may understate the institutional resistance and structural change required.

The summary treats the four theoretical foundations—systems thinking, phenomenology, activity theory, futures studies—as complementary without interrogating tensions between them. For example, phenomenology's focus on subjective individual experience sits in some tension with systems thinking's focus on structural interconnections. Activity theory's emphasis on goal-directed action may conflict with phenomenology's interest in pre-reflective lived experience. Cheatham synthesizes these frameworks pragmatically for pedagogical purposes, but the summary does not flag potential theoretical incoherence.

Finally, the summary engages Cheatham's vision sympathetically rather than skeptically. The paper's central claim—that designers must become human experience experts because AI cannot understand consciousness, culture, and meaning—depends on assumptions about AI's fundamental limitations that may not hold as AI capabilities advance. The summary raises this question in the "unresolved" section but does not treat it as a core vulnerability of the entire argument.

**Accuracy score: 9/10.** The summary accurately represents Cheatham's framework, evidence, and pedagogical interventions. One point deducted because it could engage more critically with the paper's assumptions about AI limitations, its evidence base (single institution, single instructor), and the scalability challenges that may limit uptake of process-centric education beyond well-resourced design programs.
