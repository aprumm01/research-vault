# Reconciling-ADDIE-and-Agile-instructional-design-models-case-study

## Summary
Using the model of analysis, design, development, implementation and evaluation (ADDIE) with regards to instructional design has become a prevalent practise among e-learning designers and developers This model assumes assigning specific roles to project team members and their linear cooperation However, in case of working in a dynamic and complex environment, the ADDIE model may evolve into more adaptive and people-oriented Agile instructional models.

## Key Concepts
- ADDIE model (Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation) as prevalent instructional design framework
- Agile instructional design as adaptive people-oriented alternative to linear ADDIE processes
- Hybrid approach combining ADDIE structure with Agile flexibility for specific project contexts
- Geographically dispersed project teams requiring modified instructional design workflows
- Dynamic and complex environments necessitating evolution beyond linear cooperation models
- Communication skills and personal qualities as crucial success factors in atypical projects
- Small team size as constraint affecting applicability of traditional instructional design models
- Cultural awareness in specific situational contexts as case study focus

## Theoretical Framework
The case study examines reconciliation between two competing instructional design paradigms. ADDIE represents systematic linear approach with discrete phases and defined role assignments, emphasizing predictability and structured deliverables. Agile models prioritize iterative development, team collaboration, and adaptive response to emerging requirements. The hybrid framework positions these not as mutually exclusive but as complementary depending on project characteristics. When teams are small, geographically dispersed, or operating in volatile contexts, strict ADDIE phases become impediments. The reconciliation involves: (1) maintaining ADDIE's systematic analysis and evaluation rigor, (2) incorporating Agile's iterative cycles within development phase, (3) emphasizing people-oriented communication over role-based handoffs, and (4) adapting process formality to project-specific constraints rather than universal prescriptions.

## Main Arguments
- ADDIE model's linear cooperation assumptions become limiting in dynamic complex environments requiring adaptation
- Hybrid approaches combining ADDIE structure with Agile principles successfully accommodate multinational project constraints
- Small team size and geographic dispersion create conditions where rigid role assignment proves counterproductive
- Communication skills and personal qualities emerge as more critical than process adherence for atypical project success
- E-learning course development for cultural awareness training demonstrates feasibility of reconciling seemingly incompatible models
- Context-specific project characteristics should drive instructional design model selection rather than universal model preference
- Successful multinational projects require flexibility to evolve design processes based on situational demands

## Limitations & Critiques
- Single case study limits generalizability of findings about when hybrid approaches prove superior to pure models
- Limited documentation of specific decision points where team chose ADDIE versus Agile practices
- Does not provide clear criteria for other practitioners to assess when hybrid approach warranted versus pure model
- Unclear whether communication and personal quality emphasis reflects hybrid model benefits or simply good practice
- Case study focuses on e-learning development which may not transfer to other instructional design contexts
- No comparison with pure ADDIE or pure Agile implementations on similar projects to isolate hybrid approach benefits
- Qualitative analysis without quantitative metrics on efficiency, quality, or stakeholder satisfaction outcomes
- May conflate successful project management with instructional design model effectiveness
