# Instructional Design and Creativity: A Response to the Criticized

## Summary
Rowland (1995) responds to Dick's defense of Instructional Systems Design (ISD) against critics who argue ISD produces uncreative instruction. Rather than defending creativity as a criterion that should "dominate" (Dick's framing), Rowland argues the debate reveals deeper disagreements about the nature of instructional design itself: whether it is a technical process with predictable outputs or a reflective, creative practice more akin to design than engineering. Rowland challenges the assumption that following high-quality procedures reliably produces high-quality outcomes.

## Key Concepts
- Instructional Systems Design (ISD) creativity debate
- Design vs. engineering models of ID practice
- Reflective practitioner vs. systematic designer
- Creativity as integral to the ID process
- Situated cognition and constructivism in ID

## Theoretical Framework
Draws on design theory, constructivism, and Schön's reflective practice; contrasts the "algorithmic" model of ID (follow the process, get the outcome) with a "designerly" model that sees creative judgment as irreducible; sympathetic to Situated Cognition/Constructivist perspectives that Dick was responding to.

## Methods
Response paper; close analysis of Dick's (1995) arguments; counterargument development through examination of the underlying assumptions about process-outcome relationships in instructional design.

## Main Arguments
- Dick's claim that "ISD methods do result in creative instruction when used appropriately" contains a hidden assumption: that "appropriate use" is specifiable — but what counts as appropriate requires the very creativity being defended
- The debate is not about whether creativity is good but about whether the design of instruction is fundamentally a technical (engineering) or reflective (design) activity
- Process-outcome relationships in design are not predictable in the way Dick assumes — "high-quality procedures" do not guarantee outcomes because design is inherently situated, contextual, and indeterminate
- ISD's strength (systematic process) is also its limitation: it provides structure but can suppress the judgment-based adaptation that characterizes expert design practice

## Limitations
- Written in 1995; the ID field has evolved significantly, with many practitioners now embracing more agile, design-thinking-oriented approaches that partially resolve this debate
- Response format limits the scope of Rowland's own constructive alternative
