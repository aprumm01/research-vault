# Case and Piñeiro 2009 - Stop whining, start doing! Identity conflict in project managed software environments

## Summary
In this article we explore the relationship between software developers and IT project managers as expressed through narrative exchanges in an on-line discussion forum We interrogate a naturalistic data set to show how the conflict between IT professionals and their immediate managers (project managers) is manifest through the identity work that they engage in To this end, the article draws attention to strategies of resistance and dissent expressed in the narratives of software developers, contrasting these with the performative expectations espoused by project managers.

## Key Concepts
- **Identity work**: The processes through which individuals construct, maintain, and express their professional identities, particularly in contexts of organizational conflict and power dynamics
- **Performative strategies (Austinian sense)**: Speech acts and communicative practices that preserve programmers' status, professional identity, and organizational power in response to project management disciplines
- **Aesthetics of programming**: The artistic, creative, and craft dimensions of coding that form a central part of programmers' professional identity, contrasting with project management's performative aesthetics
- **Identity conflict**: The manifestation of worker-management tension through competing identity constructions, expressed through narrative exchanges between software developers and project managers
- **Knowledge asymmetry**: The structural anomaly where project managers often lack both the educational credentials and technical knowledge of the programmers they manage, creating status ambiguities
- **Symbiotic relationship**: The interdependence between IT programming and project management—programmers create software that facilitates PM work, while PM methodology is integral to software development

## Theoretical Framework
The paper integrates multiple theoretical perspectives:

1. **Identity theory (Alvesson & Willmott, 2004; Alvesson, 2004)**: Examines knowledge worker identity construction and co-construction of identity conflict, focusing on how professional identities are negotiated in organizational contexts

2. **Structuralist and post-structural power theory**: Interprets conflict both through structuralist accounts (ontologically grounded class division, opposed worker-management interests) and post-structural approaches (discursive power dynamics, resistance strategies)

3. **Speech act theory (Austin)**: Analyzes how programmers use performative language strategies to maintain status and power despite formal hierarchical subordination to project managers

4. **Labour process theory**: Situates the analysis within broader debates about worker-management conflict, control, and resistance in capitalist employment relationships (Burawoy, 1979; Thompson and McHugh, 1995)

The framework emphasizes how the particular nature of programmer-project manager relationships (knowledge differences, symbiotic dependence) demands more nuanced interpretation than purely structuralist accounts provide.

## Methods
Naturalistic data analysis of online discussion forum threads from Slashdot (www.slashdot.org) over five years. Focus on threads concerning "aesthetics of coding" and "project management for programmers" that reveal programmer-project manager relationships. Participants mostly programmers or IT professionals, posting either as registered members or anonymously ("Anonymous Coward"). Several hundred participants represented across analyzed threads. Narrative analysis examining identity work, resistance strategies, and performative expressions. Non-participatory approach—researchers did not engage in or influence online exchanges.

## Main Arguments
- **Identity conflict manifests through narrative resistance**: Software developers express dissent and resistance to project management through online narratives, using performative strategies to preserve professional identity and organizational power despite formal subordination in corporate hierarchy

- **Knowledge asymmetry creates status inversion**: Project managers often lack both the educational credentials and technical knowledge of programmers they manage—an anomaly not present in other employment contexts. This creates programmers' view of themselves as superior in organizational status despite formal hierarchy

- **Aesthetics as identity marker**: Programming aesthetics (craft, creativity, artistry of code) play a central role in programmers' identity expression, contrasting with and conflicting against project managers' different performative aesthetics focused on efficiency, control, and deliverables

- **Symbiotic interdependence complicates power dynamics**: The relationship is more complex than traditional worker-management conflict because programmers create software that facilitates PM work, while PM methodology is essential to software development. This mutual dependence shapes identity negotiations

- **Grassroots critique of project management**: The online forum data provides critique of project management stemming from lived experience of those involved in its co-construction, revealing tensions between technical professional values and managerial control systems

- **Dual theoretical interpretation necessary**: The narrative data supports both structuralist interpretation (opposed interests based on socio-economic division) and post-structural reading (discursive power dynamics), suggesting neither approach alone captures the full complexity

## Limitations & Critiques
- **Demographic uncertainty**: Anonymity of bulletin board forums makes it difficult to be precise about community demographics, participant backgrounds, or whether same individuals post under multiple identities. Cannot verify if participants are actually practicing programmers or project managers

- **Self-selection bias**: Forum participants who choose to engage in these discussions may not represent typical programmer-project manager relationships, potentially overrepresenting those with grievances or strong opinions

- **Limited generalizability**: Data from single online community (Slashdot) may reflect specific subcultural norms and discursive practices not representative of broader IT industry or other organizational contexts

- **Temporal and contextual constraints**: Five-year observation period captured specific historical moment in IT industry and project management practices; rapid technological and methodological changes may limit contemporary relevance

- **One-sided perspective**: Analysis primarily focuses on programmers' narratives and identity work; project managers' perspectives and identity construction less fully explored, creating potentially skewed understanding of relationship

- **Missing organizational context**: Online forum discussions lack rich organizational context—specific company cultures, project types, team structures, or material conditions that shape actual workplace relationships

- **Methodological limitations of naturalistic data**: Cannot probe deeper meanings, ask follow-up questions, or verify interpretations with participants. Narrative analysis relies entirely on what participants chose to post publicly

- **Power dynamics oversimplification**: While noting complexity, the analysis may still oversimplify power relationships by focusing primarily on knowledge/status dimensions while underexploring other forms of power (formal authority, resource control, career advancement)
