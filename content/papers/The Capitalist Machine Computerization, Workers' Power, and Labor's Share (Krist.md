# The Capitalist Machine: Computerization, Workers' Power, and Labor's Share (Kristal, 2013)

**Year:** 2013
**Venue:** chine: Computerization, Workers' Power, and the Decline in P

## Summary
Analyzes the 6% decline in labor's share of national income across 43 U.S. private industries from 1969 to 2007, arguing that the primary driver was the erosion of workers' positional power through class-biased technological change (computerization) that favored employers over most employees. Error correction models with fixed-effect estimators test the independent effects of computerization and power indicators (unionization, capital concentration, unemployment, import penetration).

## Key Concepts
- Labor's share of national income
- Computerization as class-biased technological change
- Workers' positional power
- Unionization and labor market power
- Income inequality between capital and labor

## Theoretical Framework
Power resource theory and stratification sociology; classes compete over income shares based on their positional power; challenges purely economic (productivity-based) explanations of labor's share decline.

## Methods
Quantitative panel data analysis; error correction models with fixed-effect estimators; data from 43 nonagricultural private industries and 451 manufacturing industries; 1969–2007 time period; multiple data sources combined.

## Main Arguments
- Labor's share declined 6% overall, with large declines in construction, manufacturing, and transportation, and small increases in finance and services
- Erosion of workers' positional power (declining unionization, rising capital concentration) is the primary driver of the decline
- Computerization had class-biased effects, enhancing employer power more than it boosted worker productivity or wages
- The capitalist machine metaphor: computers functioned as instruments of class power, not merely neutral productivity tools

## Limitations & Critiques
US-specific findings; time period (1969–2007) predates cloud computing, platform economy, and gig work; error correction models cannot fully disentangle computerization from concurrent structural changes; positional power variables are imperfect proxies for the underlying mechanisms.
