# Beyond Tools: Generative AI as Epistemic Infrastructure in Education

## Summary
This theoretical paper argues that analyzing generative AI as epistemic infrastructure rather than as neutral tools reveals distinctive challenges for human knowledge practices that instrumental framings obscure. Drawing on theories of technological mediation and distributed cognition, Chen advances a framework with three analytical conditions: affordances for skilled epistemic actions, support for epistemic sensitivity, and implications for habit formation. Application to AI lesson planning and feedback tools reveals a pattern of "epistemic substitution"—AI performing the cognitive operations through which professional teaching expertise develops—potentially preventing the cultivation of professional judgment.

## Key Concepts
- **Epistemic infrastructure**: The frameworks and systems through which knowledge is created, circulates, and can be validated; repositioning AI from a tool to a constitutive element of knowledge practices
- **Epistemic agency**: The capacity to actively engage in and control one's own belief formation and validation processes, rather than passively accepting information
- **Epistemic substitution**: When AI performs the cognitive operations that both reflect and develop professional expertise—distinct from cognitive offloading because the delegated operations are precisely those through which expertise develops
- **Technological mediation**: Technologies are not neutral instruments but actively shape how humans perceive the world and act within it, drawing on Ihde's (1990) phenomenological analysis
- **Epistemic sensitivity**: The capacity to recognize what counts as evidence, identify relevant considerations, and perceive ethically and epistemically salient features of educational situations
- **Habit formation**: The epistemic virtues and dispositions cultivated through repeated practice that constitute professional judgment; AI substitution risks cultivating habits of epistemic delegation rather than independent judgment
- **Composite intentionality**: Verbeek's concept describing how AI combines human and technological intentionality, blurring user-tool boundaries and reshaping what goals appear reasonable

## Theoretical Framework
The paper integrates three theoretical traditions. From philosophy of technology, it draws on Ihde's (1990) phenomenological analysis of human-technology relations (embodiment, hermeneutic, alterity, background relations) and Verbeek's (2008) concept of technological mediation. From cognitive science, it draws on Hutchins' (1991) distributed cognition and Heersmink and Knight's (2018) analysis of extended cognitive systems. From epistemology, it draws on scholarship on epistemic agency (Ahlstrom-Vij, 2013), epistemic infrastructure (Coeckelbergh, 2022; Marin, 2022), and virtue epistemology (Elgin, 2013).

The central theoretical contribution is showing how these traditions converge on a distinctive account of AI's risks: unlike previous technologies that mediated access to information or structured presentation, AI systems perform operations that were previously internal to human epistemic processes. This represents a qualitative shift in technological mediation that requires new analytical frameworks beyond instrumentalism.

## Methods
Theoretical/philosophical analysis. The paper applies the developed framework to analyze two AI systems widely deployed in educational settings: an AI lesson planning tool and an AI student feedback tool. The analysis examines how these systems mediate teaching practices where educators have traditionally exercised epistemic agency, drawing on examples, system documentation, and published analyses of these tools. This is a conceptual paper published in a peer-reviewed venue rather than an empirical study.

## Main Arguments
- AI systems in education must be understood as epistemic infrastructures that reconfigure the conditions for knowledge practice, not merely as tools that augment individual capability—this infrastructural framing reveals risks that instrumental analyses miss
- Current AI educational tools create conditions for epistemic substitution: they perform the cognitive operations through which teaching expertise develops (evaluating student work, forming pedagogical judgments, synthesizing evidence about learning) without supporting educators in exercising these operations themselves
- The three-condition framework (skilled epistemic actions, epistemic sensitivity, habit formation) reveals how AI lesson planners and feedback tools inadequately support all three dimensions: they produce outputs without sustaining the skilled actions, insufficiently foster contextual sensitivity, and risk cultivating habits of epistemic delegation
- Epistemic substitution is categorically different from cognitive offloading: it is problematic specifically because the delegated operations are those whose exercise constitutes both expertise development and the authority claims of the profession
- The distinction between AI as "background infrastructure" (shaping conditions while occupying background position) and "alterity relations" (AI as quasi-other) helps explain why AI risks are easily overlooked—infrastructural embedding escapes the critical scrutiny applied to visible tools
- Design principles for AI systems that sustain rather than supplant epistemic agency include: keeping humans in the epistemic loop, designing for transparency of AI reasoning, supporting rather than bypassing the skilled actions that develop judgment, and building in epistemic friction rather than optimizing for frictionless efficiency

## Limitations & Critiques
As a theoretical paper, the analysis is limited by the conceptual examples chosen and may not generalize to all AI educational tools. The framework's three conditions are analytically useful but their operationalization for empirical research requires further development. The paper focuses primarily on teacher-facing AI tools; student-facing tools may raise different epistemic agency considerations.

The paper's critique of AI tools as epistemic substitution may be read as implying a blanket skepticism toward AI in education that the author likely does not intend. The framework could be developed further to identify conditions under which AI genuinely supports rather than substitutes epistemic agency, rather than primarily mapping the risks of current implementations.
