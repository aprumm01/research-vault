# Motivation, Volition, and Performance

## Summary
This book chapter provides an overview of principles of motivation and volition for instructional designers and human performance technologists. The authors distinguish between motivation (the desire to pursue a goal) and volition (the self-regulatory strategies used to maintain effort and overcome obstacles during learning). Using a narrative example of a graduate student Ashley facing motivational challenges in a blended learning course, the chapter illustrates how initial motivation can wane when competing demands arise, requiring volitional strategies to persist.

The chapter synthesizes six key principles of motivation derived from Keller's ARCS model and volitional research: (1) motivation is promoted when learner curiosity is aroused through perceived knowledge gaps; (2) when content is meaningfully related to learner goals; (3) when learners believe they can succeed; (4) when learners anticipate satisfying outcomes; (5) when learners employ volitional self-regulatory strategies; and (6) when instructional designers apply systematic motivational design processes. The authors emphasize that instructional designers must understand both motivational and volitional concepts to create effective learning environments, particularly in distance and blended settings where learner self-regulation is critical.

The chapter concludes by highlighting trends in motivational research relevant to instructional technology, including growing interest in web-based and distance learning contexts where motivation and volition challenges are particularly acute.

## Key Concepts
- **Motivation**: A person's desire to pursue a goal, manifested by choice of goals and effort (persistence plus vigor) in pursuing them; an internal construct embedded in personal experience and expectations
- **Volition**: Self-regulatory strategies used to protect intentions and maintain effort when facing obstacles, distractions, or competing goals during goal pursuit
- **ARCS Model**: Keller's motivational design model comprising four categories: Attention (arousing curiosity), Relevance (connecting to goals), Confidence (building success expectancy), and Satisfaction (managing outcomes)
- **Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivation**: Intrinsic motivation involves engaging in tasks for inherent pleasure; extrinsic motivation involves engaging for external rewards
- **Learned Helplessness**: A condition where people believe they cannot succeed regardless of effort, often resulting from repeated uncontrollable failures

## Theoretical Framework
The chapter integrates multiple theoretical traditions: expectancy-value theory, attribution theory, self-efficacy theory, locus of control, and self-regulation research. Keller's ARCS model provides the primary organizing framework, synthesizing motivational constructs into actionable design categories. The authors extend traditional motivational models by incorporating volitional theory, recognizing that generating initial motivation differs from sustaining effort through completion.

## Methods
This is a theoretical/conceptual chapter that synthesizes existing literature rather than presenting original empirical research. The authors draw on decades of motivational research and Keller's extensive work developing and validating the ARCS model across diverse instructional contexts.

## Main Arguments
- Instructional designers must be competent in motivational design to create effective learning experiences, not just cognitively sound instruction
- Motivation and volition, while both goal-directed, require different types of support and intervention
- Motivation involves generating the desire to engage; volition involves protecting that intention against competing demands
- Holistic motivational design approaches (like ARCS and Wlodkowski's time-continuum model) are more effective than focusing on single motivational factors
- Blended and distance learning contexts present particular motivational challenges requiring explicit design attention
- Systematic motivational design processes can predictably influence learner motivation and persistence

## Limitations & Critiques
The chapter acknowledges that motivation remains an internal psychological construct that cannot be directly observed, only inferred from behavior. The relationship between motivation and actual performance is complex and mediated by many factors. While the ARCS model provides a systematic design process, determining optimal motivational tactics for specific situations still requires designer judgment. Research on integrating volitional strategies into instructional design is still emerging, and the precise mechanisms linking motivational interventions to learning outcomes require further investigation.
