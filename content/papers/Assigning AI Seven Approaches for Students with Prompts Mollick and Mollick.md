# Assigning AI: Seven Approaches for Students with Prompts

## Summary
This practitioner-oriented paper from Wharton's Ethan and Lilach Mollick proposes a seven-role framework for assigning AI use to students: AI-tutor, AI-coach, AI-mentor, AI-teammate, AI-tool, AI-simulator, and AI-student. Each role carries distinct pedagogical benefits and associated risks, supported by worked example prompts. The framework's overarching aim is to help educators intentionally leverage AI for learning while keeping students as the "human in the loop" — actively overseeing, challenging, and complementing AI outputs rather than passively delegating to them.

## Key Concepts
- **Seven AI Roles**: AI-tutor (personalised instruction), AI-coach (metacognitive reflection), AI-mentor (ongoing feedback), AI-teammate (collaborative intelligence), AI-student (teaching-by-explaining), AI-simulator (deliberate practice), AI-tool (task extension)
- **Human in the Loop**: The normative principle that students must maintain oversight, responsibility, and critical engagement with AI outputs — not outsourcing their thinking but using AI as a scaffold for higher-quality work
- **Complacency Risk**: The tendency for students to accept AI outputs uncritically; addressed in each role's guidelines by designing prompts that require active evaluation and comparison
- **Confabulation (Hallucination)**: LLMs produce plausible but erroneous outputs; the framework addresses this risk by instructing students to verify facts against reliable sources
- **Active Oversight**: Students are positioned not as passive recipients of AI outputs but as evaluators who must interrogate, refine, and supplement AI content with their own insights
- **Dual Purpose of AI Assignments**: Each approach is designed both to help students learn course content (with AI) and to help them develop AI literacy (about AI) — treating AI fluency as a learning outcome itself
- **Pedagogical Risk-Benefit Mapping**: The paper explicitly maps benefits and risks for each role, enabling instructors to make informed decisions about task design and student guidance

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on established pedagogical theories for each role. The tutor role invokes direct instruction research showing personalised instruction significantly outperforms large-group teaching. The coach role builds on metacognition and self-regulated learning literature, particularly the value of reflection and pre-mortem analysis. The mentor role draws on feedback research showing that frequent, specific feedback improves outcomes even when not all advice is taken. The teammate role invokes research on collaborative intelligence and devil's advocate techniques for reducing groupthink. The student role is grounded in the learning-by-teaching effect (protégé effect), where explaining knowledge to others deepens understanding. The simulator role invokes deliberate practice theory (Ericsson), positioning AI as a scalable sparring partner for low-stakes practice.

## Methods
This is a practitioner design paper, not an empirical study. The methodology involves applying the authors' prior work on AI in teaching and assignment generation to the specific challenge of assigning AI use to students. For each role, the authors: (1) outline the pedagogical rationale, (2) provide an example prompt with sample output, (3) list risks and guidelines for instructors, and (4) provide instructions for students. The paper does not report data collection or statistical analysis; its contribution is a structured design framework supported by theoretical reasoning and illustrative examples.

## Main Arguments
- There are at least seven meaningfully distinct ways to assign AI to students, each with unique learning mechanisms and risk profiles; treating AI as a monolithic "tool" obscures these differences
- The tutor role's risk is the AI's uneven knowledge base and confabulation tendency, which requires students to verify factual claims independently rather than treating AI as an authoritative source
- The coach role is particularly valuable for increasing metacognition through structured reflection (pre-mortem analysis, performance reflection), but risks giving advice in a tone mismatched to the student
- The student role, where the AI adopts the role of a learner and the student must teach it, leverages the protégé effect — forcing students to organise, explain, and defend their knowledge
- All seven roles share a common risk: students may develop complacency and accept AI outputs as "good enough," producing work that is technically adequate but personally under-engaged
- The framework explicitly rejects both extremes — banning AI and uncritically embracing it — positioning educators as designers of AI-integrated experiences that preserve active student cognition

## Limitations & Critiques
The paper is a design framework without empirical validation; claims about pedagogical effectiveness rest on the underlying theories rather than studies of the roles themselves in AI contexts. The prompt examples, while illustrative, may not transfer well across disciplines, student populations, or LLM versions. The framework assumes instructors have sufficient AI literacy to adapt the approaches, which may not hold in many institutions. Ethical considerations around academic integrity — while acknowledged — are not systematically developed; the paper focuses primarily on pedagogical risk rather than broader institutional or equity concerns. The roles are treated as independent, but in practice students may shift between them fluidly.
