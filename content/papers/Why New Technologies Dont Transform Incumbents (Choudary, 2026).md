# Why New Technologies Don't Transform Incumbents

## Summary
This Harvard Business Review practitioner essay argues that large, established companies (incumbents) consistently fail to benefit from new technologies because they mistake tool adoption for transformation. Choudary contends that incumbents layer new technologies on top of existing workflows, producing efficiency gains but not competitive advantage. The real opportunity lies in restructuring the unit of work and the coordination architecture around it.

The article uses two primary case examples: Adobe vs. Figma and traditional fashion vs. Shein. Adobe successfully transitioned to cloud subscription but preserved a file-centric architecture assuming sequential, individual editing. Figma, by contrast, made design elements (not files) the basic unit of work, enabling simultaneous collaboration without version conflicts. Design governance shifted from review processes to shared component libraries. Similarly, traditional fashion organizes around seasonal collections; Shein collapsed the unit of work to individual testable product concepts, enabling AI-driven trend detection, design generation, and small-batch production to run simultaneously.

Choudary introduces two key variables defining transformation potential: the unit of work (what the organization treats as the basic object of production) and the coordination mechanism (whether work moves through sequential approvals or continuous synchronization). Incumbents typically shift only tooling while keeping coordination structure intact; challengers shift both simultaneously.

## Key Concepts
- **Unit of Work**: The basic object of production that an organization treats as the thing being made, managed, and handed off
- **Coordination Mechanism**: The pattern by which work moves between people and teams (sequential approvals vs. continuous synchronization)
- **Architectural Self-Preservation**: Resistance to restructuring that leaders mislabel as cultural inertia; actually reflects protection of existing influence
- **Tool Adoption vs. Transformation**: Distinction between adding AI to existing workflows (efficiency) vs. redesigning work structure (transformation)

## Theoretical Framework
Though not explicitly named, the argument draws on coordination theory (Malone and Crowston), organizational architecture (Nadler and Tushman), and sociotechnical systems theory (Trist and Bamforth). The analysis connects to HPT-style gap analysis, diagnosing symptoms at the wrong level.

## Methods
Practitioner-oriented comparative case analysis. The evidence is entirely case-based, using Adobe/Figma and traditional fashion/Shein comparisons to illustrate rather than test the argument. This is consistent with HBR genre conventions written to persuade executives rather than build cumulative research evidence.

## Main Arguments
- Incumbents fail because they apply new technologies to old structures rather than recognizing that new technologies enable new ways of coordinating work
- The diagnostic frame matters: executives blame execution gaps, training lag, or middle management resistance when the real cause is structural
- AI's real opportunity is decomposing work into smaller pieces, recombining them in new ways, and coordinating across them continuously rather than sequentially
- Competitive advantage comes from restructuring that the tool enables, not from the tool itself
- Challengers move diagonally: they redefine both the unit of work and the coordination mechanism simultaneously
- Changing the unit of work redistributes influence inside organizations; those who manage approvals have real reasons to resist
- Resistance to restructuring is political and structural, not merely cultural

## Limitations & Critiques
- Both cases are technology-adjacent companies with modular, measurable outputs; applicability to services, education, or knowledge work is unclear
- No systematic evidence: no interviews with leaders, no longitudinal data, no comparative sample
- The causal story is plausible but not tested; Choudary is advancing a framework, not reporting findings
- Does not address situations where incumbents have successfully restructured work
- Does not explain how organizations identify the right unit of work to redesign or navigate power dynamics during restructuring
- HBR genre aims to persuade rather than build cumulative evidence; this is a practitioner essay, not peer-reviewed research
