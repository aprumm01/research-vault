# Automating Teacher Work: A History of the Politics

## Summary
Historicizes debates on automation in education by analyzing Swedish policy and archival materials from 1957 to the present, showing how automation promises for teachers' work follow a recurring "techno-solutionist" pattern. Argues that automation does not reduce teacher labor but rearranges and intensifies it, and that global platform infrastructures have shifted the scale and political stakes of this dynamic.

## Key Concepts
- Techno-solutionism in education
- Platform infrastructures and digital labor
- Policy assemblage
- Teacher work and automation
- Critical AI studies

## Theoretical Framework
Draws on Deleuze's theory of machines and societal control, Winner (1980) on artifacts and politics, and post-digital critical frameworks; frames policy as a dynamic "assemblage" that naturalizes and stabilizes techno-solutionist assumptions over time.

## Methods
Historical-comparative analysis of Swedish digital and archival policy materials spanning 1957 to present; policy assemblage analysis; document analysis across several decades.

## Main Arguments
- Automation debates in education repeat a familiar techno-solutionist pattern in which promises of labor-saving technology serve to individualize teacher responsibility rather than reduce workload
- Automated systems do not eliminate teacher work; they rearrange, intensify, and make it center on the machine's requirements
- Scale and impact of automation is shifting with global platform infrastructures, making critical debate increasingly difficult to sustain
- The critical question is how to preserve space for debate about what automation in education means politically

## Limitations & Critiques
Primarily Sweden-focused with a specific social democratic labor history; draws on policy documents rather than teachers' own accounts; the Swedish labour movement context may not generalize to other national settings or systems without strong union traditions.
