# Integrating Futures Imaginaries, Expectations and Anticipatory Practices: Practitioners of Artificial Intelligence Between Now and Future (Hautala & Ahlqvist, 2024)

## Summary
Johanna Hautala (University of Vaasa) and Toni Ahlqvist (University of Turku) present an empirical study of how AI practitioners translate state-scale AI futures imaginaries into grassroots expectations and anticipatory practices, based on 26 interviews with practitioners in Finland and Singapore and observations at 20 AI events (all in 2019). They propose a new integrative conceptual framework combining futures imaginaries (state strategies), expectations (practitioners' individual interpretations), and anticipatory practices (how practitioners respond to expectations in "the now"). Key finding: AI practitioners operate in an "anticipatory zone" where far-future imaginaries, near-term expectations, and present practices co-constitute each other through an oscillating feedback process. Published in Technology Analysis & Strategic Management 36(9):2100-2112, 2024 (online 2022).

## Key Concepts
- Futures imaginaries: state-scale sociotechnical visions (here, national AI strategies in Finland and Singapore) that structure practitioners' expectations
- Expectations: practitioners' individual interpretations of imaginaries -- specific beliefs about AI development translated into present action
- Anticipatory practices: practitioners' work-based responses to expectations in "the now" (riding the wave, proactive/critical development, educating customers)
- Anticipatory agency: the strategic ability to construct future-oriented goals and implement them; emerges in the "anticipatory zone"
- Anticipatory zone: the zone of intersection between far-future imaginaries, expectations, and present practices where the future is co-constituted
- "AI as the new electricity": the dominant national imaginary across both Finnish and Singaporean strategies, framing AI as universal infrastructure

## Theoretical Framework
Sociotechnical imaginaries (Jasanoff & Kim 2009); sociology of expectations (Borup et al. 2006; van Lente); anticipatory practices (Anderson 2007, 2010); multilevel perspective on sociotechnical transitions (Geels & Schot 2007); social practice theories (Sovacool & Hess 2019); futures semiosis (Ahlqvist & Rhisiart 2015); Technology Analysis & Strategic Management 36(9), 2024.

## Methods
Empirical case study conducted in 2019: semi-structured interviews with 26 AI practitioners (Finland n=14: 12 from companies, 2 from universities; Singapore n=12: 6 from companies, 6 from universities); participant observation at 20 AI-related events (Turku.AI meetups in Finland; SGInnovate, AI Singapore, Innovfest Unbound in Singapore); directed qualitative content analysis of transcripts in three iterative rounds; field notes (64 pages); analysis of national AI strategy documents as representations of futures imaginaries.

## Main Arguments
- AI practitioners do not passively receive top-down state imaginaries but actively interpret and translate them into expectations, and then into anticipatory practices -- the grassroots and strategic levels co-constitute each other in an ongoing oscillating process
- Three anticipatory practices emerged: (1) "riding the wave" of AI hype (cynically branding projects as AI to attract funding), (2) proactively and critically developing AI (taking up practical challenges), (3) educating customers and the public about AI misunderstandings
- A key tension: many practitioners simultaneously ride the AI hype and critically educate against it -- the same practitioner may brand their project as AI in PowerPoint while privately dismissing the "AI" label as marketing
- Finnish practitioners expressed more critical skepticism toward AI hype than Singaporean practitioners; Singapore practitioners more focused on practical AI development and race-to-adopt framing; this reflects differences in national imaginary emphasis

## Limitations & Critiques
Only Finland and Singapore studied (both technologically advanced, highly educated populations -- not representative of non-Western or less-resourced AI development contexts); convenience sample from AI practitioner networks (Turku.AI, SGInnovate) biases toward already-engaged practitioners; mostly male sample (women very difficult to find in this practitioner community); data collected in 2019 before the current generative AI wave -- significant anticipatory lag since then; quantitative Delphi complement recommended by authors but not attempted.
