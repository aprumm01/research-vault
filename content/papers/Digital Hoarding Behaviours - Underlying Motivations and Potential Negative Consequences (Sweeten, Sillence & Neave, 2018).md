# Digital Hoarding Behaviours: Underlying Motivations and Potential Negative Consequences

## Summary

Sweeten, Sillence, and Neave (2018) present one of the first empirical studies to systematically investigate digital hoarding—defined as the excessive accumulation of digital materials (emails, photographs, files, software) accompanied by difficulty deleting them. Published in *Computers in Human Behavior*, this qualitative study surveyed 46 UK participants using open-ended questions derived from the Hoarding Rating Scale. The research identifies five barriers to digital data deletion and four categories of negative consequences, establishing digital hoarding as a distinct behavioral phenomenon that parallels but differs from physical hoarding disorder. The study is notable for revealing that unlimited storage capacity, particularly in workplace settings, removes natural constraints on accumulation and enables hoarding behaviors—a finding with significant implications for sustainable computing.

## Key Concepts

### Digital Hoarding
Defined as "the accumulation of digital files to the point of loss of perspective, which eventually results in stress and disorganisation" (van Bennekom et al., 2015). Distinguished from simple data accumulation by the presence of difficulty deleting and potential negative consequences.

### Personal Information Management (PIM)
The ways in which individuals collect, store, organize, and retrieve digital items. Research indicates users typically keep half of emails received and rarely engage in proactive management or "clean-up" of stored information.

### Barriers to Deletion
Five identified barriers:
1. **Keeping data for future/"just in case"** - Fear that data may have future value
2. **Keeping data as evidence** - Data retained as proof of work or communication
3. **Laziness/time constraints** - Deletion seen as unrewarding, low-priority task
4. **Emotional attachment** - Particularly strong for photos and personal communications
5. **"Not my server, not my problem"** - Unique to workplace contexts with unlimited cloud storage

### Negative Consequences
Four categories identified:
1. Effects on productivity (digital clutter impairs searching, concentration)
2. Effects on psychological wellbeing (stress, anxiety, feeling overwhelmed)
3. Cybersecurity vulnerabilities (accumulated sensitive data increases breach risk)
4. Links with physical hoarding (behavioral crossover between domains)

## Key Findings

### Barriers to Deletion

**Just in Case Mentality**: The most common barrier—participants feared data might be needed in the future:
> "I struggle really hard deleting folders. I always fear that what they contain will be very important to me in the future. Even though I do not use them, I still do not wish to delete them just in case." (P4)

**Data as Evidence**: Particularly in workplace contexts, data retained for accountability:
> "Proof I have passed a message on / booked a job in / expressed concern / chased something up with a supplier etc." (P27)

**Time Constraints**: Deletion viewed as unrewarding relative to other priorities:
> "I'm just too lazy to delete them. Life is busy and I feel that I have better things to do than to spend my life going through my backlog of e-mails and sorting them out." (P9)

**Emotional Attachment**: Strong feelings, especially regarding photos:
> "Photos are special, I wouldn't want to get rid of them. It would be difficult because I would feel that I'm deleting little bits of me and my past." (P45)

**Storage Externalization**: Unlimited workplace storage removes personal incentive for curation:
> "I'm not bothered about my inbox. Did not think there would be 9201 emails in my deleted though. I do not see data as a physical thing and we have unlimited data at work so I don't really care." (P23)

### Negative Consequences

**Productivity Impacts**:
> "When something is too cluttered it makes it difficult to be efficient. I waste too much time looking for things when they are out of order." (P37)

**Psychological Distress**:
> "Stressful. Although not actually taking up physical space, it 'feels' clutter like." (P42)

**Cybersecurity Concerns**:
> "If a hacker hacked into my laptop, they could find all my pictures, all my music taste and most importantly all my interactions via email... This could then lead to a potential digital impersonation of me." (P4)

**Physical Hoarding Crossover**:
> "I seem to hoard a lot outside of the virtual world also, perhaps there's a crossover... I knew I hoard physical items and assumed I'd hoard some aspects of digital items but not to this amount." (P7)

## Relevance to Sustainable Computing

This paper provides foundational psychological insights for sustainable computing research:

1. **Understanding the Human Factor**: Digital hoarding behaviors directly contribute to data storage growth and associated environmental costs (energy consumption, carbon emissions, e-waste). Understanding the psychological drivers—particularly emotional attachment and the "just in case" mentality—is essential for designing effective interventions.

2. **The Storage Paradox**: The finding that unlimited storage enables rather than solves data accumulation challenges the assumption that cheaper, larger storage is inherently beneficial. This "tragedy of the commons" dynamic where individuals don't perceive storage costs suggests that making environmental costs visible could promote sustainable behaviors.

3. **Intervention Design**: The five barriers framework provides targets for sustainable design interventions:
   - Automated retention policies to address "just in case" hoarding
   - Alternative evidence systems to reduce data-as-evidence accumulation
   - Friction reduction in deletion processes to address time constraints
   - Sensitive handling of emotionally-valued content
   - Individual-level feedback on storage costs to counter "not my server" attitudes

4. **Organizational Policies**: The workplace-specific "not my server, not my problem" barrier suggests that organizational data governance policies are crucial for sustainable data management, as individual motivation alone is insufficient when storage appears unlimited and free.

5. **[[Data Minimization]]**: The paper supports data minimization principles by documenting the negative consequences of excessive accumulation, providing behavioral evidence for technical and policy approaches to sustainable data storage.
