# Computer-Mediated Intersensory Learning Model for Students with Learning Disabilities

## Overview of the Document

The authors of this theoretical model are Soonhwa Seok, Boaventura Dacosta, Carolyn Kinsell, John C. Poggio, and Edward L. Meyen, representing a multidisciplinary team with expertise spanning curriculum and instruction, instructional systems design, human performance, educational psychology, and special education. Soonhwa Seok, the lead author, holds an MA and PhD in curriculum and instruction with research interests in educational communication and technology, particularly for English language learners and special education. At the time of publication, she was conducting postdoctoral research examining intersensory learning models, assistive technology, and motivation for students with learning disabilities. Boaventura DaCosta brings a computer science background (BS) combined with instructional systems design credentials (MA, PhD) and 15 years of experience in commercial and government training as a software engineer. His research focuses on cognitive psychology, information and communication technology innovations, and game-based learning.

Carolyn Kinsell contributes 18 years of experience spanning analysis, virtual environment development, human performance standards, and research on training applications, with extensive work supporting military training systems and commercial clients. John C. Poggio serves as co-director of the independent Center for Educational Testing and Evaluation and as a professor of educational psychology and research at the University of Kansas, with deep involvement in Kansas statewide testing systems for K-12 students including those with special needs. Edward Meyen, a Budig Professor of Special Education and co-director of the e-learning Design Lab at the University of Kansas, focuses on developing e-learning tools for K-12 and postsecondary educators, particularly tools that blend assessment with standards-aligned instruction.

This document is a theoretical article published in TechTrends (Volume 54, Number 2, March/April 2010), proposing a conceptual framework rather than reporting empirical findings. The paper sits at the intersection of special education, educational technology, cognitive neuroscience, and instructional design. It matters because it addresses a persistent challenge in inclusive education: how to design computer-mediated instruction that leverages what neuroscience reveals about hemispheric brain functions and intersensory learning to support students with learning disabilities who are mandated by the 2004 Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) to be educated in general education classrooms and held to the same mastery standards as their peers. The work attempts to bridge brain-based research with practical instructional technology design, offering a theoretical foundation for software and curriculum development that could help close performance gaps for students with LDs in the digital age.

## Research Overview

The central research question is theoretical and design-oriented: How can computer-mediated instruction be designed to stimulate intersensory abilities across both brain hemispheres in ways that address the specific information processing deficits experienced by students with learning disabilities in inclusive classrooms? The authors seek to develop a theoretical model integrating cognitive neuroscience findings about hemispheric brain functions with instructional technology principles and learning strategy research to propose an alternative to traditional approaches that they argue often fail to leverage students' compensatory strengths.

This work represents theoretical model development rather than empirical research. The five authors collaboratively synthesized existing literature from cognitive neuroscience, learning theory, instructional technology, and special education to construct their proposed framework. No original data collection occurred; instead, the authors integrated findings from multiple research traditions to build a conceptual model for how computer-based learning environments should be designed.

The methodology involves identifying key theoretical constructs from different fields and showing how they converge to support the intersensory learning model. The authors draw on information processing models that describe how humans take in, manipulate, and store information through sensory registers, working memory, and long-term memory. They incorporate brain-based research on hemispheric functions, particularly work documenting that "both the right and left hemispheres work together, in a complementary fashion, to process language mutually and in parallel" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64), contrary to the myth that only the left hemisphere controls language-related functions.

Key research concepts require unpacking. Learning disabilities are defined as "disorders in learning and information processing related to verbal, written, and behavioral language usages, such as reading, writing, speaking, listening, and numeracy" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64), viewed as "disorders of psychological processing in the central nervous system" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64). The authors emphasize that students with LDs commonly experience "deficits in aural and visual processing of language" and "limitations in phonological processing" along with "deficiencies in information processing" affecting "working memory and long-term memory, the organization of information, the auditory or visual perception of language, meta-cognition, and attention" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64).

Intersensory learning refers to educational approaches that intentionally engage multiple sensory modalities simultaneously across both brain hemispheres. The authors explain that "intersensory holistic activities can be enhanced" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65) because sensory systems are neurologically interconnected: "the visual cortex is located in both hemispheres of the brain and closely influences the auditory, motor, and senses in both hemispheres because they are connected" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65). Computer-mediated learning in this model means using computers as "mindtools," defined as "computer application[s] that can be used to engage learners in critical thinking about the subjects they are studying" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 63), with interface designs that deliberately stimulate visual, auditory, motor, and tactile senses to activate compensatory strengths.

Hemispheric complementarity describes how the right and left brain hemispheres contribute different but mutually supportive functions to language processing. For speech perception, "the right hemisphere tends to make a distinction in favor of the low frequencies, while the left hemisphere is more in charge of the higher frequencies of vocal sounds" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64-65). For decoding written text, "the right hemisphere provides considerably more stored memory and recall of visual representation" like font and case, while "the left hemisphere is more involved in abstract representation" like word and letter recognition (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65).

## Theories of Knowledge

The primary theoretical framework organizing this work is information processing theory, which models human cognition as analogous to computer processing with distinct stages. The authors explain that "information processing models are composed of the sensory registers (sensory memory), immediate memory (short-term or working memory), and the long-term memory" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64), citing Anderson's 1983 architecture of cognition and other foundational work. This framework assumes that learning occurs through the transformation and movement of information through these processing stages, and that learning disabilities represent breakdowns or inefficiencies in these information-handling processes.

A second major theoretical structure derives from neuroscience research on hemispheric brain functions and hemispheric complementarity. The authors challenge what they call "a myth that only the left hemisphere controls language-related information" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64), citing Banich and Nicholas (1998) and Bates and Roe (2001) to establish that "both the right and left hemispheres work together, in a complementary fashion, to process language mutually and in parallel" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64). This framework draws on split-brain research pioneered by Sperry, Gazzaniga, and Bogen (1969) and extensive subsequent work mapping specific language functions to different hemispheric regions. The key insight is that hemispheres are "complementary to each other" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64) rather than redundant, with each contributing specialized processing capabilities.

Specifically addressing reading disabilities, the authors invoke Bakker's balance model (1990), which "explains that the alteration hemispheres function from right to left as reading skills advance" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65). This neuropsychological model proposes that "the right hemisphere perceives information in the reading processes at the beginning of reading. When reading involves advanced syntax and semantics, the area of control shifts from the right hemisphere to the left" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65). The model suggests that readers with disabilities may experience problems when this hemispheric shift fails to occur appropriately, remaining reliant on right-hemisphere processing when left-hemisphere linguistic processing should dominate.

A third theoretical thread comes from Gardner's multiple intelligences theory (2006), which the authors use to support their claim that learning strengths in one area can compensate for deficits in another. They cite Rose and Meyer's (2002) adaptation of this work, arguing that "students have various multifaceted learning abilities and potentials and that if they have deficits in a specific area(s), they will be compensated for by the strength in others" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 66). Gardner's framework identifies nine distinct intelligences (musical, bodily-kinesthetic, linguistic, spatial, logical-mathematical, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalist, and existentialist), each with different neurological substrates. The theory's implication for instructional design is that "a student's learning gap may be closed by employing learning strategies that resonate with the student's strengths" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 66).

A fourth theoretical structure comes from Rose and Meyer's (2002) Universal Design for Learning framework, which identifies three brain networks with distinct functions: recognition networks (in the back of the hemispheres, recognizing auditory, visual, and tactile senses), strategic networks (at the front, controlling cognitive processes and motor movement), and affective networks (centered between and dispersed across hemispheres, managing attention and motivation). The authors map traditional learning processes onto these networks, arguing that "perception and cognition are tasks related to the recognition networks. Information processing and meta-cognition are the tasks of the strategic network, whereas attention and motivation are the tasks belonging to the affective networks" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 66).

Finally, the model draws on constructivist learning theory through its invocation of Vygotsky (1978), who "postulated the following three prerequisites for learning: (a) recognition of knowledge of upcoming learning, (b) application of methods to acquire the knowledge, and (c) engagement with task" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 66-67). This positions learning as an active construction process requiring learner agency and engagement rather than passive reception.

## Central Arguments

The central argument the authors advance is that traditional instructional approaches for students with learning disabilities are inadequate because they fail to leverage what neuroscience reveals about hemispheric complementarity and intersensory brain networks. The authors contend that while students with LDs experience "deficits in phonological processing, working memory, and information processing speed" (Seok et al., 2010, p. abstract), these deficits are often specific to certain brain regions or processing pathways, meaning other areas retain or even develop compensatory strengths. As they state, "if we can facilitate the development of both hemispheres, students with LDs can learn better and can close the performance gaps they typically experience in the inclusive classroom" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65-66).

A second key claim is that computer technology uniquely enables the kind of multisensory, hemisphere-activating instruction that can leverage these compensatory strengths. The authors argue that "computers as mindtools play an important role in instructional design and the development of programs to facilitate students' multi-sensory latent abilities in the digital age" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 67). Unlike traditional instruction which may primarily engage linguistic processing through text and lecture, computers can simultaneously present information through "visual, auditory or tactile modes" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 67), allowing "students with LDs to interact and engage in a task at their preferred pace by using specialized user interfaces" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 67).

The third major argument challenges the left-hemisphere-centric view of language and reading instruction. The authors emphasize that research shows "both the right and left hemispheres work together, in a complementary fashion, to process language mutually and in parallel" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64) with "mutual language processes includ[ing] speech perception, decoding print, understanding word meanings, understanding sentences and discourse, and analyzing syntax" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64). This means that instructional designs should deliberately engage both hemispheres through complementary functions: the right hemisphere's processing of "low frequencies," "visual representation," and "distant meanings, such as jokes, inferences, and metaphors," and the left hemisphere's processing of "higher frequencies," "abstract representation," and "closely connected meanings" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64-65).

A fourth claim addresses the intersensory nature of brain organization. The authors argue that "intersensory holistic activities can be enhanced" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65) because "the visual cortex is located in both hemispheres of the brain and closely influences the auditory, motor, and senses in both hemispheres because they are connected" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65-66). This neurological interconnection means that "auditory-visual skills can be developed in a complementary manner" and "can be better developed by memory and attention skills" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65-66). The implication for instruction is that engaging multiple senses simultaneously can create synergistic effects rather than simply additive ones.

Fifth, the authors argue that effective computer-mediated learning must incorporate research-validated learning strategies developed over 30 years by the Center for Research on Learning. They emphasize that "no single learning strategy or computer program is a panacea for those with LDs" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 67), but that software incorporating strategies that "address the way students learn, lead the students to solve problems, and enable the students to apply their learning in a real-life situation" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 67) can significantly improve outcomes.

Finally, the authors argue that computers reduce cognitive load while providing multisensory learning opportunities. They explain that computers can "present information in ways that reduce cognitive load" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 67) while simultaneously teaching "meta-cognitive skills and how to apply what they have studied to their learning" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 67). The model positions the computer not as a replacement for teacher instruction but as a mediator that handles routine processing tasks, freeing cognitive resources for higher-order learning.

## Evidence

The evidence supporting these arguments comes entirely from synthesis of existing research literature rather than original empirical findings. For claims about information processing deficits in students with LDs, the authors cite foundational work documenting that these students experience "deficiencies in information processing" affecting "working memory and long-term memory, the organization of information, the auditory or visual perception of language, meta-cognition, and attention" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64), drawing on Catts (1993), Galaburda and Livingstone (1993), and Smith et al. (2004) among others.

For the complementary functions of brain hemispheres, the evidence synthesis draws heavily on Beeman and Chiarello's (1998) edited volume on right hemisphere language comprehension. The authors cite multiple studies showing differential hemispheric processing: for phonetics, "the right hemisphere tends to make a distinction in favor of the low frequencies, while the left hemisphere is more in charge of the higher frequencies of vocal sounds" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 64-65), supported by work from Molfese (1980) and Ross (1981). For text decoding, evidence comes from Baynes and Eliassen (1998) and Chiarello (1988) documenting that right hemisphere processing provides "considerably more stored memory and recall of visual representation" while left hemisphere processing handles "abstract representation" including "word and letter recognition" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65).

Support for Bakker's balance model comes from empirical work by Donders and van der Vlugt (1984), who "studied the eye movement patterns of students with reading problems while they were engaged in reading, numerical, and perceptual assignments. Results showed that older readers with reading problems used the left hemisphere" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65), suggesting inappropriate hemispheric reliance. Lambert et al. (1999) provided additional support, showing that "children with normal reading skills used the right hemisphere where visual-spatial intelligence is located" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65) during early reading development.

For intersensory brain network claims, the authors cite Birch and Belmont (1964) and Birch and Lefford (1963) demonstrating that "intersensory holistic activities can be enhanced" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 65), along with Glickstein (2000) and Macaluso (2006) documenting the neurological connections between visual, auditory, motor, and tactile cortices. Evidence that "auditory-visual skills can be developed in a complementary manner" and "can be better developed by memory and attention skills" comes from Senf (1969) and Senf and Feshbach (1970).

For effectiveness of computer-mediated instruction generally, the authors cite meta-analyses and research reviews by Kulik (1983), Kulik, Banger, and Williams (1983), and Kulik and Kulik (1986, 1987) showing that "using computers as mindtools for learning enhances the academic achievement of students at all levels" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 63) and that "computer-based learning has been shown to have positive effects on academic disposition to specific subjects, learning and teaching" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 63).

Specific evidence for technology supporting students with LDs comes from multiple sources. Hasselbring and Williams (2000) and Hudson, Lignugaris-Kraft, and Miller (1993) documented that "when computer-adapted instruction and educational software are used for the best practice of instructional development, students' learning outcomes are more gainful and productive" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 69). Lancaster, Schumaker, and Deshler (2007) "developed and validated an interactive hypermedia version of the self-advocacy strategy for students with LDs by using video and audio segments, tests, and graphics" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 63). Glazer and Cury (1998) and Montgomery and Marks (2006) found that "computer-integrated strategic instruction has been found to improve the writing skills of students with disabilities" (Seok et al., 2010, p. 67).

The authors provide detailed descriptions of specific software examples claimed to embody their principles: Wiggleworks (Scholastic, 1996) for reading in divided linguistic units with text-to-speech and customizable visual interfaces; textHELP Read & Write Gold (1999) for writing with self-questioning and error monitoring strategies; Sunburst Building Perspective Deluxe (1997) for collaborative geometric problem-solving. However, the paper does not present original evaluation data for these programs' effectiveness, instead relying on their design features aligning with the theoretical model.

The study's limitations stem from its theoretical nature. First, as a conceptual model rather than empirical research, the framework's effectiveness remains undemonstrated. The authors synthesize neuroscience findings, learning theory, and technology capabilities to propose what should work, but provide no direct evidence that implementing this model produces better outcomes for students with LDs compared to other approaches.

Second, the neuroscience evidence cited comes primarily from basic research on typical populations or clinical populations with specific brain injuries (split-brain patients, stroke patients), not necessarily from studies of students identified with learning disabilities in educational settings. The translation from laboratory findings about hemispheric functions to classroom applications for students with LDs requires inferential leaps that may not hold in practice.

Third, the software examples provided are described in terms of their design features (text-to-speech, customizable interfaces, segmented text presentation) but the paper doesn't present controlled studies demonstrating that these specific programs are more effective than alternatives for students with LDs, or that their effectiveness derives specifically from the intersensory, bi-hemispheric activation mechanisms proposed by the model rather than from other factors like increased practice time, motivation, or individualized pacing.

Fourth, the model assumes that activating both hemispheres and multiple sensory modalities is inherently beneficial, but doesn't address potential cognitive load concerns. For students already experiencing information processing difficulties, simultaneous presentation through visual, auditory, and motor channels might overwhelm rather than support learning in some contexts. The conditions under which multisensory presentation helps versus hinders remain unspecified.

Fifth, the paper was written in 2010, and both the neuroscience understanding of reading disabilities and the capabilities of educational technology have evolved substantially since. The software examples mentioned (Wiggleworks, textHELP Read & Write Gold) represent 1990s-2000s technology; contemporary tools incorporating speech recognition, natural language processing, and adaptive learning algorithms weren't available or considered. Finally, the model focuses almost exclusively on cognitive and neurological factors, giving limited attention to motivational, social, emotional, or contextual factors that also profoundly affect learning outcomes for students with disabilities in inclusive classrooms.

## Conclusion

This research contributes a theoretical framework that attempts to bridge cognitive neuroscience, learning theory, and instructional technology to address a persistent challenge in special education: designing effective instruction for students with learning disabilities in inclusive classrooms. The core contribution lies in synthesizing evidence that both brain hemispheres contribute complementarily to language processing and that intersensory brain networks are neurologically connected, then translating these findings into design principles for computer-mediated instruction. By arguing that technology can simultaneously engage visual, auditory, motor, and tactile senses across both hemispheres while reducing cognitive load and incorporating research-validated learning strategies, the model offers a conceptual foundation for software and curriculum development that moves beyond one-size-fits-all approaches.

The model's emphasis on leveraging compensatory strengths rather than simply remediating deficits represents an important shift in perspective. If students with LDs have specific processing deficits (phonological processing, working memory, attention) but retain or develop strengths in other areas (spatial processing, visual memory, alternative sensory pathways), then instructional design should deliberately activate these strengths to support learning rather than focusing solely on drilling weak areas. This connects to broader movements in special education toward Universal Design for Learning and strength-based approaches.

The framework's integration of multiple theoretical perspectives—information processing models, hemispheric brain functions, multiple intelligences, UDL brain networks, learning strategies research—creates a rich conceptual foundation that acknowledges the multifaceted nature of learning and learning disabilities. By showing how these different theoretical lenses converge on similar instructional recommendations (multisensory engagement, strategic learning tools, customizable interfaces, reduced cognitive load), the authors strengthen the warrant for their design principles.

However, the gap between this theoretical model and demonstrated effectiveness remains substantial. The paper essentially argues "neuroscience shows X about how brains work, learning theory shows Y about effective instruction, and technology offers capabilities Z; therefore, combining X + Y + Z should produce better outcomes for students with LDs." This logical structure is reasonable but unproven. Many plausible-sounding instructional theories fail when implemented because the complexity of real classrooms, the heterogeneity of students, and the particulars of how theories get operationalized introduce factors not captured in the idealized model.

What remains open from this work is extensive. First, empirical validation is entirely absent. Does computer-mediated instruction designed according to these intersensory, bi-hemispheric principles actually produce better outcomes (achievement, engagement, skill development) for students with LDs compared to other instructional approaches? Under what conditions, for which subtypes of learning disabilities, and for which learning goals?

Second, the mechanisms proposed require validation. The model assumes that software features that engage multiple senses simultaneously activate both brain hemispheres in complementary ways that leverage intact processing pathways to compensate for impaired ones. But does Wiggleworks reading software with text-to-speech and customizable fonts actually produce the hypothesized bilateral hemispheric activation? Do students with phonological processing deficits compensate by recruiting right-hemisphere visual processing, and if so, does this compensation lead to durable learning or just temporary performance support?

Third, the model treats "learning disabilities" as a relatively unified category, but educational and neuropsychological research increasingly recognizes substantial heterogeneity. Students with dyslexia, dyscalculia, dysgraphia, ADHD with learning impacts, language processing disorders, and nonverbal learning disabilities may have quite different cognitive profiles and benefit from different instructional approaches. Does the intersensory learning model work equally well across these diverse profiles, or are there specific LDs for which it's particularly appropriate or inappropriate?

Fourth, the relative importance of different model components remains unspecified. Is the key factor the multisensory presentation, the bilateral hemispheric engagement, the incorporation of learning strategies, the cognitive load reduction, the customizable interfaces, or the immediate feedback? If resources or design constraints force trade-offs, which elements are most essential?

Fifth, the role of explicit instruction versus discovery learning within this framework needs elaboration. The model emphasizes engagement, problem-solving, and meta-cognitive strategies, suggesting some constructivist influence, but students with LDs often benefit from explicit, systematic instruction. How does the model balance these potentially competing priorities?

For a PhD student returning to this paper six months from now, the key takeaway is this: theoretical models that synthesize neuroscience, learning theory, and technology capabilities can generate productive design principles for educational technology, but the distance from "this should work based on what we know about brains and learning" to "this does work as demonstrated in classroom implementation" is substantial. The value of this paper lies not in validated solutions but in a conceptual framework that organizes thinking about how to design for students with LDs in ways informed by brain research. Whether that framework actually yields better outcomes than alternatives remains an open empirical question twenty-plus years after publication.

## Discussion Questions

1. The model synthesizes neuroscience findings from studies of typical populations, split-brain patients, and stroke victims to make design recommendations for students with learning disabilities in classrooms. What are the conceptual and methodological challenges in translating basic neuroscience research findings into educational practice? Are there fundamental differences between laboratory-identified brain functions and educationally-relevant learning processes that this translation might miss?

2. The authors argue that computer-mediated instruction can simultaneously engage visual, auditory, motor, and tactile senses to activate both brain hemispheres and leverage intersensory connections. But students with LDs often experience working memory limitations and processing speed deficits. Under what conditions might multisensory, multi-hemispheric stimulation support learning versus overwhelming limited cognitive resources? How would you design studies to identify those boundary conditions?

3. The paper was published in 2010, describing software from the 1990s-2000s (Wiggleworks, textHELP Read & Write Gold). Contemporary educational technology incorporates speech recognition, natural language processing, adaptive learning algorithms, and generative AI that weren't available then. How might these newer capabilities extend, challenge, or transform the intersensory learning model? Are the underlying principles technology-agnostic, or do specific technological affordances matter?

4. The model emphasizes leveraging compensatory strengths (using visual processing to support phonological deficits, for instance), drawing on multiple intelligences theory and UDL frameworks. This contrasts with remediation approaches that focus on directly training weak areas through intensive practice. What does research in cognitive psychology and learning sciences tell us about when compensation versus remediation strategies are more effective? Are there developmental considerations—times when building foundational skills matters more than bypassing them through accommodation?
