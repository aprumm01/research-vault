# Generative AI in Design Thinking Pedagogy: Enhancing Creativity, Critical Thinking, and Ethical Reasoning in Higher Education

## Summary
This study investigates how GenAI integration within Design Thinking pedagogy transforms undergraduate students' creativity, critical thinking, and ethical reasoning across a 12-week course. Using thematic and sentiment analysis of 112 student reflections, the authors identify four key themes: Perceived Benefits (enhanced creativity and accessibility), Ethical Concerns (bias and authorship ambiguity), Hesitance and Acceptance (evolution from skepticism to strategic adoption), and Critical Validation (development of epistemic vigilance). The paper argues that pedagogically-scaffolded GenAI augments rather than replaces human judgment, and that students evolve from passive users to critical evaluators—a transformation requiring constructivist, reflective learning design.

## Key Concepts
- **Epistemic Vigilance**: An emerging critical competency where students learn to question, verify, and critically evaluate GenAI outputs—developing awareness of hallucinations, bias, and the limits of AI knowledge
- **Pedagogical Scaffolding with GenAI**: The structured pedagogical design that positions GenAI as a co-creator in learning rather than a productivity tool—requiring reflective, team-based activities rather than individual output generation
- **Data Colonialism**: The concern that AI training data reflects dominant (often Eurocentric) knowledge systems and aesthetics, systematically marginalizing Indigenous and non-Western ways of knowing—particularly relevant to DALL-E's image generation defaults
- **Passive User to Critical Evaluator**: The developmental trajectory observed—students initially accept AI outputs uncritically but through structured scaffolding develop capacity to evaluate, question, and refine
- **Algorithmic Opacity**: The condition where students cannot access or understand AI decision-making processes, making bias detection difficult and potentially fostering uncritical acceptance of authoritative-seeming outputs
- **Automation Bias in Design**: The tendency for students to offload creative and analytical judgment to AI, especially in low-confidence situations or when tasks are perceived as outside one's professional expertise
- **Constructivist GenAI Integration**: The theoretical position that positions GenAI as compatible with constructivist learning theory when properly scaffolded—students remain active knowledge constructors rather than passive recipients of AI-generated content

## Theoretical Framework
The paper constructs a theoretical framework combining design thinking's five stages (empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test) with constructivist learning theory—drawing on Vygotsky's social development theory, Kolb's experiential learning model, and Dewey's pragmatist view of education as reflective action. This combination is positioned as a response to the limitations of traditional cognitive taxonomies (Bloom's) for GenAI contexts, where critical thinking no longer means analysis and synthesis alone but now includes AI content verification, task stewardship, and bias detection.

The paper also integrates critical pedagogy (Burbules and Berk) to argue that critical thinking with AI should involve ideological critique and action-oriented learning, not only skill acquisition—addressing the socio-political forces embedded in educational technologies and challenging students to interrogate AI's cultural and epistemological assumptions.

## Methods
Mixed-methods analysis of 112 student reflections from a 12-week course embedding GenAI tools (ChatGPT and DALL-E) across Design Thinking's five stages at the University of Doha for Science and Technology, Qatar. Thematic analysis identified four key themes in the qualitative data. Sentiment analysis classified responses as positive/negative/neutral, revealing that 86% of responses were overall positive while ethical concerns generated significant negative sentiment (62%). The mixed-methods design integrates the breadth of sentiment analysis with the depth of thematic analysis to capture both cognitive and emotional dimensions of student GenAI experience.

## Main Arguments
- Pedagogically-scaffolded GenAI augments rather than replaces human judgment—students who evolved from passive users to critical evaluators developed strategies for bias detection, source validation, and AI output refinement that represent genuine cognitive development
- The development of epistemic vigilance (the critical scrutiny of AI outputs) represents a new form of critical thinking that challenges traditional cognitive taxonomies—it involves not just evaluating arguments but interrogating the socio-political and epistemological assumptions embedded in AI systems
- Ethical concerns (bias, authorship ambiguity, data colonialism) generated the most emotionally charged responses, suggesting students have strong intuitions about AI's limitations that educators can mobilize for deeper critical engagement
- The evolution from skepticism to strategic adoption was not uniform—some students maintained resistance throughout, others became over-reliant, and a minority achieved the target critical-evaluative stance—indicating that individual factors and scaffolding quality both matter
- GenAI's impact on design thinking is phase-specific: most valuable for ideation and prototyping (accelerating divergent generation and rapid visualization), most ethically fraught in empathy and definition phases (where AI-generated personas risk bias and misrepresentation)
- The paper calls for reimagining AI literacy as a multidimensional competence encompassing creativity, ethics, and critical reasoning—not simply technical proficiency—and for faculty development that equips educators to design GenAI-integrated learning environments that promote agency

## Limitations & Critiques
The study is conducted at a single institution in Qatar, which may have specific cultural and educational dynamics (international student body, particular attitudes toward AI and academic integrity) that limit generalizability. Thematic and sentiment analysis of student reflections are subject to social desirability bias—students may write what they believe instructors want to hear rather than authentic experiences. The 12-week timeframe may be insufficient to distinguish genuine epistemic development from temporary course-context behavior.

The paper's engagement with data colonialism and Eurocentric AI aesthetics, while important, is somewhat underdeveloped—it raises the critique but does not provide concrete pedagogical interventions for addressing it. The call to "reimagine AI literacy" is aspirational but the specific curricular mechanisms remain under-specified.
