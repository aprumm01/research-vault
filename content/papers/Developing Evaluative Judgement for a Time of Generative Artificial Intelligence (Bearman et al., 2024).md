# Developing Evaluative Judgement for a Time of Generative Artificial Intelligence

## Summary
This conceptual paper, published in Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education (2024), argues that evaluative judgement — the capability to judge the quality of work of self and others — is both urgently needed in a GenAI world and uniquely positioned to be developed through engagement with GenAI. The authors propose three intersecting foci: (1) developing evaluative judgement of GenAI outputs, (2) developing evaluative judgement of GenAI processes, and (3) GenAI assessment of student evaluative judgements. They argue this represents a distinctively human capability that must be cultivated, not ceded, as GenAI proliferates.

## Key Concepts
- **Evaluative Judgement**: The capability to judge the quality of work of self and others; a contextualised, multicriterion capability that combines an internal conception of quality with holistic disciplinary standards
- **Humans as Arbiters of Quality**: The normative claim that regardless of how sophisticated GenAI outputs become, human judgment remains the ultimate standard; GenAI can produce outputs but cannot authoritatively evaluate them against contextually situated quality standards
- **Three Foci Framework**: (1) Judging GenAI outputs (accuracy, appropriateness), (2) Judging GenAI processes (when and how to use AI, what to delegate), (3) GenAI as an assessor of student evaluative judgment (AI used as a tool to develop, not replace, human judgment)
- **Sustainable Assessment**: Boud's (2000) concept that assessment should prepare students to engage in self-directed evaluation throughout their lives — reinterpreted here as requiring the capacity to judge AI outputs independently
- **Hallucinations as Quality Challenge**: GenAI "confident responses that are faithful in appearance but non-sensical in light of common knowledge" require evaluative judgment to detect — making this capability especially critical
- **Analytic vs. Holistic Judgment**: Novice evaluators rely on explicit criteria (rubrics); expert evaluators develop tacit, holistic judgment — GenAI integration must support this developmental progression
- **Evaluativist Epistemology**: Kuhn et al.'s (2000) framework in which knowing requires people to make alignments between internal perspectives and external evidence — providing the epistemological grounding for evaluative judgment

## Theoretical Framework
The paper builds its conceptual foundation on Tai et al.'s (2018) formulation of evaluative judgement: a person must hold an internal understanding of quality and make contextualised judgements about specific work. This is distinguished from generalised critical thinking by its specific focus on complex appraisal of texts, artefacts, or performances against disciplinary standards. The paper draws on Sadler's (1989) account of multicriterion holistic judgment — noting that expert appraisal is tacit and integrative, not reducible to stepwise checklist application.

Boud's (2000) sustainable assessment framework provides the normative motivation: education should develop graduates who can independently evaluate the quality of work they encounter and produce across their lives. In a GenAI world, this extends directly to the outputs of AI systems. The paper also draws on Bearman and Luckin's (2020) argument that evaluative judgement is a uniquely human capability, contrasting it with cognitive tasks that AI can perform, framing it as a graduate attribute worth preserving.

## Methods
Conceptual paper. The authors synthesise assessment theory, emerging empirical data on student GenAI use, and educational technology literature to develop a structured argument. No primary data are collected. The paper draws on early survey data on student GenAI use patterns (e.g., 56% of US students using AI for assignments by late 2023, per Nam 2023) to establish the urgency of the problem, then develops its framework through theoretical reasoning and illustrative examples.

## Main Arguments
- Evaluative judgement is urgently needed in a GenAI world precisely because AI produces hallucinations with confident presentation, requiring students to have developed internal quality standards to detect errors
- The relationship between evaluative judgement and GenAI is bidirectional: not only must students judge AI outputs, but AI can also be used as a pedagogical tool to develop and exercise evaluative judgment through formative feedback strategies
- Existing formative assessment strategies (self-assessment, peer review, criteria clarification, exemplar analysis) naturally develop evaluative judgement and can be repurposed to interrupt uncritical AI use
- Educators and institutions have a collective responsibility not to relinquish their roles as arbiters of quality; allowing students to outsource this judgment to AI permanently undermines a foundational educational goal
- The three-foci framework provides concrete pedagogical direction: instructors should design activities targeting output judgment, process judgment, and using AI itself as a development tool for human evaluative capacity
- The paper argues that GenAI actually creates new opportunities for developing evaluative judgement — by generating a larger volume of varied outputs for students to appraise, it provides richer practice material than traditional assessment alone could supply

## Limitations & Critiques
As a conceptual paper, it does not provide empirical evidence that the proposed three foci are effective in practice, and the framework requires empirical validation across disciplines and student populations. The paper focuses on higher education and may not translate directly to other learning contexts. The reliance on early (2023) student use data means the landscape has already shifted considerably since publication. The paper's emphasis on evaluative judgement as a uniquely human capability may underestimate the extent to which AI systems are already developing quality assessment capabilities in specific domains.
