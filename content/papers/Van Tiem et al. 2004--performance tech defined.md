# Van Tiem et al. 2004--performance tech defined

## Summary
Van Tiem, Moseley, and Dessinger (2004) define and elaborate Human Performance Technology (HPT) as a systematic approach to improving human performance through analysis, cause analysis, intervention selection, design, development, implementation, and evaluation. Drawing on the International Society for Performance Improvement's HPT model, the chapter distinguishes HPT from training by emphasizing that performance gaps have multiple causes—not only lack of knowledge and skill—and require a range of interventions spanning job design, incentives, feedback systems, tools, and resources. The authors present HPT as a field grounded in behaviorism, cognitive science, instructional systems design, and organizational development.

## Key Concepts
- **HPT (Human Performance Technology)**: Systematic approach to closing performance gaps through analysis, intervention, and evaluation
- **Performance analysis**: Determining the gap between actual and desired performance and its causes
- **Cause analysis**: Identifying whether performance gaps stem from environmental (information, resources, incentives) or individual (knowledge, capacity, motivation) factors
- **Intervention selection**: Matching solutions to causes; training is only one of many possible interventions
- **ISPI HPT Model**: The International Society for Performance Improvement's framework guiding HPT practice

## Theoretical Framework
Integrates behavioral psychology (Skinner, Gilbert), cognitive science, instructional systems design, and organizational development into a comprehensive performance improvement framework. HPT is positioned as the application of scientific principles to human performance problems in organizational contexts.

## Methods
Conceptual and definitional chapter presenting the ISPI HPT model and its theoretical foundations; practitioner-oriented with case illustrations.

## Main Arguments
1. Most performance gaps are not training problems; HPT practitioners must conduct rigorous cause analysis before selecting interventions
2. Environmental factors (feedback, tools, incentives, resources) often account for more variance in performance gaps than individual knowledge or skill deficits
3. HPT requires a systematic process—from analysis through evaluation—rather than jumping directly to intervention design
4. HPT's multi-disciplinary foundations (behavioral, cognitive, organizational) enable it to address complex, systemic performance challenges

## Limitations
The HPT model's comprehensiveness can make it difficult to apply; practitioners without strong analytical skills may default to training regardless of formal process. The organizational consulting context in which HPT typically operates may introduce political pressures that override analysis-driven intervention selection.
