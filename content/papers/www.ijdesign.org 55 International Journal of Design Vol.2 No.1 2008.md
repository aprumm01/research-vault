# The Nature of Design Practice and Implications for Interaction Design Research (Nelson & Stolterman, IJOD 2008)

## Summary
Nelson and Stolterman's influential paper argues that design practice is characterized by intentional creation of the "ultimate particular" — a unique artifact situated in a specific context that has never existed before and addresses a situation that may never recur. They contrast design with science (which seeks general laws) and art (which expresses subjective vision), positioning design as a third mode of human inquiry. The paper has significant implications for interaction design research, arguing that design scholarship must develop methods appropriate for studying unique, intentional, and situated artifacts rather than borrowing wholesale from natural science.

## Key Concepts
- Ultimate particular (the unique designed artifact as design's fundamental unit)
- Design as intentional creation (vs. scientific discovery or artistic expression)
- Instrumental judgment in design
- Designerly inquiry vs. scientific inquiry
- Wicked problems and design response

## Theoretical Framework
Philosophy of design practice; draws on Aristotle's practical reasoning (phronesis), Rittel's wicked problems, and Schön's reflective practitioner to characterize design's distinctive epistemology.

## Methods
Conceptual and philosophical argument; no empirical data; analysis of design practice characteristics through philosophical lenses.

## Main Arguments
- Design's fundamental goal is the ultimate particular: a specific, intended, never-before-existing artifact
- This goal distinguishes design from science (universal knowledge) and art (expression) and requires different methods and evaluation criteria
- Interaction design research must grapple with the tension between seeking generalizable knowledge and supporting unique design outcomes
- Design inquiry requires judgment, not just method — practical wisdom that cannot be fully codified

## Limitations & Critiques
The paper's philosophical focus can make it difficult to operationalize for design researchers; the emphasis on uniqueness may understate the extent to which designers work with patterns, frameworks, and reusable knowledge.
