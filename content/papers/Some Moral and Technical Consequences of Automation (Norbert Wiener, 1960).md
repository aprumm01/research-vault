# Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation (Norbert Wiener, 1960)

**Year:** 1999
**Venue:** chine's computation and action. Wiener makes the point that

## Summary
Wiener's prescient 1960 article argues against the naive assumption that machines cannot possess originality or escape meaningful human control, warning that the speed differential between machine computation and human response creates dangerous gaps in oversight. Using game-playing machines as an illustration, Wiener demonstrates that machines can learn from past performance in ways that transcend their designers' specific intentions.

## Key Concepts
- Cybernetics
- Machine learning and adaptivity
- Human-machine control gap
- Time constant problem
- Machine "originality"

## Theoretical Framework
Wiener's own cybernetics framework: views machines and living organisms as control-and-communication systems; challenges the "nothing comes out that wasn't put in" assumption that had given people false confidence about machine safety.

## Methods
Theoretical argument based on analogical reasoning; game-playing machines (checkers, chess) as concrete illustration of adaptive learning; reference to industrial and military automation contexts.

## Main Arguments
- Machines can transcend their designers' intentions through adaptive learning—this is not hypothetical but already demonstrated in game-playing programs
- The time differential between machine action speed and human comprehension speed means effective oversight may arrive too late to matter
- Moral consequences of automation are profound: society must develop understanding of machine behavior pari passu with the machine's development
- The assumption that machines are simply tools subject to human control at any moment is dangerously wrong

## Limitations & Critiques
Historical text from 1960; written before modern computing; some specific predictions have proven accurate (machine learning, AI in games) while others reflect Cold War-era concerns; lacks specificity about governance mechanisms; Penrose's later counter-argument about machine consciousness is not addressed.
