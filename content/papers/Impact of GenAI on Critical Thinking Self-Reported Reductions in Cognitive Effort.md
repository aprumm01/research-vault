# The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking: Self-Reported Reductions in Cognitive Effort and Confidence Effects From a Survey of Knowledge Workers

## Summary
This CHI 2025 paper surveys 319 knowledge workers who collectively shared 936 first-hand examples of using GenAI in work tasks, directly measuring perceptions of critical thinking enaction and cognitive effort. The study finds that GenAI shifts critical thinking from task execution toward information verification, response integration, and task stewardship — and that self-confidence in GenAI (not just task difficulty) is a key predictor of whether critical thinking is engaged at all. Higher confidence in GenAI reduces critical thinking effort, while higher self-confidence in one's own abilities increases it.

## Key Concepts
- **Enaction of Critical Thinking**: Observable actions that signal critical thinking (goal-setting, prompt refinement, output verification) rather than critical thinking as a pure mental phenomenon
- **Task Stewardship**: A new cognitive role that emerges with GenAI use, where the worker shifts from producing content to overseeing, verifying, and integrating AI-generated material
- **Mechanised Convergence**: The tendency for GenAI-assisted workers to produce a less diverse set of outputs for identical tasks, interpreted as a decline in personal, contextualised critical judgment
- **Self-Confidence as Moderator**: Workers' confidence in their own task abilities is positively associated with more critical thinking effort; confidence in GenAI is negatively associated with it
- **Information Verification Shift**: GenAI reduces effort in content generation but increases it in verification — workers must cross-check AI outputs against external sources and their own expertise
- **Barriers to Critical Thinking**: Lack of awareness, time pressure, and difficulty improving AI responses in unfamiliar domains all inhibit reflective engagement with GenAI outputs
- **Bloom's Taxonomy Application**: The study maps knowledge workers' critical thinking activities onto Bloom's taxonomy (recall, organisation, problem-solving, analysis, synthesis, evaluation)

## Theoretical Framework
The paper positions GenAI as the latest in a long lineage of technologies that raise questions about cognitive atrophy — from writing (Socrates), printing (Trithemius), calculators, and the internet. Drawing on Bainbridge's (1983) "automation irony," the authors argue that by automating routine tasks, AI deprives users of the practice opportunities needed to maintain judgment. This creates a training deficit: workers become less prepared for exceptions precisely because AI handles the routine cases that previously exercised their critical faculties.

The theoretical framing of critical thinking for knowledge work builds on Drucker and Kidd's conception of knowledge workers, whose value lies in judgment and non-routine problem-solving. The study operationalises critical thinking through Bloom's Revised Taxonomy, enabling systematic mapping of which cognitive levels GenAI affects and how.

## Methods
Survey study with 319 knowledge workers recruited via a professional survey panel. Participants provided 936 first-hand examples of GenAI use in work tasks, structured via a detailed instrument asking them to describe task type, effort change across Bloom's taxonomy dimensions, occurrence of critical thinking, and factors facilitating or inhibiting it. Analysis combined quantitative regression modelling (predictors of critical thinking enaction and effort) and qualitative thematic analysis of free-text responses. The study focuses on knowledge work broadly, not education, distinguishing it from most prior GenAI-cognition research.

## Main Arguments
- GenAI reduces perceived critical thinking effort for most workers, particularly for lower-stakes and routine tasks where workers simply accept AI output without reflection
- Higher confidence in GenAI capability is significantly associated with less critical thinking effort, suggesting that trust in the tool is a mechanism through which critical thinking is bypassed
- Higher worker self-confidence in their own abilities is associated with more critical thinking effort, as self-assured workers apply their expertise to evaluate and improve AI outputs
- GenAI shifts the nature of critical thinking toward information verification, response integration, and task stewardship — a qualitatively different cognitive posture than creative or analytical production
- Workers who use GenAI primarily for knowledge retrieval or content generation experience the greatest effort reduction at lower Bloom's taxonomy levels, but face new verification demands at higher levels
- The phenomenon of "mechanised convergence" (reduced output diversity under AI assistance) is presented as indirect evidence of critical thinking reduction, but the authors argue direct measurement of cognitive effort is necessary for robust inference

## Limitations & Critiques
As a self-report survey, the study measures perceptions of critical thinking effort rather than actual cognitive processes; participants may have poor introspective access to their own reasoning. The sample is professionally diverse but not random, potentially over-representing tech-forward workers. The study is cross-sectional and cannot establish long-term trends in cognitive atrophy. The operationalisation of "enaction" of critical thinking via observable actions may exclude important internal reasoning. The study also acknowledges that reflective thinking that leads to accepting AI output unchanged is invisible to output-based measures, so critical engagement may be systematically under-counted.
