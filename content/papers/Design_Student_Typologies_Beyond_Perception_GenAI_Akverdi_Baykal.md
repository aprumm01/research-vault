# Design Student Typologies: Beyond Perception of Generative AI

## Summary
This paper develops a typology-driven framework for understanding how undergraduate design students engage with generative AI in illustration-based courses. Through qualitative thematic analysis of 81 students' open-ended survey responses, the authors identify two intersecting spectra—a constructive spectrum describing how students integrate GenAI into creative workflows, and an ethical spectrum describing their moral positioning toward AI use. The study moves beyond simple positive/negative perception measures to reveal the nuanced, multidimensional nature of student-AI co-creation.

## Key Concepts
- **Constructive Spectrum**: A four-position typology capturing how students use GenAI across design phases: Starters (early-stage ideation), Accelerators (efficiency-focused), Doubters-Survivors (skeptical but pragmatic), and Finishers (post-production use)
- **Ethical Spectrum**: A four-position typology capturing students' moral orientation toward GenAI: Advocates (embrace AI openly), Guardians (protect creative autonomy), Witnesses (observe critically without strong stance), and Suspects (question AI's legitimacy)
- **Prompt Literacy**: The competency to craft purposeful textual prompts that shape generative outputs—identified as a new creative skill students must develop
- **Over-Reliant Users**: Students who depend heavily on AI outputs, risking "creative erosion" and weakening of intrinsic motivation and personal style development
- **Human-AI Co-creation**: The collaborative dynamic where student maintains creative judgment and final decision-making authority while AI contributes generative capabilities
- **Originality-Creativity Dilemma**: The tension between AI's capacity to generate novel combinations and its tendency toward stylistic imitation and homogenization
- **Creative Maturity**: The way academic year and design experience shapes students' capacity to integrate GenAI critically rather than dependently

## Theoretical Framework
The study is grounded in constructivist learning theory and studio-based design pedagogy, where creativity, originality, and critical reflection are foundational competencies. The authors draw on recent GenAI literature around four intersecting themes: GenAI's role in creative production, ethical debates about authorship and ownership, prompt literacy as a creative skill, and GenAI's influence on ideation processes. The typological approach builds on thematic analysis methodology (Byrne's six-step framework) with axial coding to identify emergent patterns.

The dual-spectrum model treats student engagement not as a linear progression from novice to expert, but as a multidimensional space where creative use patterns and ethical orientations are distinct but intersecting—a student can be an Accelerator (high constructive use) while also being a Guardian (ethically cautious), for example.

## Methods
An anonymous online survey was administered to 81 Communication and Design undergraduates at Özyeğin University (Istanbul) from first through fourth year, all enrolled in or having completed illustration courses. Nine open-ended questions covered AI tool use, creative thinking processes, and ethical evaluations. Data were analyzed using Byrne's six-step thematic analysis with axial coding, and reliability was ensured through double-coding with a second researcher. The cross-level design enabled examination of how design proficiency and creative maturity shape GenAI use patterns.

## Main Arguments
- GenAI supports at least three distinct use patterns in ideation: Inspiration Seekers (stimulus and exploration), Balanced Seekers (regulated use to preserve agency), and Over-Reliant Users (heavy dependence with risk of creative erosion)—indicating that uniform pedagogical responses are insufficient
- In iterative development phases, two orientations emerged—Augmentation Seekers who leverage AI without surrendering control, and Deep Divers who iteratively refine AI outputs while maintaining intent—suggesting students can develop sophisticated co-creative strategies
- In visualization, students adopted diverse strategies including Speed Seekers, Concept Polishers, Picky Users, and Post-Production Users, demonstrating that AI's efficiency benefits are valued but selectively appropriated
- The originality-creativity dilemma is navigated differently across students: some view originality as dependent on user intention rather than the tool itself, while others experience AI as fundamentally threatening to personal creative voice
- Ethical positioning (the ethical spectrum) was not correlated with academic year, suggesting ethical awareness is not simply a product of maturation but develops through specific experiences with GenAI in studio contexts
- The paper argues design curricula must address both constructive integration and ethical accountability simultaneously, rather than treating them as sequential concerns

## Limitations & Critiques
The study is limited to a single institution in Turkey, raising questions about cultural transferability. The small, homogeneous sample (illustration students at one university) may not represent the diversity of design disciplines. Qualitative typologies derived from self-reported survey responses may not capture actual creative behaviors, which can diverge from stated attitudes. The cross-sectional design cannot establish whether students progress through typologies over time or whether these represent stable individual differences.

The typology framework, while descriptive, does not yet specify pedagogical interventions for each type—the translation from diagnosis to curriculum design remains underdeveloped.
