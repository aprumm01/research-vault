# Shaping Human Capital Standards - Exploring the intersections of the future of work and AI

## Overview of the Document

The authors of this report are Tania Saba, Anne-Marie Hubert, and Myriam Bernet, representing a blend of academic expertise and policy experience positioned at the intersection of labor relations, governance, and artificial intelligence. Tania Saba holds a PhD and the CRHA Distinction Fellowship, serving as a Full Professor in the School of Industrial Relations at the Université de Montréal's Faculty of Arts and Science. She chairs the BMO en diversité et gouvernance (BMO Chair in Diversity and Governance) and directs equity, diversity, and inclusion initiatives for Obvia, an open research network funded by the Fonds de recherche du Québec that brings together over 250 researchers across humanities, social sciences, engineering, and health fields. Anne-Marie Hubert brings extensive governance experience, holding the designations C.M. (Member of the Order of Canada), C.Q. (Officer of the National Order of Quebec), FCPA (Fellow of CPA Canada), and an honorary doctorate (LL.D.). She serves as an Invited Fellow at CIRANO and chairs the board of the Human Technology Foundation in Montreal. Myriam Bernet, holding an MA in Political Science, works as a Research Professional for the BMO Chair in Diversity and Governance. This combination of deep academic grounding in labor relations, senior governance experience, and political science research positions the team to bridge scholarly analysis with policy-relevant recommendations.

This document is a policy research report published in January 2025 by Obvia (Observatoire international sur les impacts sociétaux de l'intelligence artificielle et du numérique) with financial support from the Fonds de recherche du Québec. It appears as a white paper or working document rather than a peer-reviewed journal article, designed explicitly to inform policymakers and investors about the social dimensions of sustainable finance standards. The report sits at the nexus of several fields: sustainable finance, labor economics, human resource management, technology policy, and international governance. It matters because it addresses a critical gap in emerging sustainability reporting frameworks, where environmental standards are relatively well-developed but social dimensions, particularly human capital metrics in the age of AI, remain poorly defined. The work anticipates organizations' challenges as they must soon comply with International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) disclosure requirements, providing conceptual grounding and evidence for what human capital indicators should be measured and why.

The report's significance extends beyond academic contribution to direct policy influence. As central banks increasingly integrate climate risk into financial decisions following the Paris Agreement (2015) and the Kunming-Montreal Accord (2022), and as investors demand reliable non-financial sustainability information, the IFRS Foundation faces pressure to establish meaningful social standards comparable to its environmental frameworks. This report aims to shape those standards by systematically identifying which dimensions of human capital are most affected by AI transformation, thereby influencing what organizations worldwide will eventually be required to disclose to investors and regulators.

## Research Overview

The central research question asks: What are the critical dimensions of human capital at the intersection of work, sustainability, and artificial intelligence that policymakers and investors must consider when developing global disclosure standards? More specifically, the authors seek to identify how AI reshapes human capital elements such as skills, knowledge, access to resources, diversity, and collective capabilities, and to categorize these impacts as opportunities, risks, or uncertainties that should inform sustainability frameworks.

The research was conducted by the three authors named above, working through Obvia and the BMO Chair in Diversity and Governance. Rather than collecting original empirical data, they performed a systematic literature review and analysis of institutional reports from authoritative international organizations. As the authors explain their approach, "we consulted databases of international, Canadian and European institutions known for influential research in these fields" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 6), selecting organizations based on "established credibility and the relevance of their research to policy formulation and investment strategy" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 6).

The methodology involved searching the databases and websites of organizations including the OECD, ILO, IMF, World Economic Forum, Canadian institutions (Diversity Institute, Future Skills Centre), and European bodies (Eurofound, European Commission, European Parliament, CFE-CGC). The search strategy employed targeted English keywords: "work", "future of work", "artificial intelligence", "technology", "human capital", "sustainability", "workers' rights", "diversity", "green transition", "environment" and "policy" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 6). Given the rapid evolution of AI, the researchers limited their analysis to publications from 2022 to 2024, with most reports published in 2024, ensuring currency and relevance.

The final dataset consisted of 31 reports: 17 from the OECD (including four co-authored with UNESCO, the Inter-American Development Bank, and the International Trade Centre), five from the ILO, two from the IMF, two from the Future Skills Centre, and one each from the WEF, Eurofound, the European Commission, the European Parliament, and CFE-CGC. The analytical approach involved first identifying dimensions explicitly associated with human capital in these reports, then conducting an in-depth assessment of each dimension to evaluate its implications in terms of risks, opportunities, and uncertainties. The findings were categorized and aggregated to highlight recurring patterns aligned with the priorities of policymakers and investors.

Key research concepts require clarification. Human capital, originally defined by Gary Becker in 1964, referred to "skills, knowledge, experience, and abilities that enhance individual productivity and economic potential" with emphasis on "investments in education, training, health, and mobility" being "as critical as investments in physical capital" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 8). The authors trace how this concept has evolved to encompass health and well-being, social and emotional skills, diversity and inclusion as strategic assets, AI-specific competencies, interdisciplinary skills for green jobs, and workforce autonomy and ethical practices. Sustainable finance refers to integrating social and environmental considerations into financial systems and investment decisions, driven by frameworks like the Paris Agreement and operationalized through IFRS Foundation standards. The ISSB (International Sustainability Standards Board) establishes these standards, prioritizing transparency and decision-useful disclosure for investors assessing sustainability risks and opportunities.

## Theories of Knowledge

The primary theoretical framework grounding this research is human capital theory, explicitly traced to Gary Becker's 1964 seminal work. Becker's theory posits that investments in people through education, training, and health are productive investments analogous to investments in physical capital, enhancing both individual productivity and broader economic growth. The authors cite Becker directly: he "defined human capital as the skills, knowledge, experience, and abilities that enhance individual productivity and economic potential" and "emphasized that investments in education, training, health, and mobility are as critical as investments in physical capital for fostering innovation and driving economic growth" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 8). This foundational theory provides the conceptual lens through which the report examines how AI affects the value and composition of human capital.

However, the authors explicitly note that the concept has evolved substantially since Becker's original formulation. They trace an expansion to include dimensions that "reflect the diverse needs of modern economies" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 8), including not just foundational skills and knowledge but also "health and well-being, both physical and mental" as "essential for maintaining a capable and resilient workforce" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 8). The contemporary understanding further encompasses "social and emotional skills, such as adaptability, teamwork, and emotional intelligence" which have "gained prominence, highlighting their value in dynamic and collaborative work environments" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 8). Importantly, "diversity and inclusion have become strategic aspects of human capital, emphasizing the importance of leveraging varied perspectives to drive innovation and competitiveness" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 8).

A second theoretical thread, less explicitly articulated but clearly present, draws on sustainable development frameworks, particularly the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the Paris Agreement on climate change. These frameworks embed assumptions about the inseparability of economic, social, and environmental dimensions of development. The report positions human capital development as necessarily aligned with "broader societal and sustainability goals" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 8), rejecting purely economic optimization in favor of multidimensional well-being.

Third, the report implicitly invokes stakeholder theory from corporate governance and organizational studies. By framing the research around what "policymakers and investors" need to know, and by positioning the work within IFRS Foundation disclosure standards, the authors assume that corporations have accountability not just to shareholders for financial returns but to broader stakeholders (workers, communities, society) for social and environmental impacts. This represents a shift from shareholder primacy toward multi-stakeholder capitalism, where human capital is not merely an input to be optimized but a constituency whose well-being must be disclosed and managed.

Finally, the analytical framework of opportunities, risks, and uncertainties itself represents a risk management and foresight methodology common in policy analysis and strategic planning. This tripartite categorization allows for nuanced assessment that avoids both technological determinism (AI will automatically produce certain outcomes) and technological neutrality (AI's effects are entirely socially constructed), instead acknowledging genuine potential benefits, identifiable hazards, and irreducible unknowns.

## Central Arguments

The central argument of this report is that human capital standards for sustainability disclosure must account for AI's transformative but highly differentiated impacts across multiple dimensions, and that current frameworks inadequately capture the social aspects of this transformation. The authors contend that while environmental sustainability standards are "more clearly established," social dimensions "continue to lag in terms of well-defined frameworks and indicators" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 5), creating an urgent need for systematic identification of human capital metrics relevant to AI-driven organizational change.

A second major claim addresses the dual nature of AI's impact on human capital. The authors argue that AI simultaneously creates opportunities such as "the increase of productivity and the development of new skill sets" while introducing "risks like job polarization and challenges related to inclusivity" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 7). This framing rejects both uncritical AI boosterism and blanket technopessimism, instead insisting that "by examining these factors, organizations can better understand the evolving role of human capital in an increasingly AI-driven environment" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 7).

The third key argument concerns the inadequacy of traditional educational and workforce development systems. The research documents that "education systems, often slow to adapt, face difficulties integrating green and digital skills into curricula" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 9), while "deficits in STEM education hinder the development of talent for high-growth sectors like AI and green technology" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 9). This represents a structural mismatch between the pace of technological change and institutional capacity to prepare workers, necessitating greater emphasis on "lifelong learning in preparing workers for rapidly evolving job roles" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 9).

Fourth, the authors argue that workforce diversity and inclusion are not peripheral concerns but central to human capital quality in AI contexts. They emphasize that "gender gaps persist, with women underrepresented in AI-related fields and leadership positions, while systemic barriers continue to exclude marginalized groups" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 9). This claim positions diversity not as a moral add-on but as a core component of human capital that directly affects organizational capacity for innovation and adaptation.

Fifth, the report contends that AI-driven workplace monitoring creates serious threats to worker autonomy and well-being. The authors document how "AI-driven monitoring tools erode privacy and autonomy, contributing to stress and dissatisfaction" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 9), while "bias in AI hiring algorithms perpetuates inequities" and "task automation risks deskilling workers, reducing engagement and career growth opportunities" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 9). This argument positions worker well-being and autonomy as human capital dimensions that can be degraded by AI even as productivity increases.

Finally, the authors argue that global disparities in AI access and capability represent a critical dimension of human capital inequality. They note that "many developing nations lack access to AI resources, limiting their ability to build talent and participate in global advancements" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 9), with "digital and educational inequalities" further exacerbating "skill gaps, with vulnerable populations such as women and rural communities facing additional barriers" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 9-10). This frames AI transformation not as a universally available tide that lifts all boats but as a potentially disequalizing force requiring deliberate policy intervention.

## Evidence

The evidence base for these arguments comes entirely from the 31 institutional reports analyzed rather than from original data collection. The authors categorize findings into opportunities, risks, and uncertainties across multiple dimensions.

Regarding opportunities, the report identifies six categories supported by citations to multiple sources. For resource optimization, the authors cite how "predictive climate modeling and advanced energy systems" contribute to "sustainable development" and how "digital twins" improve "resilience against environmental changes" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 10), drawing on OECD reports from 2022b, 2024b, and 2024d. For enhanced decision-making, evidence comes from studies documenting how AI can mitigate "biases" and foster "fairness across organizational and industrial settings" while "human-AI collaboration enhances the precision and ethical alignment of decisions" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 10), citing Krämer and Cazes (2022) and UNESCO/OECD/IDB (2022).

For workforce transformation, the evidence shows "AI-driven automation mitigates risks in repetitive tasks, enhancing occupational safety and enabling workers to focus on higher-value activities" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 10), with support from Cappelli and Rogovsky (2023) and Chagny et al. (2023). Additionally, "generative AI democratizes access to sophisticated tools, empowering individuals with limited formal training or experience" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 10), citing OECD 2024c, 2024e, and WEF 2024. For innovation acceleration, AI's role in scientific breakthroughs and value chain reconfiguration is documented through WEF (2024).

Regarding risks, the report provides detailed evidence across eight categories. Environmental impacts are substantial: AI's "high computational power demands lead to considerable energy consumption, often outpacing the efficiency gains achieved through hardware improvements" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 11), particularly in "resource-intensive sectors like mining and manufacturing" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 11), supported by OECD 2022b, 2024d and Krämer and Cazes 2022. Algorithmic bias risks are documented through evidence that "biases embedded in training datasets can result in discriminatory outcomes, particularly in sensitive areas such as recruitment, lending, and law enforcement" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 11), citing Cappelli and Rogovsky 2023, Chagny et al. 2023, and multiple OECD reports.

Job displacement evidence is particularly strong: "automation driven by AI is displacing jobs, particularly those involving repetitive tasks, disproportionately affecting low- and medium-skilled workers" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 12), with specialized AI skill requirements creating "a talent gap, widening disparities across regions and sectors" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 12), supported by citations to Future Skills Centre, ILO, OECD, and UNESCO sources. Privacy and security vulnerabilities arise because "AI's reliance on vast amounts of data increases vulnerabilities to breaches and adversarial attacks" while "monitoring systems" amplify "ethical issues related to surveillance and the erosion of individual privacy" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 11), citing multiple OECD reports and WEF 2024.

Accountability challenges stem from "the opaque nature of AI decision-making processes, particularly in critical sectors like healthcare and finance, where the lack of explainability can lead to ethical dilemmas and potential legal liabilities" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 11-12). Generative AI introduces manipulation risks as these "tools facilitate the creation of synthetic content, deepfakes, and false narratives, eroding social trust and stability" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 12), citing OECD 2024b, 2024c, 2024i. Power concentration is documented through evidence that "concentration of AI resources and expertise within a few dominant tech entities restricts equitable access and stifles competition and innovation" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 12), citing Chagny et al. 2023 and OECD 2024b.

For uncertainties, the report identifies seven categories. Technological measurement complexity arises because "AI-specific environmental impacts are often intertwined with broader information and communication technology (ICT) systems, complicating assessments of AI's full environmental footprint" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 13), citing Cappelli and Rogovsky 2023, ILO 2024b, and OECD sources. Emergent capabilities represent genuine unknowns: "large-scale AI systems exhibit emergent capabilities, such as unexpected autonomous decision-making, which introduces new layers of unpredictability" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 13), citing Krämer and Cazes 2022 and WEF 2024.

Labor market dynamics remain ambiguous: "the impact of AI on the job market remains ambiguous, with its potential to create jobs balanced against risks of displacement, which vary across demographics and sectors" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 13), supported by Alexander et al. 2024, André and Gal 2024, Future Skills Centre 2024, ILO 2024b, and multiple OECD reports. Equity concerns persist as "emerging economies face significant barriers" in AI access, "exacerbating global inequalities in technology adoption and utilization" (Saba et al., 2025, p. 13), citing ILO 2024b and OECD 2022a, 2022b, 2024a.

The study's limitations stem primarily from its methodological design. First, as a literature review of institutional reports rather than original research, the findings are constrained by what these organizations chose to study and publish. The 31-report sample, while representing authoritative sources, reflects institutional priorities and biases of predominantly Western international organizations (OECD, IMF, WEF) and may underrepresent perspectives from the Global South or from labor movements, civil society organizations, or affected workers themselves.

Second, the focus on English-language publications from 2022-2024 creates both temporal and linguistic constraints. Older foundational research is excluded, and non-English scholarship remains invisible. Third, the report examines human capital impacts at a high level of aggregation across multiple sectors and geographies, necessarily obscuring important contextual variation. The experiences of AI's impact on human capital likely differ substantially between manufacturing workers in Germany, agricultural workers in India, and gig economy workers in Brazil, but these differences are flattened in the synthesis. Fourth, the opportunities-risks-uncertainties framework, while useful for policy analysis, may artificially categorize phenomena that are genuinely ambiguous or that shift between categories depending on context and implementation.

## Conclusion

This research makes its primary contribution by systematically mapping the landscape of human capital dimensions affected by AI transformation, specifically organized to inform the development of sustainability disclosure standards. At a moment when the IFRS Foundation and ISSB face pressure to establish meaningful social metrics comparable to environmental standards, this report provides conceptual and evidentiary grounding for which human capital indicators should be prioritized for measurement and disclosure. The comprehensive cataloging of opportunities (resource optimization, enhanced decision-making, workforce transformation, innovation acceleration, social inclusion, sustainability support), risks (environmental impact, algorithmic bias, privacy erosion, job displacement, accountability gaps, manipulation potential, power concentration, governance fragmentation), and uncertainties (measurement complexity, emergent capabilities, labor market ambiguity, equity challenges, regulatory lag, reskilling demands) offers policymakers and standard-setters a structured framework for understanding AI's multifaceted human capital impacts.

The work's emphasis on expanding the conception of human capital beyond Becker's original formulation to include well-being, emotional intelligence, diversity, autonomy, and ethical considerations reflects and potentially accelerates a broader shift in corporate governance toward multi-stakeholder accountability. By positioning these dimensions not as nice-to-have additions but as core components that organizations will need to disclose to investors and regulators, the report gives institutional weight to concerns that might otherwise remain peripheral to business strategy and financial decision-making.

The finding that AI's impacts are highly differentiated across dimensions, sectors, and populations provides important nuance for policy design. The evidence that education systems struggle to adapt, that gender gaps persist in AI fields, that monitoring tools erode worker autonomy, and that global disparities in AI access are widening all suggest that AI transformation without deliberate policy intervention risks exacerbating existing inequalities rather than creating broadly shared prosperity. This challenges narratives of inevitable technological progress and underscores the importance of proactive governance.

However, substantial questions remain open. First, the report identifies what should be measured but provides limited guidance on how to measure it. Developing reliable, comparable metrics for dimensions like workforce well-being, diversity quality (beyond simple representation numbers), worker autonomy, or AI's impact on skill development requires methodological work that lies beyond this report's scope. Second, the relative importance of different dimensions remains unclear. Should disclosure standards prioritize all identified dimensions equally, or should some receive more emphasis? What trade-offs exist between comprehensive coverage and practical feasibility?

Third, the mechanisms through which disclosure standards translate into organizational behavior change are not examined. Will requiring companies to report on human capital dimensions actually lead to improvements in those dimensions, or might it lead to measurement gaming and box-checking compliance? What complementary policies (regulation, taxation, labor law, competition policy) are needed alongside disclosure requirements to ensure meaningful impact?

Fourth, the report captures a snapshot from 2022-2024, but both AI capabilities and social responses are evolving rapidly. Which findings will prove durable and which will be overtaken by technological or social change? How should standard-setting processes balance the need for stability and comparability over time against the need to adapt to rapidly shifting conditions? Finally, the report's focus on formal employment and traditional organizational contexts may miss important dynamics in informal economies, gig work, and precarious employment arrangements where AI's human capital impacts may be most pronounced but least visible to standard-setters and investors.

For a PhD student returning to this document six months from now, the key takeaway is this: when international organizations develop standards for sustainability disclosure, the categories they choose to measure shape what corporations attend to and what investors value. This report represents an effort to ensure that human capital dimensions affected by AI receive attention proportional to their importance, not just environmental metrics that are easier to quantify. The success of this effort will determine whether AI transformation unfolds within a governance framework that treats workers as stakeholders whose well-being and capabilities matter, or whether social impacts remain externalities that organizations can ignore in pursuit of productivity gains.

## Discussion Questions

Does this expansion strengthen or dilute the concept's analytical utility? At what point does "human capital" become so broad that it loses specificity as an analytical category?

How might this tripartite framework shape what gets prioritized in sustainability disclosure standards? Are there AI impacts that don't fit neatly into these categories, and what happens to concerns that are categorized as "uncertainties" rather than identifiable "risks"?

3. The report documents that "social dimensions continue to lag in terms of well-defined frameworks and indicators" compared to environmental standards, creating pressure to develop human capital metrics for AI impacts. What are the potential unintended consequences of quantifying and standardizing human capital metrics? How does this methodological choice shape the report's framing of AI's human capital impacts? What perspectives or concerns might be systematically underrepresented in this evidence base, and how would the analysis differ if it centered worker testimony or Global South scholarship?
