# From "Ban It Till We Understand It" to "Resistance is Futile": How University Programming Instructors Plan to Adapt as More Students Use AI Code Generation and Explanation Tools such as ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot

## Summary
This is the first empirical study of how university introductory programming instructors plan to respond to the widespread availability of AI code generation and explanation tools (ChatGPT, GitHub Copilot). Through interviews with 20 instructors across 9 countries conducted in early 2023, the authors document a rare snapshot of educator attitudes at the moment these tools became broadly accessible. Short-term reactions centered on anti-cheating measures, while longer-term opinions bifurcated between those wanting to ban AI and those wanting to integrate it into their courses.

## Key Concepts
- **AI coding tools**: LLM-based tools including GitHub Copilot (IDE-integrated), ChatGPT (conversational), and OpenAI Playground (standalone) that can generate, explain, and debug code
- **Resist vs. embrace bifurcation**: The central tension in instructor attitudes — some want to preserve fundamental programming skills by banning AI, others see integration as necessary preparation for industry
- **Academic integrity concerns**: The immediate near-universal concern about AI-enabled cheating, leading to increased exam weight, proctoring, and policy changes
- **AI-proof assignments**: Design strategies to create tasks AI cannot easily complete (e.g., oral exams, in-class coding, highly contextualized problems)
- **Prompt engineering as curriculum**: The idea that learning to specify tasks for AI systems is itself a valuable computational thinking skill worth teaching
- **Instructor positionality**: The variation in attitudes correlated with country, institution type, and personal AI tool experience

## Theoretical Framework
The study is grounded in qualitative educational research traditions, using semi-structured interviews analyzed through thematic coding. The authors adopt a human-centered framing, treating instructor cognition and decision-making as the primary unit of analysis. The theoretical backdrop draws loosely on technology adoption theory (the tension between early adopters and resisters) and educational philosophy debates about authentic learning versus practical preparation.

The paper makes no strong theoretical claims, instead presenting its value as an empirical snapshot at a historically significant moment. The dichotomy of "resist vs. embrace" is inductively derived from the data rather than imposed a priori, making it descriptive rather than prescriptive.

## Methods
Semi-structured qualitative interviews with 20 introductory programming course instructors (9 women, 11 men) recruited from 9 countries (Australia, Botswana, Canada, Chile, China, Rwanda, Spain, Switzerland, United States) spanning all 6 populated continents. Interviews were conducted in early 2023 (the first academic term after ChatGPT's November 2022 release). Participants ranged from those with zero prior AI tool experience to those who had used them for personal programming. Transcripts were analyzed using thematic coding.

## Main Arguments
- **All instructors were immediately concerned about cheating**: Short-term responses were nearly universal — increasing exam scores' weight, creating policies explicitly addressing AI use, or showing students AI capabilities and limitations as a deterrent.
- **Longer-term opinions split into two futures**: One group (resisters) wanted to ban AI tools and focus on foundational programming skills, arguing that understanding computational fundamentals is still essential. The other (embracers) wanted to integrate AI tools to prepare students for jobs where such tools will be ubiquitous.
- **Resisters proposed "AI-proof" assignment designs**: These included in-person oral exams, live coding with proctors, process documentation requirements, and highly contextualized problems that require deep course knowledge.
- **Embracers envisioned new AI-collaborative assignment types**: These included having students collaborate with AI to build larger projects, critically evaluate AI output, and develop prompt engineering skills — arguing that these are skills industry already requires.
- **Early 2023 represents a historically unique snapshot**: Instructors are forming initial opinions without best practices, making this a pivotal moment where community norms and policies are still malleable.
- **Equity and global access are understudied concerns**: Instructors from less-resourced countries (e.g., Rwanda) raised concerns about internet access disparities, while some noted that AI tools could democratize access to coding help for students without tutors.

## Limitations & Critiques
The 20-participant sample, while geographically diverse, is small and may not be representative of the broader population of programming instructors. Participants were self-selected and likely more engaged with the AI topic than the average instructor. The study captures intentions and plans rather than actual behavior, and subsequent research would be needed to verify whether instructors followed through on their stated plans. The early 2023 timing means some expressed concerns and ideas may already be outdated as tools and norms have evolved.

The paper does not deeply analyze pedagogical effectiveness of proposed approaches — it captures opinions without evaluating whether "AI-proof" assignments actually improve learning or whether integrating AI actually prepares students better.
