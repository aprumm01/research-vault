# What Field Did You Say You Were In? Defining and Naming Our Field

## Summary
This foundational book chapter examines the evolving definitions and names used to describe the field of instructional design and technology (ID&T). Reiser traces how the field's identity has shifted over nearly a century, from early definitions focused on instructional media (films, pictures, audiovisual materials) to more recent conceptualizations emphasizing systematic design processes and performance improvement. The chapter addresses fundamental questions practitioners must grapple with: what are the boundaries of the field, and how should it be defined and named?

The evolution of definitions reflects changing priorities and practices. Early 20th-century definitions focused on "visual instruction" and the media used to present instruction. By the 1960s and 1970s, leaders began discussing instructional technology as a process rather than just media, leading to landmark definitions from professional organizations. The 1970 Commission on Instructional Technology provided two definitions: one traditional (media-focused) and one emphasizing systematic design processes. The 1977 AECT definition expanded to sixteen statements covering complex, integrated processes for analyzing problems and managing solutions.

The most recent 2008 AECT definition describes educational technology as "the study and ethical practice of facilitating learning and improving performance by creating, using, and managing appropriate technological processes and resources." Reiser presents his own textbook definition emphasizing two foundational practices: the systematic design of instruction and the use of media for instructional purposes, while also acknowledging the field's growing focus on human performance improvement in workplace contexts.

## Key Concepts
- **Instructional Technology as Media**: The traditional view equating the field with physical means of presenting instruction (films, computers, television)
- **Instructional Technology as Process**: The systematic approach to designing, carrying out, and evaluating learning, emphasizing method over medium
- **Performance Improvement**: The expanding focus on improving human performance through both instructional and non-instructional solutions
- **Systematic Instructional Design**: The core practice of analyzing problems and designing, implementing, and evaluating solutions to learning challenges
- **Field Naming Debates**: The ongoing discussion about appropriate labels (educational technology, instructional technology, instructional design and technology)

## Theoretical Framework
The chapter provides a historical and conceptual framework for understanding the field's identity. Reiser draws on official definitions from professional organizations (AECT, Commission on Instructional Technology) while acknowledging that no single definition captures all practitioners' perspectives. The framework recognizes that field definitions are socially constructed, reflecting the practices, priorities, and professional contexts of those who create them.

## Methods
This is a conceptual/historical chapter that traces the evolution of field definitions through analysis of official statements from professional organizations and key publications. Reiser presents chronological examination of definitions from 1963, 1970, 1977, 1994, and 2008, analyzing how each reflects changing understandings of the field's scope and purpose.

## Main Arguments
- The field has been called many names over the years (audiovisual instruction, educational technology, instructional technology, instructional design and technology)
- Definitions have evolved from media-focused to process-focused to performance-focused
- No single definition will ever capture the thinking and actions of all professionals in the field
- The 2008 AECT definition advances the field by emphasizing ethics, facilitation of learning, and performance improvement
- Two foundational practices define the field: systematic instructional design and use of media for instructional purposes
- The field has increasingly expanded to address workplace performance through both instructional and non-instructional interventions
- The term "instructional design and technology" appropriately acknowledges both core practices

## Limitations & Critiques
The chapter acknowledges that definitions are necessarily incomplete and reflect particular perspectives. No definition statement has been universally accepted, and professionals operate from diverse personal, institutional, and theoretical perspectives. The chapter focuses primarily on U.S. professional organizations and may not fully represent international variations in how the field is conceptualized. The historical overview necessarily simplifies complex debates and developments that occurred over decades.
