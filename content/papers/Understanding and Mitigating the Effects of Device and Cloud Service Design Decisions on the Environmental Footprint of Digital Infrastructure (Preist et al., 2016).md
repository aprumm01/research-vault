## Summary

This paper critically examines how current design practices for interactive devices and cloud services contribute to the environmental impact of digital infrastructure. The authors identify what they call the "cornucopian paradigm"—a set of implicit design principles driven by user-centered design values (personal, variety, instant, shareable, high quality, pervasive, continuous access, eternal, ephemeral, rich/cross-modal/ubiquitous) that collectively drive growth in digital infrastructure demand.

The paper extends Blevis's Rubric of Material Effects to create the "Rubric of Infrastructural Effects" (RoIE), which poses five key questions for designers to consider:
1. Does the design encourage infrastructural expansion or obsolescence?
2. Does the design encourage increased infrastructural use?
3. Does the design mitigate or reduce infrastructural use in some way?
4. Does the design encourage digital waste, or the avoidance of it?
5. Does the design promote the sharing of infrastructure?

The authors then move beyond an eco-efficiency paradigm to consider more radical perspectives including "computing within limits" (based on planetary boundaries), "collapse informatics" (preparing for societal collapse scenarios), and "responsible design/Sustainable HCI 2.0" (questioning whether certain services should exist at all).

## Key Concepts

### The Cornucopian Paradigm
Ten design principles that drive infrastructure demand:
- **C1 PERSONAL**: Services increasingly individual rather than shared
- **C2 VARIETY**: Large choice expecting everything available
- **C3 INSTANT**: No noticeable access time expected
- **C4 SHAREABLE**: Creating and sharing content with others
- **C5 HIGH QUALITY**: Ever-increasing resolution and fidelity
- **C6 PERVASIVE**: Available across all devices
- **C7 CONTINUOUS ACCESS**: Access anytime, anywhere
- **C8 ETERNAL**: Always alive and always available content
- **C9 EPHEMERAL**: Creating/saving content regardless of future use
- **C10 RICH, CROSS-MODAL, UBIQUITOUS**: Multiple interacting services, IoT

### Rubric of Infrastructural Effects (RoIE)
Five questions extending Blevis's rubric to infrastructure:
- E1: Does design encourage infrastructural expansion/obsolescence?
- E2: Does design encourage increased infrastructural use?
- E3: Does design mitigate/reduce infrastructural use?
- E4: Does design encourage digital waste or its avoidance?
- E5: Does design promote sharing of infrastructure?

### Digital Waste
The accessing of cloud-based services without actual use:
- Content downloaded but never used (e.g., podcast subscriptions)
- Content partially used (streaming video as background music)
- Content that is never accessed after creation/upload
- Web page bounce (visiting pages immediately left)

### Alternative Paradigms
- **Computing within Limits**: Infrastructure must operate within planetary boundaries
- **Collapse Informatics**: Preparing for reduced infrastructure availability
- **Sustainable HCI 2.0**: Questioning whether services are worth their environmental cost

## Key Findings

1. **Infrastructure accounts for up to 85% of environmental impact** of some digital services, yet receives little attention in Sustainable HCI research.

2. **Reinforcing feedback loop**: Infrastructure capacity enables new services, which stimulate demand, which drives further infrastructure growth.

3. **Peak demand problem**: Services used at peak times require more infrastructure capacity even if total usage is similar to off-peak alternatives.

4. **Design interventions identified**:
   - Nudging users toward lower-bandwidth options (e.g., BBC iPlayer defaulting to SD)
   - Time-shifting downloads to off-peak hours
   - Encouraging sharing over individual access (multicast, mesh networks like Freifunk)
   - Detecting and reducing digital waste (video scrubber previews, intelligent download)

5. **Four future research directions**:
   - Accessible guidelines for designers to consider infrastructure impacts
   - Integration with Industrial Ecology quantification methods
   - Better understanding of user needs vs. wants for cloud services
   - Scenario analysis of future infrastructure trajectories within planetary limits

## Relevance to Sustainable Computing

This paper is foundational for understanding the relationship between HCI design decisions and environmental sustainability. It shifts focus from device-level concerns (materials, e-waste) to the broader digital infrastructure that supports interactive services.

**Key contributions to Sustainable HCI**:
- First systematic analysis of how user-centered design principles drive infrastructure growth
- Extension of Blevis's influential rubric to cover infrastructure effects
- Bridge between Sustainable HCI and Industrial Ecology perspectives
- Introduction of "computing within limits" thinking to HCI discourse
- Practical design rubric for evaluating infrastructure impacts

**Implications for practice**:
- Designers should consider not just device impacts but supporting infrastructure
- Default settings should favor lower-impact options (lower resolution, off-peak timing)
- Services should be designed to enable sharing and reduce waste
- Some services may not be worth their infrastructure cost regardless of efficiency
