# Characteristics and Evolution of Foundational Instructional Design Models

## Summary

This book chapter provides a comprehensive overview of instructional design (ID) models, tracing their historical evolution and identifying shared characteristics. The authors position instructional design within a systems theory framework, emphasizing that ID models emerged from the need to systematically develop effective instruction, particularly during and after World War II when military training demands drove innovation in educational methods.

The chapter focuses extensively on the ADDIE model (Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation), presenting it as the foundational framework from which most other ID models derive. The authors explain that ADDIE is not a specific model but rather a conceptual framework or acronym representing the core phases present in virtually all instructional design approaches. They trace ADDIE's origins to work at Florida State University in the 1970s for the U.S. Army.

The authors identify seven characteristics common to instructional design models: (1) learner-centered orientation, (2) goal-oriented focus, (3) focus on meaningful performance, (4) assumption that outcomes can be measured, (5) iterative and self-correcting nature, (6) empirical and replicable processes, and (7) team-based effort. The chapter includes visual representations of ID models in rectilinear, curvilinear, and waterfall formats, demonstrating how the same underlying process can be depicted differently.

## Key Concepts

- **ADDIE Model**: Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation—the foundational framework for instructional design
- **Systems Theory**: The theoretical foundation emphasizing interconnected components working toward common goals
- **Instructional Design**: The systematic process of developing educational experiences based on learning theory and instructional theory
- **Rectilinear Model**: Linear visual representation of ID processes with distinct sequential phases
- **Curvilinear Model**: Circular visual representation emphasizing iterative, cyclical nature of ID
- **Waterfall Model**: Cascading visual representation showing how phases flow into one another

## Theoretical Framework

The chapter is grounded in systems theory, which views instructional design as a system of interconnected components (inputs, processes, outputs, feedback) working toward defined goals. The authors also draw on behaviorist and cognitivist learning theories as historical influences on ID model development, while acknowledging constructivist influences on more recent models.

## Methods

This is a scholarly synthesis chapter rather than empirical research. The authors conduct a historical and conceptual analysis of instructional design models, drawing on primary sources, seminal ID texts, and the broader literature on educational technology and learning design.

## Main Arguments

1. ADDIE represents a generic process framework, not a specific prescriptive model
2. Most instructional design models share common characteristics derived from systems theory
3. The evolution of ID models reflects changes in learning theory (behaviorism to cognitivism to constructivism)
4. Visual representations of ID models (rectilinear, curvilinear, waterfall) reflect different emphases but describe similar underlying processes
5. Instructional design is inherently iterative and self-correcting, despite linear visual representations
6. Systematic approaches to instruction emerged from practical needs (military training) before being theorized academically
7. ID is fundamentally a team-based endeavor requiring diverse expertise

## Limitations & Critiques

- Heavy focus on ADDIE may underrepresent alternative approaches (rapid prototyping, agile ID, design thinking)
- Limited engagement with critiques of systematic ID as overly rigid or technicist
- Does not fully address how ID models apply to informal or self-directed learning contexts
- The military origins of ID are acknowledged but their implications for civilian education not critically examined
- Cultural assumptions embedded in Western ID models receive minimal attention
- Limited discussion of how digital technologies are transforming ID practice beyond traditional models
