# Towards a responsidble AI organizational maturity model

## Summary
Towards a Responsible AI Organizational Maturity Model AMY K HEGER, Microsoft, USA SAMIR PASSI, Microsoft, USA SHIPI DHANORKAR, Microsoft, USA ZOE KAHN, University of California, USA RUOTONG WANG, University of Washington, USA MIHAELA VORVOREANU, Microsoft, USA Artificial intelligence (AI) holds tremendous potential but also poses consequential risks Regulation frameworks like the EU AI Act aim to mitigate these risks, yet organizations struggle to understand and operationalize Responsible AI (RAI).

## Key Concepts
- RAI (Responsible AI) organizational maturity framework (RAI-OM)
- 24 dimensions across three categories: Organizational Foundations, Team Approach, RAI Practices
- Gap between RAI principles and on-the-ground practice
- Organizational barriers to RAI (incentive structures, lack of leadership support, technical solutionism)
- Interdependent nature of RAI factors requiring collaboration
- Need to start RAI early in AI lifecycle

## Theoretical Framework
Design science research, maturity model development frameworks (de Bruin et al.), sociotechnical systems theory, CSCW perspectives on collaborative work

## Methods
Two-step qualitative study: (1) 47 in-depth semi-structured interviews with RAI specialists, (2) 23 small-group and 17 individual co-design sessions (N=56) to refine framework. Phronetic iterative qualitative analysis combining inductive and deductive coding.

## Main Arguments
- Organizations struggle to operationalize RAI principles into practice; principles are necessary but insufficient without organizational change
- Current RAI efforts overemphasize technical solutions ("technological solutionism") while undervaluing sociotechnical and organizational factors
- Key organizational barriers include: lack of incentives for RAI work, absence of leadership buy-in, fear of punishment for raising concerns, insufficient resources and time, hierarchies of knowledge between disciplines
- RAI maturity requires addressing three interconnected categories: foundational organizational structures, team collaboration approaches, and specific RAI practices
- RAI tools and checklists have limited impact when implemented piecemeal without integration into workflows and organizational culture
- Reactive rather than proactive approach to RAI prevents systematic implementation
- Organizations need comprehensive understanding of "articulation work" required for RAI across its lifecycle

## Limitations & Critiques
- Most participants (82 of 90) from same global technology company, limiting generalizability
- Framework requires deployment and evaluation for usefulness, accuracy, validity
- Study focused on identifying dimensions rather than validating progression across maturity levels
- RAI-OM framework represents initial step toward full maturity model, not complete validated model
