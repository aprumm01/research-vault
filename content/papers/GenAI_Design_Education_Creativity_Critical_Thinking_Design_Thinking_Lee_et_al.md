# Generative AI for Design Education: Enhancing Creativity and Critical Thinking in Design Thinking Courses

## Summary
This study investigates how GenAI integration affects creativity and problem-solving in a semester-long design thinking course at the National University of Singapore, involving 65 engineering students across 14 teams working on a healthcare design challenge. Using a mixed-methods approach including surveys, workshops, in-class observations, and focus group interviews, the study finds that students perceive GenAI as a "creative catalyst" rather than a "creative creator"—effective for quantity-based divergent ideation but producing "one-dimensional" outputs that require human elaboration. The paper emphasizes the importance of verification literacy and ethical AI use, particularly in healthcare design contexts.

## Key Concepts
- **Creative Catalyst vs. Creative Creator**: The key student-generated distinction—GenAI is viewed as accelerating and inspiring creativity rather than originating it; students retain creative authorship but leverage AI for quantity and breadth
- **Quantity-Based Ideation**: The specific affordance students found most valuable—using GenAI during divergent design thinking phases to rapidly generate large numbers of idea variations before human curation and selection
- **Verification Literacy**: An emerging competency identified when students encountered fabricated GenAI results—the capacity to critically evaluate, fact-check, and detect errors or hallucinations in AI outputs
- **Double-Diamond Design Thinking**: The structured methodology used in the course (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver), providing a framework for mapping specific GenAI integration points across the design process
- **One-Dimensional AI Ideas**: Students' characterization of AI-generated ideas as lacking the contextual depth, emotional nuance, and stakeholder specificity that effective human-centered design requires
- **Visual Creativity Enhancement**: Finding that GenAI particularly benefited students without formal design training by enabling rapid visualization of concepts previously inaccessible to non-visual thinkers
- **Representation Ethics**: Ethical concerns unique to the healthcare context—AI-generated images of patients and care scenarios raised questions about bias, authenticity, and accurate representation of vulnerable populations

## Theoretical Framework
The study is grounded in design thinking as a human-centered, iterative methodology, with GenAI integration mapped across the double-diamond framework. The authors draw on recent empirical literature showing mixed effects of GenAI on creativity—including evidence that GenAI enhances individual creativity but reduces collective diversity of novel content (a tension central to team-based design work), and that unique AI personas can counteract diversity reduction. The paper engages with the tension between efficiency gains and educational depth, arguing that structured pedagogical support and explicit AI literacy instruction are prerequisites for meaningful GenAI integration.

## Methods
Semester-long mixed-methods study (January-April 2025) at NUS with 65 students from diverse engineering backgrounds (Computer Science 23.9%, Electrical Engineering 17.4%, Biomedical Engineering 13.0%, and others), divided into 14 teams working with healthcare industry partners on pediatric medical equipment design. Methods included: baseline surveys assessing initial AI attitudes and capabilities; structured GenAI workshops introducing tools and use cases; in-class observations during GenAI-supported design activities; and focus group interviews collecting reflective data on creativity, critical thinking, and ethical experiences.

## Main Arguments
- GenAI functions most effectively as a divergent-phase tool in design thinking, supporting quantity-based ideation when students face creative blocks or need to rapidly expand their solution space before human judgment and selection narrow the field
- Students without formal design backgrounds experienced disproportionate benefit from visual creativity support—GenAI enabled non-designers to produce quick visualizations that previously required extensive training, democratizing visual ideation but potentially masking skill development gaps
- Critical thinking development emerged through iterative engagement with AI outputs rather than passive acceptance—students learned to interrogate AI suggestions, identify what was missing, and elaborate toward human-centered solutions
- Verification literacy became an urgent competency when students encountered fabricated results (hallucinations) presented with AI's characteristic confident fluency, requiring explicit curriculum intervention rather than discovery learning
- The healthcare context amplified ethical dimensions: AI-generated storyboards depicting patients and caregivers raised questions about bias, representation, and authentic empathy that generic AI literacy frameworks fail to address
- Pedagogical support structures (structured workshops, explicit guidelines for each design phase, instructor facilitation) were essential—unstructured AI access led to either over-reliance or avoidance

## Limitations & Critiques
The study lacks a control condition (no-AI cohort), making it impossible to isolate the effects of GenAI integration from other course factors. The student population (engineering students in a design thinking course) is not representative of design education broadly—their different baseline visual skills and design experience affect how they interact with GenAI. The focus on student perception rather than objective creative output measures limits claims about actual creativity enhancement.

The healthcare context creates specific ethical dynamics that may not generalize to other design domains. The paper does not measure long-term retention of critical thinking skills or whether the verification literacy developed in this course transfers to other contexts.
