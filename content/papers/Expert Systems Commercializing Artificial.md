# Expert Systems Commercializing Artificial

## Summary
Guest editors’ introduction to special issue on the history of expert systems commercialization (1970s-2000). This issue documents the historical wave of expert systems AI in the 1980s through a May 2018 pioneer meeting at the Computer History Museum bringing together 20 pioneers who formed expert systems companies and conducted research during 1970-2000. Unlike today’s machine learning, expert systems used logical reasoning with "If, Then" rules representing domain expert knowledge. The issue includes articles from historians, industry newsletter editor Paul Harmon, and company founders including Doug Lenat (MCC/CyCorp), Peter Hart (Syntelligence), and Monte Zweben (Red Pepper/Blue Martini), examining why most companies did not grow significantly and why the industry declined abruptly despite initial promise.

## Key Concepts
- Expert systems - AI approach using logical reasoning to draw conclusions based on large sets of "If, Then" rules engineered to represent domain expert knowledge
- Commercialization wave (1980s) - period of expert systems commercialization by startups, private firms, and U.S. military contractors
- Knowledge-based systems - cognitive systems capturing expert knowledge and reasoning using decision rules and formulations
- Inference engines - components solving problems by processing knowledge bases in medicine, engineering, and business domains
- Lisp machines - specialized computers developed for AI programming using Lisp language
- Heuristic search programs - early AI approaches for problem-solving
- Industry decline - factors leading to demise of most expert systems companies despite early promise

## Theoretical Framework
**Historical Documentation Framework**: This editorial introduces a special issue employing multi-method historical research combining:
- Pioneer meetings with 20 industry founders and researchers
- Oral histories conducted by historians
- First-person accounts from company founders
- Analysis by computer historians
- Industry perspective from newsletter editor Paul Harmon

**Periodization**: Structures expert systems history across distinct phases:
1. 1955-1965: Early AI science (learning machines, neural networks, pattern recognition)
2. 1965-1980: Cognitive and knowledge-based systems development at Stanford, MIT, Carnegie Mellon
3. 1970s-1980s: Company formation and commercialization wave
4. 1990s-2000s: Industry decline and transition to machine learning
5. 2000s-present: Rise of machine learning and modern commercial AI

## Methods
**Documentary Approach**: Special issue based on Software Industry Special Interest Group (SI SIG) and Computer History Museum (CHM) two-day pioneer meeting (May 2018)

**Meeting Structure**: Eight sessions covering:
1. Purpose/structure and participant introductions
2. AI science & technology 1955-1965
3. Expert systems science & technology 1965-1980
4. Business reviews for initial companies (Machine Intelligence, Intelligenetics/Intellicorp, Inference, Advanced Decision Systems, AI Corp, Carnegie Group, Symantec, Teknowledge, MCC)
5. Business reviews for later companies (CyCorp, Syntelligence, Neuron Data, Franze Inc., Aion/Trinzic, IBM, Schlumberger)
6. Expert systems applications (problems addressed, methodologies for capturing knowledge bases)
7. Why expert systems industry declined (factors, survival strategies)
8. AI and machine learning in 1990s to present

**Participants**: 20 expert systems pioneers plus historians from University of Pennsylvania, Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, George Washington University, University of Wisconsin Milwaukee

**Documentation**: Video recordings, edited transcripts, and oral histories posted on CHM website (www.computerhistory.org)

**20-Year Effort**: 13th in series of SI SIG/CHM/Charles Babbage Institute pioneer meetings documenting software industry formation and growth in 20th century

## Main Arguments
- Today’s commercial AI (machine learning) has important historical precedents in the expert systems wave of the 1980s, providing lessons for understanding current AI commercialization
- Despite significant investment and attention, most expert systems companies did not achieve significant scale and the industry ended "somewhat abruptly" - understanding why remains important for contemporary AI development
- Expert systems technology, while declined as independent industry, continues to be "widely used by companies throughout the world" suggesting transformation rather than complete obsolescence
- The Stanford University research group led by Edward Feigenbaum was central to spawning numerous companies through graduate students and postdocs
- Historical documentation of expert systems commercialization was "underdocumented and underexamined" despite clear resonances with present AI moment
- Connection exists between expert systems story and contemporary machine learning development, warranting examination of technological and business continuities/discontinuities

## Limitations & Critiques
**Editorial Scope**: As guest editors’ introduction, does not present original research but frames special issue content

**Meeting Limitations**:
- Focus on successful pioneers who attended may underrepresent failed ventures or alternative perspectives
- Participant selection may create survivorship bias in historical narrative
- Two-day meeting format limits depth possible for comprehensive industry history
- Geographic concentration (many Stanford-connected ventures) may underrepresent other research centers’ contributions

**Historical Coverage**:
- Limited examination of why technology declined despite promise
- Brief treatment of transition period from expert systems to machine learning
- Focus on U.S. companies may miss international developments
- Technology-focused narrative may underexplore social, cultural, and economic contexts

**Documentation Gaps**: Editorial acknowledges history was "underdocumented and underexamined" - special issue aims to address but cannot fully resolve decades of limited documentation
