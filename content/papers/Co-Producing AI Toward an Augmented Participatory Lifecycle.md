# Co-Producing AI Toward an Augmented Participatory Lifecycle

## Summary
Despite efforts to mitigate the inherent risks and biases of artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms, these algorithms can disproportionately impact culturally marginalized groups A range of approaches has been proposed to address or reduce these risks, including the development of ethical guidelines and principles for responsible AI, as well as technical solutions that promote algorithmic fairness Drawing on design justice, expansive learning theory, and recent empirical work on participatory AI, we argue that mitigating these harms requires a fundamental re-architecture of the AI production pipeline.

## Key Concepts
- **Augmented participatory lifecycle**: Proposed framework for involving culturally marginalized communities throughout AI development, from problem definition through deployment and maintenance, not just consultation phases
- **Design justice principles**: Application of design justice values (community leadership, shared power, meaningful participation) to AI production pipelines to counter extractive research/development practices
- **Expansive learning theory**: Framework from activity theory emphasizing how collaborative knowledge creation can transform systemic practices and power relations in sociotechnical systems
- **Pipeline re-architecture**: Fundamental restructuring of AI development processes to center affected communities as co-producers rather than data subjects or end-users only
- **Disproportionate algorithmic impacts**: How AI systems' biases and risks concentrate harm on culturally marginalized groups despite universal deployment, requiring targeted participatory interventions

## Theoretical Framework
The paper synthesizes design justice (Costanza-Chock), expansive learning theory (Engeström), and participatory design traditions to argue for fundamental transformation of AI production. Design justice provides normative framework for who should lead and benefit from AI development. Expansive learning theory offers process model for how communities and developers can collaboratively reshape AI practices. Combines with critical algorithm studies examining how AI systems encode and amplify structural inequalities. Framework positions participation not as consultation but as shared power over technical decisions, resource allocation, and outcome evaluation.

## Methods
Conceptual/position paper synthesizing design justice literature, expansive learning theory, and empirical participatory AI research. Draws on prior studies of community-engaged AI development to illustrate principles. Likely includes case studies or examples demonstrating participatory AI approaches and their outcomes. Develops theoretical framework for expanded participation across AI lifecycle stages.

## Main Arguments
- **Ethical guidelines insufficient**: Technical fairness solutions and ethical principles fail to address root causes of algorithmic harm because they leave existing power structures and production pipelines intact
- **Current participation too narrow**: Typical "participatory AI" efforts limit community involvement to consultation or testing phases rather than shared decision-making throughout development lifecycle
- **Pipeline architecture encodes power**: Standard AI development processes (academic research labs, corporate teams, data extraction models) structurally exclude affected communities from meaningful influence
- **Expansive learning enables transformation**: Collaborative boundary-crossing work between communities and developers can generate new practices that fundamentally alter who controls AI development priorities
- **Design justice requires co-production**: True community leadership means shared authority over problem framing, technical choices, resource allocation, deployment decisions, and benefit distribution
- **Augmentation means amplifying community capacity**: Participatory lifecycle should build community technical literacy and institutional capacity, not just extract knowledge to improve AI products

## Limitations & Critiques
- **Implementation complexity**: Fundamental re-architecture of AI production requires resources, time, and institutional commitment far beyond typical project constraints; unclear how to scale participatory approaches
- **Romantic view of community**: Assumes communities have unified interests and perspectives; actual marginalized groups contain internal diversity, conflicts, and power dynamics that complicate "community voice"
- **Technical expertise questions**: Doesn't fully address how to bridge gaps between specialized ML knowledge and community expertise, or whether technical decisions require professional training
- **Resource asymmetries**: Power imbalances between well-funded AI organizations and under-resourced communities may persist despite participatory processes, limiting genuine shared authority
- **Tokenism risks**: "Co-production" label could legitimize superficial participation without real power transfer, becoming new form of extractive research with participatory branding
- **Measurable outcomes missing**: Limited concrete metrics for assessing whether augmented participatory approaches actually reduce algorithmic harms compared to conventional development
- **Generalizability concerns**: Examples may come from specific contexts (community organizations, academic research) that don't map onto commercial AI development at scale
- **Time-scale tensions**: Expansive learning requires extended engagement, but AI development cycles and market pressures demand rapid iteration, creating structural conflicts
