## Summary
Argues that HCI research aimed at supporting interaction design practice has largely failed because it borrows approaches and methods from science that are incommensurable with the unique complexity of design. Makes the case that design has its own distinct form of rigor—grounded in handling "wicked" problems—that must be understood and made explicit before research can genuinely support practitioners.

## Key Concepts
- Design complexity vs. scientific complexity
- Wicked problems
- Designerly ways of knowing
- HCI research-practice gap
- Design rigor and discipline

## Theoretical Framework
Draws on Cross (2001), Schön (1983), and design studies tradition; frames design as a unique human activity of inquiry and action with its own epistemology, not reducible to scientific problem-solving.

## Methods
Perspective/theoretical paper; comparative conceptual analysis of complexity in science vs. design; no original empirical data; critique of Rogers (2004) as empirical foundation.

## Main Arguments
- Design complexity is fundamentally different from scientific complexity—it is irreducible, contextual, and situational
- Borrowing scientific methods for design research is epistemologically incommensurable with design practice
- Design practice has its own rigor and discipline that can be made explicit and developed further
- HCI research must be grounded in a deep understanding of design practice to produce useful outcomes

## Limitations & Critiques
Theoretical/positional with no empirical validation; the science-design dichotomy may be overstated; many successful HCI contributions blur this boundary; the paper's normative claims may be difficult to operationalize for researchers trained in scientific traditions.
