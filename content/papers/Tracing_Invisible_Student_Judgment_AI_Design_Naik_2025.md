# Tracing the Invisible: Understanding Students' Judgment in AI-Supported Design Work

## Summary

This study examines how design students exercise judgment when working with AI tools in creative projects. Through analysis of 175 students across 35 teams in a graduate design course, researchers developed a taxonomy of six judgment categories that emerged when students integrated AI into their design workflows. The findings highlight the invisible intellectual labor students perform when working with generative AI.

## Key Concepts

- **Design Judgment**: Decisions designers make throughout the design process about quality, appropriateness, and direction
- **AI-Supported Design Work**: Creative practice involving generative AI tools as collaborators or resources
- **Invisible Labor**: The often-unrecognized cognitive and evaluative work involved in AI-mediated design
- **Human-AI Collaboration**: The dynamic interplay between designer expertise and AI capabilities
- **Calibration**: The ongoing process of aligning AI outputs with design intentions

## Theoretical Framework

Builds on scholarship of design judgment (Nelson & Stolterman), professional expertise, and human-AI interaction. Positions design judgment as critical mediator between AI capabilities and design quality, challenging narratives that AI simply "replaces" human creativity.

## Methods

- **Context**: Graduate design course with AI integration component
- **Participants**: 175 students in 35 teams
- **Data Sources**: Student project documentation, reflections, process artifacts
- **Analysis**: Qualitative coding to develop judgment taxonomy
- **Focus**: How students evaluated, refined, and integrated AI outputs

## Main Arguments

**Six Categories of Design Judgment Identified:**

1. **Reliability Judgment**: Assessing trustworthiness and accuracy of AI outputs; recognizing hallucinations or errors

2. **Appropriateness Judgment**: Evaluating fit between AI outputs and project context, user needs, and design goals

3. **Output Quality Judgment**: Assessing aesthetic, functional, and technical qualities of AI-generated content

4. **Calibration/Refinement Judgment**: Iteratively adjusting prompts, selecting from alternatives, and combining outputs to achieve desired results

5. **Ethical/Epistemic Judgment**: Considering implications of AI use including attribution, originality, and responsible practice

6. **Agency Distribution Judgment**: Deciding how to balance human vs. AI contributions; maintaining creative ownership

**Key Insights:**
- AI tools require significant human judgment to use effectively
- Students developed sophisticated strategies for evaluating AI outputs
- The "ease" of AI generation masks substantial intellectual labor
- Professional formation includes learning to work with AI critically

## Limitations

- Single educational context limits generalizability
- Self-reported data may not capture all judgment instances
- Rapidly evolving AI tools may shift judgment requirements
- Focus on student work rather than professional practice
- Short timeframe may not capture long-term judgment development
- No comparison with non-AI design processes
