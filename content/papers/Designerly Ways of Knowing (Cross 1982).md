## Summary
Nigel Cross argues that design constitutes a "third culture" alongside sciences and humanities, with its own distinct epistemology rooted in constructive problem-solving and abductive reasoning. He contends that design education must move beyond instrumental training toward developing intrinsic designerly values: the ability to model, represent, and synthesize solutions to ill-defined problems. The paper is foundational for framing design as a legitimate cognitive discipline.

## Key Concepts
- Designerly ways of knowing
- Third culture (design distinct from science and humanities)
- Abductive reasoning in design
- Ill-defined problems
- Design as a discipline

## Theoretical Framework
Drawing on Bruce Archer's earlier arguments, Cross builds a theory of design cognition grounded in the idea that designers develop a unique form of intelligence that transforms abstract situations into concrete artifacts.

## Methods
Theoretical and conceptual analysis drawing on design theory, philosophy of science, and educational theory.

## Main Arguments
- Design is neither science nor humanities but a distinct third area of human knowledge
- Designerly knowing involves reading and writing in "object languages" (visual, spatial, material)
- Design education should cultivate intrinsic values (judgment, reflection, creativity), not just technical skills
- Design cognition involves tacit knowledge that resists full articulation
