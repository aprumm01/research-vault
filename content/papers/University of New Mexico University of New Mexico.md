## Summary
This paper reports on an eight-week AI literacy cohort program designed for academic professionals (librarians, administrators, faculty support staff) at the University of New Mexico, examining how a structured, cohort-based approach to AI literacy development differs from self-directed learning and one-time workshops. The program was designed around practical AI use cases relevant to academic work, supported by peer learning, and included critical reflection components. Participants reported significant gains in AI confidence and competence, and the cohort model produced community effects beyond individual skill development.

## Key Concepts
- AI literacy program design (cohort model)
- Academic professional AI development
- Peer learning and community in AI literacy
- Practical AI skills for academic contexts
- Critical AI reflection and responsible use

## Theoretical Framework
Professional learning communities; adult learning theory (andragogy); AI literacy frameworks; draws on cohort-based professional development research to design and evaluate the program.

## Methods
Program evaluation study; pre/post assessment of AI literacy knowledge and confidence; qualitative data from participant reflections and interviews; analysis of cohort dynamics and peer learning effects.

## Main Arguments
- Cohort-based AI literacy programs produce more durable learning than isolated workshops or self-directed learning because they combine skill development with community building
- Academic professionals benefit from AI literacy programs tailored to their specific work contexts, not generic AI overviews
- Peer learning in cohorts addresses the social anxiety around AI tool use that self-directed learning cannot
- Critical AI reflection must be integrated into literacy programs to prevent uncritical adoption while developing practical competence

## Limitations & Critiques
Single institution program evaluation; self-reported confidence measures may not reflect actual competence development; the cohort model requires significant organizational investment; generalizability to other academic professional populations needs validation.
