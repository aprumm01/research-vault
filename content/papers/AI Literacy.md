# AI Literacy

## Summary
Lo's follow-up longitudinal study tracks how AI literacy among U.S. academic library employees evolved from 2023 to 2024, building on a prior baseline study. Using a TPACK-informed framework, the research finds meaningful improvement in AI understanding and increased adoption of generative AI tools, but reveals that training participation alone does not significantly improve literacy, whereas access to premium AI tools strongly correlates with better outcomes. Persistent barriers including cost, institutional resistance, and ethical uncertainty continue to disadvantage under-resourced institutions and early-career professionals.

## Key Concepts
- AI literacy in academic libraries
- TPACK framework (Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge)
- Premium tool access vs. training participation
- Institutional barriers to AI adoption
- Longitudinal professional development

## Theoretical Framework
The study adapts the TPACK framework (Mishra and Koehler, 2006) to the academic library context, treating AI literacy as the intersection of technological knowledge, pedagogical application, and content awareness. This frames AI competency as requiring integration across multiple knowledge domains rather than isolated skill acquisition.

## Methods
Longitudinal survey study comparing responses from 2023 and 2024. Quantitative analysis of changes in AI understanding, tool familiarity, training participation, and premium access. Statistical testing to assess correlations between training, tool access, and literacy outcomes.

## Main Arguments
- AI literacy among academic library employees improved measurably between 2023 and 2024, largely driven by generative AI tool adoption
- Access to premium AI tools is a stronger predictor of literacy growth than training alone
- Persistent structural barriers, particularly cost and institutional skepticism, create inequitable outcomes across library types
- Scenario-based training combined with equitable resource distribution is needed to meaningfully advance AI literacy

## Limitations & Critiques
The study is limited to U.S. academic libraries, and self-reported measures may overstate actual competency. The correlation between premium tool access and literacy cannot establish causation, and institutional variation in what constitutes "training" complicates cross-site comparisons.
