## Summary
This paper investigates how conversational AI assistants can support UX evaluation activities, examining what kinds of questions UX practitioners ask AI systems during evaluations and which question forms elicit useful responses. Through user studies with UX practitioners conducting AI-assisted evaluations, the authors identify categories of productive questions (heuristic interpretation, guideline application, pattern recognition) and unproductive ones (requiring contextual judgment, stakeholder knowledge, or domain-specific nuance). The paper provides practical guidance for UX practitioners integrating conversational AI into evaluation workflows.

## Key Concepts
- Conversational AI for UX evaluation
- Question formulation and prompt design for UX
- Heuristic evaluation with AI assistance
- AI limitations in contextual judgment tasks
- UX evaluation workflow AI integration

## Theoretical Framework
Cognitive task analysis of UX evaluation; human-AI collaboration in professional practice; usability evaluation methods; draws on Nielsen's heuristic evaluation and AI-assisted work literature.

## Methods
User studies with UX practitioners conducting heuristic evaluations with AI assistance; think-aloud protocols; analysis of question types and response quality; qualitative coding of AI interaction patterns.

## Main Arguments
- AI assistants are most useful for UX evaluation when practitioners ask questions that can be answered from general UX knowledge (heuristics, principles, patterns)
- Questions requiring contextual knowledge about users, business constraints, or organizational culture produce unreliable AI responses
- Effective AI-assisted UX evaluation requires practitioners to develop new prompt literacy skills specific to evaluation tasks
- AI assistance is best positioned as a first-pass tool that practitioners then refine using domain expertise and contextual knowledge

## Limitations & Critiques
Study uses specific AI tools at a point in time; results may change as AI capabilities evolve; the sample of UX practitioners may skew toward those already comfortable with AI; evaluation contexts studied may not represent the full range of UX evaluation types.
