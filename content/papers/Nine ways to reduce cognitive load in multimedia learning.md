# Nine ways to reduce cognitive load in multimedia learning

## Summary
EDUCATIONAL PSYCHOLOGIST, 38(1), 43–52 Copyright © 2003, Lawrence Erlbaum Associates, Inc WAYS TO REDUCE MAYER COGNITIVE AND MORENO LOAD Nine Ways to Reduce Cognitive Load in Multimedia Learning Richard E Mayer Department of Psychology University of California, Santa Barbara Roxana Moreno Educational Psychology Program University of New Mexico First, we propose a theory of multimedia learning based on the assumptions that humans possess separate systems for processing pictorial and verbal material (dual-channel assumption), each.

## Key Concepts
- Cognitive load theory in multimedia contexts
- Dual-channel processing (visual and verbal)
- Limited capacity of working memory
- Active processing and integration of information
- Extraneous cognitive load reduction strategies
- Essential processing and generative processing
- Modality effects in multimedia
- Spatial and temporal contiguity principles
- Redundancy and coherence in multimedia design

- Experimental research synthesis

- Empirical testing of multimedia design principles

## Main Arguments
- Humans possess separate channels for processing pictorial and verbal material
- Each channel has limited capacity requiring careful instructional design
- Extraneous cognitive load should be minimized to preserve capacity for learning
- Nine specific strategies can reduce cognitive load in multimedia learning
- Spatial contiguity: place related text and images near each other
- Temporal contiguity: present related narration and animation simultaneously
- Coherence: exclude extraneous material
- Modality: use narration rather than on-screen text with animation
- Redundancy: avoid presenting identical information in multiple formats
- Multimedia design significantly impacts learning effectiveness
- Evidence-based principles should guide multimedia instructional design

## Limitations & Critiques
- Primarily based on studies with novice learners, may not apply to experts
- Laboratory settings may not reflect authentic learning contexts
- Individual differences in cognitive processing not fully addressed
- Cultural variations in multimedia processing underexplored
- Some principles may conflict in complex instructional scenarios
- Limited guidance for emerging technologies and interactive media
- Does not fully account for motivational factors in multimedia learning
- Practical implementation challenges in real-world instructional design
- May oversimplify complex cognitive processes involved in learning
