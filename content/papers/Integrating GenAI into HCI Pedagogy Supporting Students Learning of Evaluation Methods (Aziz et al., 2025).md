# Integrating GenAI into HCI Pedagogy: Supporting Students' Learning of Evaluation Methods

## Summary
This paper presents an empirical classroom study exploring how generative AI can scaffold undergraduate HCI students' understanding of evaluation methods — an area that has received less attention than AI's role in ideation and content creation. The authors designed group activities where students compared their own evaluation suggestions with AI-generated ones, and used AI as a step-by-step facilitator during practical evaluation exercises (cognitive walkthrough, heuristic evaluation). Results showed that GenAI can deepen conceptual understanding and stimulate reflection, but raised concerns about oversimplification and reduced critical thinking.

## Key Concepts
- **GenAI as pedagogical scaffolding**: Using AI not to replace instruction but as a structured guide that supports multi-step reasoning through complex methodological tasks
- **HCI evaluation methods**: Techniques including Think Aloud, System Usability Scale (SUS), Cognitive Walkthrough, and Nielsen Heuristic Evaluation that are core HCI curriculum but cognitively demanding for students
- **Comparison-based reflection**: The pedagogical design of having students compare their own answers with AI suggestions to surface gaps in understanding and prompt critical thinking
- **AI as facilitator vs. AI as oracle**: The distinction between using AI to guide a process step-by-step (facilitator) versus asking AI to perform a task on your behalf (oracle) — both explored in this study
- **Flipped classroom**: The pedagogical structure used in the module, where theoretical content is covered prior to the session and class time focuses on application activities
- **Oversimplification risk**: The concern that AI explanations of complex methods may strip away important nuance, leaving students with superficially correct but inadequate understanding

## Theoretical Framework
The study is situated within HCI education literature and draws on pedagogical frameworks around scaffolded learning and reflective practice. The authors invoke Vygotsky-adjacent ideas about AI as a mediating tool in the zone of proximal development — offering structured support that enables students to engage with concepts they could not tackle alone. They also cite the design thinking and user-centered design literature to frame evaluation as a critical phase requiring methodological judgment, not just procedural execution.

The mixed-methods design (survey + open-ended qualitative responses) reflects pragmatic educational research conventions. The study does not claim to develop new theory but rather to contribute empirical evidence about a specific pedagogical integration approach in a real undergraduate HCI course.

## Methods
Mixed-methods study conducted in a 12-week third-year undergraduate HCI module at Newcastle University (UK), 2025/2026 academic year. The module had 74 registered students; post-activity surveys were completed by 46 students. Two types of activities were studied: (1) lecture group activities where students compared their evaluation suggestions with AI-generated answers, and (2) practical session activities where students used AI as a facilitator for cognitive walkthroughs and experimented with AI performing evaluations. Quantitative Likert-scale survey data captured perceived helpfulness; open-ended questions provided qualitative insight into attitudes and concerns.

## Main Arguments
- **GenAI helped students understand and compare evaluation methods**: Students reported that seeing AI-generated answers alongside their own helped them notice approaches they had missed and clarified the differences between methods, particularly in the lecture group activities.
- **AI-as-facilitator supported systematic evaluation practice**: In the cognitive walkthrough practical, providing a detailed structured prompt that positioned AI as a step-by-step guide helped students navigate a method they found abstract, improving engagement with the methodological process.
- **Concerns about trust and oversimplification were prominent**: Many students expressed uncertainty about whether AI responses were accurate or complete, and some worried that receiving pre-packaged answers reduced their need to think deeply about method selection.
- **AI is more effective as a complement than a replacement for instruction**: The study recommends that AI be positioned as a supplementary tool with explicit pedagogical scaffolding, not as a substitute for instructor explanation or peer discussion.
- **GenAI activities worked better in structured comparison mode than in autonomous evaluation mode**: The activities where students first formed their own answers before seeing AI suggestions were received more positively than activities where AI performed the evaluation on behalf of students.
- **Critical AI literacy is an emerging HCI education need**: The study underscores that students need explicit guidance on when to trust AI, how to assess AI output quality, and how to use AI reflectively rather than passively accepting outputs.

## Limitations & Critiques
The study involved a single module, one university, and one cohort of third-year UK computing students, limiting generalizability. The survey completion rate of 46/74 (62%) introduces potential self-selection bias. The study does not include a control condition (students who did not use GenAI), making it impossible to attribute learning gains specifically to the AI integration. Longer-term retention and transfer effects were not measured.

The paper acknowledges that the specific design of prompts heavily influenced outcomes — a less structured prompt might have produced very different results — making replication context-dependent. The concern about oversimplification is noted but not systematically investigated, remaining largely anecdotal from open-ended responses.
