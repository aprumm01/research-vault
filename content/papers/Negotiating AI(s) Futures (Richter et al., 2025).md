# Negotiating AI(s) Futures: Competing Imaginaries of AI by Stakeholders in the U.S., China, and Germany (Richter et al., 2025)

## Summary
Vanessa Richter, Christian Katzenbach (University of Bremen), and Jing Zeng (University of Zurich) present a comparative stakeholder interview study examining how AI imaginaries are negotiated across industry, government, academia, media, and civil society in the United States, China, and Germany. Drawing on semi-structured interviews with 10-15 stakeholders per country, combined with field surveys and longitudinal Twitter analysis, the study challenges the notion that national AI imaginaries are monolithic. Instead, it reveals complex, heterogeneous discursive processes concentrated at specific geographic hubs, with strong cross-national actor co-orientation. The paper proposes "public imaginaries" as a framework for understanding multi-stakeholder imaginary negotiation. Published in Journal of Science Communication 24(02)(2025)A08.

## Key Concepts
- Public imaginaries: "publicly constructed visions of (un)desirable socio-technical futures" that guide action, mobilize resources, and lay out trajectories for materializing or preventing those futures (building on Brause et al. 2023)
- Geographic AI hubs: imaginary negotiation is localized at specific centers (Berlin for German governance/NGO cluster; south Germany for industry; Silicon Valley; various US academic/policy centers) rather than diffuse nationally
- Cross-national co-orientation: strong networking and alignment between actors across countries -- particularly industry and technical actors -- despite national differences
- Sovereignty imaginary (Germany): European/German developing counter-imaginary focused on AI governance and technological sovereignty
- "Race for AI" imaginary (US): dominant frame connecting government and industry around global dominance; Silicon Valley adds "societal good" narrative to commercial interests
- Neutralizing counter-imaginary: shared between German and US academia/NGOs -- AI as tool requiring technological specificity, critiquing AI as marketing term

## Theoretical Framework
Sociotechnical imaginaries (Jasanoff 2015); public communication about technology (PCT); stakeholder typologies for tech discourse (Gorwa 2022); situational analysis (Clarke 2019); critical discourse analysis (Wodak 2015); builds on Mager & Katzenbach (2021) contested/commodified SIs framing; JCOM special issue on "Science Communication in the Age of Artificial Intelligence."

## Methods
Three-step methodological approach: (1) field survey with AI experts across relevant stakeholder sectors; (2) longitudinal analysis of Twitter posts by AI stakeholder accounts; (3) semi-structured interviews with AI experts from industry, government, academia, media, and civil society in US and Germany (10-15 per country); transcribed interviews analyzed using situational analysis and critical discourse analysis to map relational development of imaginaries across stakeholder groups; preliminary analysis focused specifically on the US and Germany (China analysis appears in Brause et al., the companion healthcare paper, not this one).

## Main Arguments
- AI imaginaries are not nationally monolithic: within-country variation between stakeholder sectors rivals cross-national differences; the same country hosts competing imaginaries held by different actor clusters with different geographic concentrations
- Germany shows a distinct sovereignty imaginary developing: Berlin-based government/NGO cluster focused on European AI regulation and technological sovereignty, while south-German industry cluster debates who should regulate; academia provides a neutralizing counter-imaginary of AI as tool in human control
- US shows a "race for AI" imaginary connecting government and industry around global political dominance, while Silicon Valley industry paradoxically emphasizes societal good and ethical responsibility despite clear commercial core; academia/NGOs share with Germany a technological specificity critique of AI as marketing term
- Imaginary negotiation is relational: strategic activities of stakeholders condense into widely shared SIs with societal implications; these imaginaries play a decisive role in shaping AI systems and their integration into social (dis)ordering in an era of profound automation

## Limitations & Critiques
Preliminary analysis focused on US and Germany (China presented in companion papers); sample of 10-15 stakeholders per country is relatively small for cross-national comparison; geographic clusters identified but their boundaries and stability require further empirical validation; longitudinal Twitter analysis is a proxy for stakeholder discourse, not a direct measure of imaginary formation; the "public imaginaries" framework is introduced but not fully formalized; published in JCOM which prioritizes science communication framing.
