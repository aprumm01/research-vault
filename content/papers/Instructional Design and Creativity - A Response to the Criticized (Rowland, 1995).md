# Instructional Design and Creativity: A Response to the Criticized

## Summary

This paper is a response to Walter Dick's critique of constructivist approaches to instructional design (ISD), engaging in a scholarly debate about the role of creativity in instructional systems design. Rowland, identifying with the "Situated Cognition/Constructivist/Anchored Instruction" perspective, addresses arguments made by those who defend traditional ISD methods and their supposed lack of "creative" attributes. The paper explores whether "creativeness" should dominate traditional criteria of effectiveness and efficiency, and whether ISD methods truly result in "creative instruction" when used appropriately.

The author systematically examines multiple assumptions underlying the debate between critics and defenders of ISD methods. He argues that the debate often reflects deeper epistemological differences between objectivist and constructivist worldviews about how knowledge is acquired and applied. Rowland contends that the term "creative instruction" is problematic because design itself is inherently about creating something new, and that ISD methods, when properly understood, can support creative outcomes rather than constrain them.

The paper emphasizes that while systematic ISD processes have value for well-defined problems, designers increasingly face ill-defined, "wicked" problems that require more flexible approaches. Rowland advocates for clearer distinctions between epistemological and methodological issues in design thinking, and calls for further research into determinacy, indeterminacy, and perceptions of definedness in design contexts.

## Key Concepts

- **Creativity in Design**: Designs are by definition creative as they initiate change and create something new; "creativeness" applied to design products focuses on elegance, uniqueness, and parsimony
- **Well-Defined vs. Ill-Defined Problems**: Well-defined problems allow for predictable solutions through systematic methods; ill-defined (wicked) problems require designers to work with uncertainty and propose alternative models
- **Objectivist vs. Constructivist Epistemology**: Objectivism assumes knowledge exists independently and can be transmitted; constructivism holds that knowledge is actively constructed by learners in context
- **Instructional Systems Design (ISD)**: Systematic approaches to creating instruction, often following models like Dick and Carey that emphasize analysis, strategy selection, and evaluation
- **Soft Systems Thinking**: An approach that treats systems models as relevant to debate about situations rather than as representations of reality

## Theoretical Framework

The paper operates within the broader discourse on design epistemology, drawing on constructivist learning theory and soft systems methodology. It references Buchanan's work on wicked problems, Senge's learning organization concepts, and the distinction between "hard" and "soft" systems thinking. The framework positions instructional design as a form of design practice that must grapple with fundamental questions about the nature of knowledge and problem-solving.

## Methods

Theoretical argumentation and response essay engaging with specific claims made in Dick's 1995 article. The author uses logical analysis, references to design literature, and reflection on professional practice to build counterarguments.

## Main Arguments

- "Creativeness" is not a separate criterion that should dominate effectiveness and efficiency; rather, it must be balanced with other criteria based on client needs
- The criticism that ISD methods lead to boring instruction conflates method with outcome; boring examples exist regardless of method used
- Design judgments involve both deterministic elements (for well-defined problems) and creative, exploratory elements (for ill-defined problems)
- The debate between critics and the criticized often stems from differing epistemological assumptions rather than methodological disagreements
- ISD methods may be most helpful in managing complex design processes rather than directly producing creative outputs
- Expert designers treat problems as ill-defined and work with reflective skepticism throughout the process

## Limitations & Critiques

Rowland acknowledges his limited expertise in constructivism and defers to other critics for detailed epistemological arguments. The paper focuses primarily on Western instructional design traditions and may not fully address cross-cultural perspectives. The debate format means arguments are necessarily tied to specific claims made by Dick, potentially limiting broader applicability. Further empirical research is needed to understand when and how different design approaches produce effective, creative outcomes.
