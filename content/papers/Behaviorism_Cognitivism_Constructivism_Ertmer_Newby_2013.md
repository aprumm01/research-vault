# Behaviorism, Cognitivism, Constructivism: Comparing Critical Features From an Instructional Design Perspective

## Summary

This foundational article compares three major learning theories - behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism - through the lens of instructional design practice. The authors address seven key questions about learning, memory, transfer, instruction types, and the role of learners, demonstrating how each theory answers these questions differently and providing guidance for when each approach is most appropriate.

## Key Concepts

- **Behaviorism**: Learning as observable behavior change through stimulus-response associations; knowledge exists externally
- **Cognitivism**: Learning as internal mental processing; mind as information processor with schema and memory structures
- **Constructivism**: Learning as meaning-making through experience; knowledge is personally constructed, not transmitted
- **Transfer**: Application of learned knowledge/skills to new situations (near vs. far transfer)
- **Instructional Strategies**: Methods aligned with theoretical assumptions about how learning occurs

## Theoretical Framework

The article organizes comparison around seven fundamental questions any learning theory must address:
1. How does learning occur?
2. What factors influence learning?
3. What is the role of memory?
4. How does transfer occur?
5. What types of learning are best explained?
6. What basic assumptions underlie the theory?
7. How should instruction be structured?

## Methods

This is a theoretical synthesis paper rather than an empirical study. The authors:
- Review foundational literature for each learning theory
- Systematically compare theories across seven dimensions
- Apply comparative analysis to instructional design practice
- Provide practical recommendations for theory application

## Main Arguments

1. **Theory Selection Matters**: Different learning theories are suited to different types of learning tasks
   - Behaviorism: Best for rote learning, procedural skills, basic facts (What)
   - Cognitivism: Best for concepts, principles, problem-solving procedures (How)
   - Constructivism: Best for complex, ill-defined problems requiring transfer (Why)

2. **Complementary Not Competing**: The three theories should be viewed as complementary resources rather than competing ideologies - effective instructional design draws from all three

3. **Learner Expertise Continuum**: 
   - Novices benefit from more behaviorist/cognitivist approaches with explicit structure
   - Experts benefit from constructivist approaches allowing self-direction

4. **Memory Models Differ**:
   - Behaviorism: Memory as hardwired S-R connections
   - Cognitivism: Memory as encoding, storage, retrieval processes
   - Constructivism: Memory as constructed understanding, always evolving

5. **Transfer Implications**: Each theory has different implications for how knowledge transfers to new contexts

## Limitations

- Presents theories as more distinct than they may be in practice
- Limited attention to sociocultural perspectives and situated learning
- Published originally in 1993 (updated 2013), so doesn't address more recent developments
- Simplified presentation may not capture nuances within each theoretical tradition
- Focus on individual learning, less attention to collaborative/social learning
- Western-centric theoretical framing
