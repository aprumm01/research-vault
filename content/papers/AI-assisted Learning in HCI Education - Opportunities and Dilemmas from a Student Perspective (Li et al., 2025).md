# AI-assisted Learning in HCI Education Opportunities and Dilemmas from a Student Perspective

## Summary
The use of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) tools has increased across all sectors in society, including human-computer interaction (HCI) education While previous research has explored the pedagogical applications of GenAI and the challenges faced by educators and students, there is less focus on GenAI in the context of a design education We present findings from group interviews and a survey study conducted with interaction design bachelor students, foregrounding the attitudes and perspectives of students who are motivated to learn.

## Key Concepts
- AI-assisted learning: Using GenAI tools to enhance, personalize, and streamline learning experience
- Student pride and intrinsic motivation to learn over externally imposed moral concerns
- Situatedness and specificity: Context-dependent appropriateness of AI tool use
- Chatbots (ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, Perplexity) for ideation, editing, research
- Text-to-image AI (Midjourney, Dall-E, Stable Diffusion) for visual creation
- Lack of exemplary practices from educators and industry
- Ethical awareness: bias, environmental impact, data privacy, dehumanization concerns
- Discrepancy between AI for learning vs. production in professional practice
- Student-led emergent practices outpacing educator guidance

## Theoretical Framework
- Constructivist learning theory: Students as active agents in knowledge construction
- Academic integrity discourse: Cheating vs. responsible tool use
- Professional practice integration: Alignment with UX/HCI industry trends
- Reflective learning: Self-awareness about appropriate tool use contexts
- Student agency and autonomy in technology adoption

## Methods
survey, interview

## Main Arguments
- Students guided more by intrinsic motivation to learn and pride in accomplishment than by moralistic rhetoric about cheating
- Context and specificity matter: appropriateness of AI use depends on learning goals, not blanket rules
- Students demonstrate nuanced understanding of when/how to use GenAI - make considered decisions about appropriate use
- Four key themes: (1) Learning and pride drive responsible use, (2) Situatedness and specificity determine appropriateness, (3) AI-assisted practices emerging for ideation, editing, research, exam prep, (4) Lack of exemplary practices from educators/industry
- Students aware of ethical issues (bias, environmental impact, privacy, dehumanization) and moderate usage accordingly
- Students leading AI integration while educators focus primarily on safeguarding assessment
- Need for dialogue and guidance from educators on how GenAI supports learning, not just prohibitions
- Discrepancy between GenAI for learning (puzzle through, learn from mistakes) vs. production (streamline, make efficient)
- Calls for collaborative learning between students and educators around AI-assisted practices
- Open conversation more productive than reductive dictates about acceptable use

## Limitations & Critiques
- Not representative sample - interviewees selected for high engagement and commitment rather than typical students
- Small sample size: 6 group interview participants, 25 survey respondents from single program
- Limited to interaction design bachelor students at one Swedish university - lacks broader disciplinary/geographic diversity
- Focus on motivated learners may not capture perspectives of less engaged or struggling students
- Snapshot of rapidly evolving field (Feb-March 2025) - findings may quickly become dated
- No longitudinal data on whether AI-assisted learning impacts actual skill development over time
- Survey primarily analyzed free-text comments with limited quantitative analysis
- Does not examine actual learning outcomes or academic performance related to AI use
- Self-reported data on AI usage may not reflect actual practices or may be subject to social desirability bias
- Limited examination of how different AI tools affect learning in domain-specific ways
- Does not address how to support less motivated students in developing responsible AI practices
- Acknowledges educators not at forefront - study captures student-led emergent practices without pedagogical intervention
