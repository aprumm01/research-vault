# How University Students Work on Assessment Tasks with Generative Artificial Intelligence: Matters of Judgement

## Summary
This qualitative multimodal study (n=26 Australian university students) uses a novel "scroll-back interview" approach to examine how students make judgements as they work with GenAI on assessment tasks. The authors identify six distinct categories of judgement events, ranging from active knowledge appraisal to submitting AI content without judgment. The study demonstrates that GenAI use shapes student learning in complex and sometimes troubling ways, and that students' judgements about GenAI are inextricably linked to judgements about their own knowledge and its limits.

## Key Concepts
- **Judgement Event**: A socioculturally bounded, holistic phenomenon comprising an interrelated series of choices and actions made by students during assessment tasks; characterised by temporal, material, and social dimensions galvanised by purpose
- **Scroll-Back Interview**: A novel qualitative method adapted from social media studies (Robards and Lincoln, 2017) in which interviews revisit students' historical AI interaction traces — scrolling back through chat logs with the student to ground questions in actual rather than reconstructed use
- **Six Judgement Categories**: (1) Making judgements about knowledge when working with GenAI; (2) Learning to judge GenAI through its limitations; (3) Relying on GenAI for things they could not otherwise do; (4) Adopting ideas with low levels of criticality; (5) Misjudging GenAI contributions as their own; (6) Submitting GenAI content without judging it
- **Meaning-Making Trajectories**: The process by which students come to tacitly or explicitly understand their judgements within events — not purely cognitive but mediated by material constraints and social context
- **Sociocultural View of Judgement**: Drawing on Säljö (2004), judgements about AI are not purely cognitive acts but are socially and contextually embedded — shaped by norms, uncertainty about institutional policies, and peer behaviour
- **Evaluative Judgement Deficit**: When students adopt or submit AI content without exercising quality appraisal, they fail to engage the evaluative judgement capacity that assessment is meant to develop
- **Knowledge Deficit Recognition**: Many students use GenAI specifically because they recognise gaps in their own knowledge, making GenAI use both a symptom and a response to incomplete learning

## Theoretical Framework
The paper's theoretical foundation draws on two interrelated frameworks. First, evaluative judgement theory (Tai et al., 2018; Bearman et al., 2024) which positions the capacity to judge quality as a core educational outcome — one that GenAI simultaneously challenges and offers new opportunities to develop. Second, a sociocultural paradigm in which "the critical and productive learning of how to use technologies is itself one of the most important socialising practices of modern education" (Säljö, 2004, p.493).

Judgement is conceptualised as an "event" following Esterhazy's (2018) notion of a feedback encounter, which expands the unit of analysis beyond isolated cognitive acts to include the whole constellation of activities, actors, and artefacts that organise student choices. Meaning-making trajectories — adapted from Esterhazy and Damşa (2019) — capture how students come to understand and commit to particular judgements over time, including tacit evaluations mediated by material affordances.

## Methods
Qualitative multimodal study. Twenty-six semi-structured interviews were conducted with Australian university students across multiple disciplines and years of study. The primary method was the "scroll-back interview": researchers asked students to share screens displaying their historical AI interaction logs (typically ChatGPT conversations), and interviews were structured around revisiting these actual traces rather than asking students to recall or reconstruct AI use. This produced richer, more grounded data than retrospective self-report. Data analysis used a narrative approach to identify judgement events and map their trajectories of meaning-making across six emergent categories.

## Main Arguments
- Students make a wide spectrum of judgements with GenAI, from active and critical (evaluating AI against their domain knowledge) to passive and uncritical (adopting ideas without appraisal, submitting AI output unchanged)
- GenAI use is strongly shaped by students' judgements about their own knowledge deficits: students who recognise they do not know something use AI to fill the gap, but this creates risk because they may lack the expertise to evaluate what AI provides
- A particularly concerning category is "misjudging GenAI contributions as their own" — students genuinely believing they authored content that was largely AI-generated, pointing to a form of source amnesia exacerbated by AI's fluent, plausible outputs
- The scroll-back interview method reveals that students' retrospective accounts of AI use are often incomplete or inaccurate compared to the actual logged interactions, highlighting the methodological importance of trace-based data
- Judgements about AI are not made in isolation but are embedded in students' social contexts — including uncertainty about institutional policies, peer norms, and the absence of explicit guidance from educators
- The study suggests that assessment design needs to explicitly create conditions for students to exercise, articulate, and develop their judgements in AI-assisted work, rather than hoping students will manage this autonomously

## Limitations & Critiques
The study is qualitative with a small purposive sample, limiting generalisability across student populations, disciplines, and institutional contexts. The scroll-back approach, while producing richer data, requires students to have retained AI interaction records, may introduce self-selection bias toward more reflective users, and may affect what students are willing to show. The six judgement categories are emergent and descriptive rather than comprehensive — the study does not claim to map all possible judgement types. The sociocultural framing, while appropriate, may underemphasise individual cognitive and metacognitive factors that also shape judgement.
