# Martin et al. 2009--HPT to a kayak company

## Summary
THE HPT MODEL APPLIED TO A KAYAK COMPANY’S REGISTRATION PROCESS Florence Martin Herman A Hall IV Amanda Blakely Matthew C Gayford Erin Gunter This case study describes the step-by-step application of the traditional human performance technology (HPT) model at a premier kayak company located on the coast of North Carolina.

## Key Concepts
- **HPT (Human Performance Technology) Model** - Systematic process to bridge gap between current and desired performance through organizational, environmental, gap, and cause analysis
- **Traditional ISPI HPT Process** - Structured framework with tripartite investigations (empirical, conceptual, technical) for visualizing and communicating performance improvement logic
- **Gilbert's PROBE Model** - Framework for identifying environmental and individual causes of performance gaps through systematic questioning
- **Information Systems (IS) Intervention** - Three-tiered approach: pure information systems (common database), decision support systems (information organization/comparison), transaction processing systems (location-independent ordering)
- **Environmental vs. Individual Causes** - Performance gaps primarily from inadequate tools (PayPal booking system) and lack of information (non-electronic single reservation book) rather than worker competence
- **Revenue Loss from Communication Breakdown** - Quantified financial impact ($4,249 in 2007) from misinformation, dissatisfaction, and scheduling errors
- **Information Blackout** - Gap between phone forwarding and manager receiving physical book where reservations cannot be processed

## Theoretical Framework
**ISPI Traditional HPT Model**: Five-phase systematic approach including (1) organizational analysis (vision, goals, values), (2) environmental analysis (interfaces, resources, constraints), (3) gap analysis (current vs. desired performance), (4) cause analysis (environmental and individual factors via Gilbert's PROBE), (5) intervention recommendation and implementation. Uses process mapping, fishbone diagrams for cause-effect relationships.

## Methods
Single-case study analysis of Premier Kayaks, a seasonal kayak tour/surf lesson business with 4 locations in coastal North Carolina. Step-by-step application of ISPI HPT model over one performance improvement cycle. Data sources: organizational documents (reservation book), process flows, revenue loss records (2007), stakeholder interviews (managers, employees). Analysis tools: process mapping, gap analysis charts, fishbone diagram, cost-benefit calculations.

## Main Arguments
- Performance problems often stem from environmental factors (inadequate tools, poor information systems) rather than individual worker deficiencies, requiring systemic interventions not training
- Single-point-of-failure systems (one physical reservation book) create cascading communication breakdowns affecting customer satisfaction, employee coordination, and revenue
- HPT model's effectiveness depends on ability to visualize and communicate process logic to clients, making it suitable for small business contexts beyond large organizational settings
- Information systems interventions should be tiered: basic shared database (Google Docs) for immediate improvement, decision support for scenario planning, transaction processing for location-independent operations
- Quantifying revenue loss from performance gaps ($4,249) provides concrete justification for intervention investment and establishes measurable success criteria (zero net loss goal)
- Service businesses require consistent information across all customer touchpoints (online, phone, in-person, affiliate locations); inconsistency damages professional reputation and customer loyalty
- Seasonal businesses face unique challenges: employee turnover, compressed operational timeframes, limited technology investment capacity requiring cost-effective solutions

## Limitations & Critiques
- Single case study limits generalizability beyond small seasonal service businesses; unclear if findings transfer to other organizational types or scales
- Analysis conducted after problem identification; no baseline data on performance before issues emerged, limiting before-after comparison
- Intervention recommendations not implemented or evaluated in paper; no evidence of actual effectiveness or unintended consequences of proposed solutions
- Heavy reliance on Google Docs as intervention assumes reliable internet connectivity across all locations (not verified for coastal North Carolina in 2009)
- Cost-benefit analysis incomplete; intervention costs (implementation time, training, ongoing maintenance) not compared to projected revenue recovery
- Gilbert's PROBE model application not fully detailed; unclear how specific questions led to environmental vs. individual cause determination
- Stakeholder perspectives limited to management view; employee and customer voices largely absent from analysis despite being key affected parties
- Does not address potential resistance to change from employees accustomed to informal scheduling or managers' concerns about distributed access/control
- Seasonal nature means 4-5 month implementation window; unclear how system would be maintained during 6-7 month off-season
- Affiliate locations (20% commission partners) identified as "breakdown" points but intervention focuses on internal systems rather than partner relationship management
