# Poets Over Quants Automation and AI Threats Increase the Value People Place on Creativity

## Summary
We examine how perceived automation and AI threats (the belief that advanced technology threatens humans’ career prospects) shape workers’ strategies for career preparation In nine studies (N = 2,320; three preregistered), we find that perceived automation threat drives people to prioritize creative skills over technical and social skills A pilot study revealed that people view creativity as less prone to automation and more likely to complement automation.

## Key Concepts
- Perceived automation threat as belief that advanced technology threatens career prospects
- Strategic skill prioritization in response to technological displacement concerns
- Creativity perceived as less automatable and more complementary to automation than technical skills
- Career preparation strategies shifting toward creative skill cultivation over technical competencies
- Differential vulnerability perceptions across skill domains (creative, technical, social)
- Generative AI's creative capabilities not diminishing prioritization of human creativity
- Labor market competition driving strategic human capital development decisions
- STEM students and creative professionals both increasing creative skill investment under threat

## Theoretical Framework
The research integrates threat response theory with human capital development to explain how workers strategically prioritize skills based on perceived automation risk. The framework posits that perceived automation threat triggers compensatory skill investment in domains viewed as complementary to rather than substitutable by technology. Drawing on complementarity theory from economics, the authors argue that workers assess which skills will maintain value alongside automation systems. The mechanism operates through: (1) threat perception triggering career security concerns, (2) assessment of skill vulnerability to automation, (3) strategic prioritization of domains perceived as automation-resistant, and (4) behavioral shifts toward creative skill cultivation and signaling.

experimental

## Main Arguments
- Perceived automation threat consistently drives workers to prioritize creative skills over technical and social competencies across multiple contexts
- People strategically view creativity as less automatable and more complementary to automation systems than other skill domains
- Automation threat leads to tangible behavioral changes including highlighting creativity in applications and seeking creative training opportunities
- STEM students and professional designers both respond to threat by cultivating creative abilities despite different baseline skill profiles
- Jobseekers increase interest in companies championing creativity when automation threat is salient
- Even when reminded of generative AI's creative capabilities, people continue prioritizing human creativity in career preparation
- The strategic response represents rational human capital investment based on complementarity logic rather than irrational fear

## Limitations & Critiques
- Self-report measures of skill prioritization may not fully capture actual behavior changes or long-term career investment
- Limited longitudinal data to assess whether strategic prioritization translates to sustained skill development efforts
- Studies primarily examine short-term responses rather than multi-year career trajectory shifts
- Unclear whether creativity prioritization represents effective strategy given rapidly advancing generative AI capabilities
- Sample predominantly educated workers who may have greater flexibility in skill development than precarious workers
- Does not examine structural constraints limiting access to creative skill training for different populations
- May overestimate creative skills' automation resistance given recent advances in large language models and image generation
- Limited exploration of whether perceived complementarity matches actual labor market demand patterns
