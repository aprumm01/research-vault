# Penn 2023 - Animo nullius - onAI’s origin story and a data colonial doctrine of discovery

## Summary
This paper traces elements of the theoretical origins of artificial intelligence to capitalism, not neurophysiology It considers efforts in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries to formalize a science of mental behaviour using the dynamics of social rather than neural phenomena I first revisit early American theorists’ controversial ambivalence toward neurophysiology, showing how this group benefited from post-war corporate and military investments in commercial and imperial expansion, which sustained and expanded their influence over the emerging field.

## Key Concepts
- Animo nullius - "nobody's mind" as colonial doctrine
- AI's theoretical origins in capitalism not neurophysiology
- Data colonialism and extraction
- Twentieth century formalization of mental behavior science
- Commercial and imperial expansion driving AI development
- Post-war corporate and military investments in AI
- Social dynamics vs neural phenomena in AI theory
- American theorists' ambivalence toward neurophysiology
- Doctrine of discovery applied to data and minds
- Historical political economy of AI

## Main Arguments
- AI's origins traced to capitalism and commercial imperialism, not scientific neuroscience
- Early American AI theorists benefited from post-war corporate/military funding for expansion
- Controversial ambivalence toward neurophysiology shaped field's development
- Formalization of mental behavior science used social rather than neural dynamics
- "Animo nullius" parallels "terra nullius" - claiming minds/data as unowned resources
- Colonial doctrine of discovery applied to mental and data domains
- Corporate and imperial interests sustained theorists' influence over emerging field
- AI development driven by extraction and control rather than understanding
- Data colonialism continues historical patterns of resource appropriation
- Origin story obscures political and economic foundations of AI

## Limitations & Critiques
- Historical focus primarily on American AI development, limited international scope
- May not fully account for genuine scientific motivations alongside economic ones
- Complex relationship between funding sources and theoretical directions requires nuance
- Does not provide detailed comparison with non-Western AI development
- Colonial framework may not capture all dynamics of AI development
- Limited discussion of resistance and alternative AI visions
- Temporal scope primarily twentieth century, contemporary developments less examined
- May underestimate role of neurophysiological research in some AI branches
- Practical implications for decolonizing AI not fully developed
- Relationship between critique and constructive alternatives could be stronger
