# Labels DO Matter! A Critique of AECT's Redefinition of the Field

**Authors:** Patrick Lowenthal, Brent G. Wilson

**Year:** 2010

## Overview of the Document

This article is authored by Patrick Lowenthal and Brent G. Wilson, writing from the University of Colorado Denver's School of Education and Human Development. At the time of publication, Lowenthal served as an Academic Technology Coordinator at CU Online while pursuing his doctorate in instructional design and technology, bringing a practitioner-scholar perspective informed by over a decade of teaching and designing instruction. Wilson held the position of professor of Information and Learning Technologies at UCD, with extensive research background in instructional design and learning technologies, having published four books and more than 100 papers in the field.

The document is a critical commentary piece published in TechTrends, a peer-reviewed journal affiliated with the Association for Educational Communications and Technology (AECT). This article type—scholarly critique responding to organizational policy decisions—situates it within the traditions of academic discourse about professional practice and disciplinary identity. The piece sits at the intersection of multiple fields: higher education administration, professional identity studies, organizational communication, applied linguistics, and the sociology of professions.

The significance of this work lies in its challenge to AECT's 2008 redefinition of the field, specifically the organization's decision to revert from "instructional technology" back to "educational technology" as the preferred terminology. This matters because AECT, as the authors note, "is the only group to systematically attempt to define the field over the years" (Lowenthal & Wilson, 2010, p. 84) through official definitions published in 1963, 1972, 1977, 1994, and 2008. The stakes are not merely semantic—the authors argue that terminology choices affect professional recognition, job prospects, program branding, and disciplinary coherence for practitioners and academics alike. As a veteran organization dating back to the early audio-visual movement, AECT's definitional authority carries weight that ripples through academic programs, professional practice, and public perception of the field.

## Research Overview

The central research question driving this work can be articulated as: How does AECT's choice to revert from "instructional technology" to "educational technology" as the field's official label align with (or contradict) the actual terminology preferences and practices of working professionals, academic programs, and employers? The authors frame their investigation with explicit skepticism: "We believe this recent change is problematic for a number of reasons, but primarily because of the weak rationale offered for the change" (Lowenthal & Wilson, 2010, p. 7).

The research was conducted by the two authors collaboratively, drawing on Lowenthal's practitioner experience and Wilson's extensive scholarly background. Their methodological approach combines systematic data collection with interpretive analysis of professional discourse. As they explain their position: "This change affects how external audiences view our profession and is likely to confuse practitioners in corporate and higher education settings in particular. We offer a review of job postings, program titles, and listserv discussions to support our case" (p. 7).

The data sources are diverse and practice-oriented. First, the authors systematically analyzed 327 professional job postings across six weeks from four major employment websites: the Chronicle of Higher Education, HigherEdJobs.com, AECT's job board, and Indiana University's Instructional Technology Job Board. They coded each posting for terminology used in job titles. Second, they analyzed the Curricula Data of Degree Programs in Educational Communications and Technology from AECT's website, examining 134 U.S. programs to identify patterns in degree names and program titles. Third, they conducted qualitative analysis of listserv discussions from ITFORUM and DEOS, searching archives using keywords like "Definition of the field" and "Labels used in the field." Fourth, they reviewed influential publications, drawing on Surry's survey of 700 foundational books in the field.

The methods employed are primarily descriptive statistics for the job posting and program title data, combined with interpretive discourse analysis of listserv conversations. The authors acknowledge this is "perhaps the least systematic" of their analyses (p. 638) but argue it captures important patterns in practitioner discourse. Key research concepts include professional branding—how organizations manage their public identity to maintain market position; communities of practice—how shared language constitutes professional identity within bounded groups (Wenger, 1999); and linguistic relativity—the principle that "language is intricately connected to thought and learning" (Vygotsky, 1962, 1978, as cited on p. 225) such that terminology changes can reshape professional identity and thinking.

A particularly important methodological note is the authors' positioning as insiders to the field who are critiquing their own professional organization. As they state: "We are not arguing necessarily for the adoption of one label over another... The key issue at hand, in our opinion, is not the move from instructional technology to educational technology but rather the method of decision-making and rationale for the change" (p. 736). This frames their critique as procedural and evidentiary rather than ideological.

## Theories of Knowledge

The article engages with several theoretical frameworks from sociolinguistics, organizational theory, and the sociology of professions, though these are often applied implicitly rather than formally theorized as research hypotheses. The primary theoretical foundation is **sociolinguistic theory** regarding the relationship between language and identity. Drawing heavily on Vygotsky (1962, 1978), the authors argue that "language is intricately connected to thought and learning" and that "language shapes thought and social practices, thought and social practices shape language" (Lowenthal & Wilson, 2010, p. 225). This Vygotskian framework positions language not as neutral labeling but as constitutive of professional identity and practice.

The authors extend this through **discourse theory** (Gee, 1996, 2000, 2002, 2003; Wenger, 1999), which understands professional communities as constituted through shared linguistic practices. As they explain: "We create ourselves and are created through the language we use" (Bruner, 1986, 1990, 2002; Gee, 2002, 2003; Vygotsky, 1962, as cited on p. 248). This theoretical lens emphasizes that changing field terminology isn't merely relabeling the same reality but potentially reshaping professional identity itself. The authors cite Wertsch (1991) to argue that "language plays a crucial role in an individual's inclusion within a specific environment or culture" (p. 258), suggesting that terminology changes can affect who feels included or excluded from the professional community.

**Branding and marketing theory** provides a second framework. The authors draw on organizational communication scholarship to argue that "connotations of labels have important impacts because people are drawn to certain names and repelled by others; hence the considerable investment in branding and promotion by marketing specialists" (p. 216). They note that "a brand is seen as a primary asset by an organization, reflecting years of investment in quality and promotion" and that "companies consider a change in a label only very reluctantly, understanding that the market may perceive a name change as a sign of trouble in the brand" (p. 219). This framework positions AECT's definitional authority as a form of brand management with professional consequences.

A third theoretical foundation comes from **the sociology of professions**, particularly regarding professional boundary work and jurisdictional claims. While not explicitly cited as such, the authors' analysis of how different labels connote different educational levels (K-12 vs. higher education vs. corporate training) and different professional roles (instructional designer vs. educational technologist vs. performance technologist) reflects scholarship on how professions establish and maintain boundaries through credential systems, specialized knowledge, and shared terminology (Abbott, 1988, not cited but conceptually present).

**Identity theory** from social psychology informs the discussion of multiple professional identities. The authors note that "theorists now agree that we each have multiple identities" and that "identity is not a static unchanging entity but rather a dynamic entity that is influenced by the communities of practice we are a part of and their associated uses of language" (Gee, 2003, as cited on p. 252). This framework explains why terminology changes matter differently to different subgroups within the field—K-12 educators, corporate trainers, higher education instructional designers, and academic researchers may prefer different labels that align with their specific professional identities.

Finally, the authors draw implicitly on **organizational theory** regarding institutional change and path dependence. They document AECT's history of name changes (from Department of Visual Instruction to Audiovisual Instruction to Audiovisual Communications to AECT) to establish that "throughout its history, AECT has continually changed with the times" (p. 669), positioning the current definitional debate within longer patterns of organizational adaptation. However, they argue that frequency of past changes doesn't justify insufficient rationale for current ones.

## Central Arguments

The central argument of this article is that AECT's 2008 decision to revert from "instructional technology" to "educational technology" as the field's official designation is problematic primarily because it lacks adequate justification and contradicts the actual terminology preferences evident in professional practice, academic programs, and employment markets. The authors state their position explicitly: "Disappointingly, the following is about the extent to which Januszewski and Molenda (2008) specifically address the change" (Lowenthal & Wilson, 2010, p. 123), before quoting a brief, vague rationale from the definition book.

This central thesis unfolds through several interconnected sub-arguments. First, the authors contend that **labels significantly affect professional perception and market positioning**. They argue: "How a professional organization labels a field can have far-reaching consequences both for members within the organization as well as those outside of it. Thus, changing a label of a field should be more than an academic exercise of picking the more general of two terms (that are sometimes used interchangeably)" (p. 181). They support this through marketing theory: "Connotations of labels have important impacts because people are drawn to certain names and repelled by others" (p. 216), noting that companies view brand changes as risky precisely because "the market may perceive a name change as a sign of trouble in the brand" (p. 219).

Second, they argue that **the empirical evidence shows "instructional technology" and "instructional design" dominate actual professional usage**, contradicting AECT's move to "educational technology." Of 327 job postings analyzed, only 9 used "educational technology" in the title, while 29 used "instructional technology" and 44 used "instructional design" (p. 342). Similarly, among academic programs listed in AECT's own database, 51 program names included "instructional technology" versus 34 with "educational technology" (p. 458). The authors emphasize that "neither the Chronicle of Higher Education nor HigherEdJobs.com labeled jobs in our field educational technology" (p. 278), with these major job boards instead categorizing the field under instructional technology or instructional design.

Third, the authors argue that **language shapes professional identity in ways that matter for inclusion and recognition**. Drawing on Vygotsky and Gee, they contend: "We create ourselves and are created through the language we use. Thus, a change in language use can bring with it changes in identity" (p. 248). They provide a vivid example: "one of us had an Associate Dean of Distance Learning tell him that she would not even consider hiring someone with a degree in Educational Technology because she believed they were not prepared the same way as graduates with a degree in Instructional Technology or Instructional Design" (p. 103). This demonstrates that terminology carries real professional consequences beyond mere semantics.

Fourth, the authors argue that **practitioner discourse reveals dissatisfaction with current labels but no consensus supporting "educational technology."** Their analysis of ITFORUM listserv discussions shows that "we did not find anyone advocating for the use of the label educational technology" (p. 506). Instead, practitioners express confusion about current terminology and debate alternatives like "learning design," "learning technology," or "performance technology," but the prevailing concern is that "educational technology" connotes K-12 focus or equipment management rather than instructional design work. One practitioner quoted states: "When I talk about educational technology, I get asked to wire classrooms" (p. 520).

Fifth, they contend that **AECT's rationale for the change is insufficient given its implications**. The authors identify two "tacit points" implicit in AECT's brief justification: "(1) One way, or perhaps the best way, to choose a label for our field and practice is by choosing the more general or broader label, and (2) Labels, or at least the label of educational technology or instructional technology, do not matter very much and the changing of these labels does not warrant a full rationale" (p. 166). They reject both premises, arguing that breadth alone doesn't determine appropriate terminology and that labels carry significant professional consequences requiring thorough justification.

Finally, the authors argue for **procedural reform in how AECT makes definitional decisions**: "Without input from practitioners and outsiders, and without a thorough and clear rationale for changes, changes are likely to have negative impacts as well as positive ones" (p. 739). They position their critique not as advocating for one specific label but as demanding better decision-making processes: "The key issue at hand, in our opinion, is not the move from instructional technology to educational technology but rather the method of decision-making and rationale for the change" (p. 736).

## Evidence

The authors provide multiple forms of empirical and rhetorical evidence to support their arguments, ranging from quantitative analysis of job postings to qualitative interpretation of professional discourse. This evidence can be organized into several categories: market-based evidence, academic program data, practitioner discourse, and theoretical framing.

**Evidence from Job Market Analysis**

The systematic analysis of 327 job postings provides the study's most robust quantitative evidence. The authors document that of these positions, "only 9 of those positions had the label educational technology in the job title. On the other hand, we did find that label instructional technology was used 29 times or more than 3 times as often as educational technology. Interestingly, instructional design was used the most often" (Lowenthal & Wilson, 2010, p. 381), appearing in 44 job titles. Table 1 in the article breaks this down by source: Indiana University's IT Job Board had 1 educational technology posting versus 9 instructional technology and 19 instructional design; AECT's own job board showed 0 educational technology versus 5 instructional technology and 1 instructional design; HigherEdJobs.com listed 4 educational technology versus 8 instructional technology and 9 instructional design; the Chronicle showed 4 versus 7 versus 15 respectively (p. 343).

Importantly, the authors note structural evidence from job board categories themselves: "neither the Chronicle of Higher Education nor HigherEdJobs.com labeled jobs in our field educational technology" (p. 278). The Chronicle's website required selecting from options including "Instruction design," "Instruction development," "Instructional technology," "Instructional technology education," and "Instructional technology/design" but not educational technology. Similarly, "if one searches on higheredjobs.com by type, our field is labeled as instructional technology and design" (p. 351). This suggests that major employment platforms serving higher education have standardized around "instructional" rather than "educational" terminology.

**Evidence from Academic Programs**

Analysis of the 134 U.S. programs listed in AECT's Curricula Data revealed that "20 of the programs used the label Educational Technology in the degree name, 29 used the label Instructional Technology, and 9 used the label Instructional Design or Instructional Design and Technology" (p. 450). Program names showed 34 with Educational Technology, 51 with Instructional Technology, and 13 with Instructional Design or Instructional Design and Technology (p. 458). The authors note the discrepancy between degree names and program names "is most likely due to the fact that as hard as it is to change the name of a program or department, it is even harder to change the name of a degree" (p. 461), suggesting institutional inertia preserves older terminology in formal degree designations while programs update their marketed names.

**Evidence from Practitioner Discourse**

The qualitative analysis of ITFORUM listserv discussions provides evidence of practitioner confusion and dissatisfaction with existing labels, though not consensus on alternatives. The authors quote multiple practitioners expressing frustration. One 2007 graduate student query captures the confusion: "What's the most efficient/marketable/current name for folks who do what we ID folks do? … [T]here are other graduate programs out there for Educational Technology, Instructional Technology, etc. I'm an instructional designer right now, but my boss asked me what I wanted on my new business card, and a quick search of the job boards shows all of the above titles" (DeFrias, 2007, as cited on p. 42).

Clark Quinn's response illustrates perceived limitations of existing terms: "Instructional and Educational are both so limiting, implying as they do only formal learning solutions (which can lump you into either the 'school' group or worse, the 'training' group, which gets no respect). However, Learning Technology, while somewhat better (can include more forms of learning than just education or instruction) still sort of misses the performance support category" (Quinn, 2007, as cited on p. 63). Another practitioner states: "When I talk about educational technology, I get asked to wire classrooms... When I use instructional technologist, I get blank looks (from, I think, confusion with Information Technology)" (p. 520).

Critically, the authors note: "we did not find anyone advocating for the use of the label educational technology" (p. 506) in the listserv archives they searched. This absence of support for the term AECT adopted represents negative evidence—the dog that didn't bark—suggesting the organizational decision diverges from practitioner preferences.

**Evidence from Publication Patterns**

The authors cite Surry's survey of 700 foundational books in the field, noting that "only one book, Trends & Issues in Educational Technology (Ely, 1989) had the label educational technology in the title. Surprisingly though, only two books had instructional technology in the title," while "there were over 19 books with instructional design in the title" (p. 636). This suggests that influential scholarly publications have gravitated toward "instructional design" terminology more than either "educational technology" or "instructional technology."

**Evidence from Real-World Professional Consequences**

Perhaps the most compelling evidence comes from documented cases of professional harm from terminology confusion. The authors report: "one of us had an Associate Dean of Distance Learning tell him that she would not even consider hiring someone with a degree in Educational Technology because she believed they were not prepared the same way as graduates with a degree in Instructional Technology or Instructional Design" (p. 103). This anecdote, while not statistically generalizable, demonstrates that terminology differences carry real stakes for job candidates and suggests that AECT's definitional authority doesn't necessarily shape how administrators and hiring managers categorize professional preparation.

**Limitations of the Evidence**

The authors acknowledge several methodological limitations. First, their publication analysis is "perhaps the least systematic" (p. 638) of their data collection efforts. Second, they recognize that "some of the information in the Curricula Data of Degree Programs could be outdated" (p. 466), potentially affecting the accuracy of their program name analysis. Third, their listserv analysis relies on keyword searches that may miss relevant discussions framed with different terminology. Fourth, the job posting sample, while substantial at 327 positions, represents only six weeks of postings and may not capture longer-term trends or seasonal variations in hiring patterns.

More fundamentally, the authors' evidence is primarily descriptive rather than causal—they document terminology patterns but cannot definitively prove that AECT's definitional authority should mirror rather than shape professional practice, nor can they rule out that "educational technology" might become more prevalent if AECT's institutional weight shifts usage patterns over time. The evidence shows current divergence between AECT's choice and market practice but doesn't settle whether AECT should lead or follow professional terminology trends.

## Conclusion

This critical commentary makes a significant contribution to ongoing debates about professional identity and terminology in instructional design and technology by systematically documenting the mismatch between AECT's 2008 adoption of "educational technology" and the actual terminology preferences evident in job markets, academic programs, and practitioner discourse. For a reader who needs to recall this work clearly six months from now, the essential takeaway is that professional terminology matters far more than mere semantics—it shapes hiring decisions, program branding, professional identity, and public recognition—and therefore organizational decisions to change field definitions require more substantial justification than AECT provided in 2008.

The work's primary contribution lies not in advocating for one specific label over another but in arguing for improved decision-making processes when professional organizations make definitional changes. The authors demonstrate through mixed-methods evidence that "instructional technology" and especially "instructional design" dominate actual professional usage far more than "educational technology," raising questions about AECT's rationale for reverting to the older term. The systematic job posting analysis (showing "educational technology" in only 9 of 327 job titles, versus 29 for "instructional technology" and 44 for "instructional design") provides particularly compelling evidence that the market has moved away from the terminology AECT chose to embrace.

The broader significance extends beyond this specific definitional debate to fundamental questions about how professional organizations maintain relevance and authority. The authors document AECT's history of organizational name changes and definitional revisions to position the 2008 change within longer patterns of adaptation, but they argue that frequency of past changes doesn't excuse insufficient justification for current ones. Their critique highlights tension between organizational interests (AECT retaining "educational" in its definition to match "educational" in its organizational name) and practitioner interests (professionals seeking terminology that accurately represents their work and enhances rather than hinders their marketability).

Several important tensions remain unresolved in this work. First, the authors acknowledge that "any label will unquestionably highlight some things while ignoring others" (p. 722) and agree "to some degree" with listserv participants who question whether deriving "one right label for our field" is productive (p. 727). This creates an uncomfortable tension with their critique of AECT—if perfect terminology doesn't exist, what standard should guide organizational definitional choices? The authors resolve this partially by focusing on process ("thorough and clear rationale") rather than outcome (the specific label chosen), but questions remain about what would constitute adequate justification for terminology that diverges from current market usage.

Second, the relationship between organizational leadership and professional practice remains ambiguous. Should AECT mirror the terminology that has emerged organically in job markets and academic programs, or should it use its institutional authority to shape professional discourse toward terminology it deems more inclusive or conceptually accurate? The authors' evidence demonstrates current divergence but doesn't definitively settle whether AECT should lead or follow. They note that international members may find "instructional" terminology problematic (p. 732), raising questions about whether global considerations might justify terminology choices that diverge from U.S. market preferences.

Third, the article leaves open questions about disciplinary boundaries and whether the field's ongoing identity crisis reflects deeper substantive fragmentation. The proliferation of alternative labels discussed on listservs—learning design, learning technology, performance technology, human performance technology—suggests that practitioners themselves disagree fundamentally about the field's scope and focus. The authors acknowledge this: "With the rise of online learning and the increased presence of competing, and at times more specialized professional organizations, AECT finds itself in many ways once again in an identity crisis" (p. 682). Whether any single organization can or should attempt to define a field with such diverse practitioners remains uncertain.

What remains open for future research includes empirical studies of how terminology affects actual hiring outcomes, career progression, and public recognition of the profession. The authors provide one anecdote about an administrator refusing to consider educational technology graduates, but systematic research on how different credentials are perceived by employers would strengthen the case for terminology's professional consequences. Additionally, longitudinal research tracking whether terminology patterns shift following AECT's definitional authority would clarify whether organizational definitions lead or follow professional practice.

The work also opens questions about alternative governance models for professional definitions. The authors conclude with Ely and Plomp's (2001) call for ongoing dialogue: "Where are the voices today? What are they saying? How has the field changed? How does it need to change?" (as cited on p. 749). This suggests that definitional authority might require more participatory processes involving practitioners, employers, students, and international stakeholders, not just academic terminology committees. However, the article doesn't propose specific mechanisms for such inclusive definition-making.

For scholars and practitioners in instructional design and technology, this article provides essential evidence that professional terminology significantly affects career prospects, program recognition, and disciplinary coherence. The enduring value lies not just in the specific 2008 definitional critique but in the broader argument that labels matter and that professional organizations bear responsibility for providing clear, evidence-based rationales when making terminology changes with far-reaching professional consequences.

## Discussion Questions

1. **Institutional Authority vs. Market Forces:** The authors demonstrate that "instructional technology" and "instructional design" dominate job postings and program names far more than "educational technology," yet AECT adopted the latter. Should professional organizations mirror terminology that emerges organically in practice, or should they use institutional authority to shape professional discourse toward terminology they deem conceptually superior? What criteria should guide this choice?

2. **The Limits of Linguistic Determinism:** The authors draw heavily on Vygotsky and Gee to argue that "we create ourselves and are created through the language we use" and that terminology changes can reshape professional identity. But how much causal force does terminology actually have compared to other factors like accreditation standards, curriculum content, professional skills, and institutional prestige? Could AECT's definitional choices be largely symbolic rather than materially consequential?

3. **Disciplinary Fragmentation vs. Umbrella Organizations:** The proliferation of competing labels (instructional design, learning design, performance technology, educational technology, learning technology) might indicate that the field has fragmented too much for any single organization to define it coherently. Should AECT embrace narrower focus to differentiate from ISTE, ISPI, ASTD, and other organizations, or does the field benefit from having an umbrella organization that attempts comprehensive definition even if practitioners increasingly identify with specialized sub-communities?

4. **Global vs. Local Terminology:** The authors note that "many have pointed out that word 'instructional' has negative connotations to international members and audiences" (p. 732), suggesting that U.S. market preferences for "instructional technology" may not generalize globally. Given AECT's international membership and influence, how should the organization weigh U.S. employment market evidence against international linguistic and cultural considerations? What would more globally inclusive definition-making processes look like?
