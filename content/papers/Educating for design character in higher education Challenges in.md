# Educating for design character in higher education Challenges in

## Summary
character versus focusing solely on skills, knowledge or the cognitive processes of our students In this paper, three authors with extensive combined experience in studio learning, teaching, and scholarship address these challenges via reflection on our own experiences of research and teaching and in-depth discussion with each other We adopt a co/autoethnographic approach (Coia & Taylor, 2009), identifying a range of challenges we have faced ourselves across three es.

## Key Concepts
- **Design Character**: Holistic perspective centering designer's full person (beliefs, experiences, judgment) beyond cognitive processes and technical skills
- **Studio Pedagogy**: Specialized learning environment requiring instructor roles as guides, supporters, and collaborators rather than hierarchical authorities
- **Whole Person Education**: Attending to students' capacities, interests, habits, and emotional dimensions rather than treating them as non-designers
- **Power Dynamics in Studio**: Complex relationships balancing assessment authority with collaborative partnership and mentorship roles
- **Productive Pain in Learning**: Recognizing and managing necessary discomfort in design learning while eliminating unproductive suffering
- **Individual Development Patterns**: Appreciating diverse paths to expertise rather than assuming uniform learning trajectories
- **Co/Autoethnography**: Collaborative reflection method examining teaching selves through iterative writing, discussion, and shared practice
- **Critique Practice**: Studio interchange requiring enormous skill to respond without designing for students while developing professional character

## Theoretical Framework
Nelson and Stolterman's philosophical perspective positioning designer character (not just cognition) at center of design activity. Draws on Dewey's emphasis on individual capacities and interests, contrasting with cognitive perspectives from Schön, Cross, and Lawson. Synthesizes design pedagogy literature with studio teaching experiences across instructional design, UX design, and interior design.

## Methods
Co/autoethnographic study involving three educators with 20+ combined years studio teaching experience. Reviewed previously published studies, conducted extended collaborative discussion through lens of pedagogical challenges, generated 12 top-level issues and 50+ related challenges. Iterative process of writing, reflection, talking, and feedback culminating in collaborative analysis and writing.

## Main Arguments
- **Character-centered teaching creates unique challenges**: Educating whole designers requires different approaches than teaching skills, knowledge, or cognitive processes alone
- **Power relationships are pedagogically significant**: Instructor-student dynamics are not complications but forces requiring active negotiation for educational purposes
- **Knowing students has ethical boundaries**: Deciding how much to know about students' personal lives requires constant judgment about exploitation vs. learning benefit
- **Productive pain requires vigilance**: Recognizing, acknowledging, and managing learning discomfort demands continuous examination of studio practices and assumptions
- **Individual differences foreshadow future designers**: Students' varied approaches legitimately differ and signal distinct professional identities rather than errors
- **Colleague differences are inevitable**: Whether from studio, non-studio, or different disciplinary backgrounds, negotiating pedagogical beliefs is essential work
- **Regulatory requirements constrain character development**: Accreditation standards limit time for knowing students individually beyond immediate project concerns
- **Instructor character matters fundamentally**: Once attending to design character, instructors must recognize and inhabit roles consistent with their values through reflexive practice

## Limitations & Critiques
- **Limited disciplinary scope**: Study focuses on three fields (instructional design, UX design, interior design); may not transfer to traditional architecture or fine arts
- **No resolution offered**: Authors raise challenges without prescriptive solutions, acknowledging context-specific responses require instructor judgment
- **Privileged practitioner perspective**: Focus on instructor challenges rather than student experiences or other stakeholder perspectives
- **Resource considerations underexplored**: Brief treatment of physical space, tools, and supports needed for character-centered studio teaching
- **Power imbalance undertheorized**: While acknowledging power dynamics, limited critical analysis of how authority shapes character development
- **Assessment tensions unresolved**: Tension between grading requirements and collaborative partnership roles not fully addressed
- **Scalability questions unexamined**: Time-intensive individual attention may be unsustainable in large programs or under resource constraints
- **Outcomes measurement absent**: No evidence presented for effectiveness of character-centered approaches versus skills-focused instruction
