# Computing the Nordic Way: The Swedish Labour Movement, Computers and Educational Imaginaries

## Summary

This historical analysis examines how digital technology has been framed as both problem and solution by Swedish reformist labour movement associations from the 1950s to 1990s. The article traces how non-formal adult education (folkbildning) became a key strategy for adapting citizens to computerization, revealing education as a tool for democratic governance and political control over technological change.

## Key Concepts

- **Educational Imaginaries**: Visions of how education relates to technological futures and societal transformation
- **Folkbildning**: Swedish non-formal adult education system tied to labour movement and democratic ideals
- **Problematisation**: How issues are constructed as problems requiring specific solutions
- **Sociotechnical Imaginaries**: Collectively held visions of desirable futures enabled by technology
- **Automation Discourse**: Narratives about automation as threat or opportunity

## Theoretical Framework

Draws on Carol Bacchi's "What's the Problem Represented to Be?" (WPR) policy analysis approach, examining how computers were problematized differently across historical periods. Also employs Science and Technology Studies (STS) concepts of sociotechnical imaginaries (Jasanoff) to understand education-technology relationships.

## Methods

- **Approach**: Historical discourse analysis
- **Sources**: 35 policy texts, 8 speeches/presentations, 30 newspapers/magazines, 6 course materials from three organizations:
  - Swedish Social Democratic Party (SAP)
  - Swedish Trade Union Confederation (LO)
  - Workers' Educational Association (ABF)
- **Time Period**: 1950s-1990s (five "swoops" through history)
- **Analysis**: Identifying problematizations and educational solutions across periods

## Main Arguments

**Five Historical Periods:**

1. **1950s - Leisure Time Explosion**: Automation framed as creating leisure time problem; education needed to help workers use free time meaningfully and adapt to constant change

2. **1960s - The Absent Future**: Promised automation revolution didn't materialize; critique emerged of computers as tools for management control (Taylorism); workers declared "We are not machines!"

3. **1970s - Master Computers**: Privacy concerns, worker surveillance fears; education campaigns to help citizens understand computer implications and develop critical awareness; Co-Determination Act (1977) gave workers right to participate in technology decisions

4. **1980s - Educate Everyone**: "Computing our way out of the crisis"; massive computer literacy campaigns; goal to train all citizens to prevent "lost generation"; computers as national competitiveness strategy

5. **1990s - Computers for Everyone**: Trade Union Computer initiative to provide workers with home computers; shift from education about computers to access to computers; eventual "PC reform" (tax subsidies for home computers)

**Key Insights:**
- Education consistently positioned as solution to technology "problems"
- Tension between adapting people to machines vs. adapting machines to people
- Labour movement paradox: promoting automation while protecting workers
- Education served both emancipatory and control functions

## Limitations

- Focus on Swedish context limits generalizability
- Labour movement perspective may not represent broader society
- Policy documents may not reflect actual educational practice
- Limited attention to how learners experienced these programs
- Stops at 1990s, doesn't address internet era transformations
- Single-country case limits comparative insights
