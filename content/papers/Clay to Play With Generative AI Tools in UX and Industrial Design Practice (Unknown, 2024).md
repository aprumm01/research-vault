# ”Clay to Play With” Generative AI Tools in UX and Industrial Design Practice

## Summary
1 Generative artifcial intelligence (GAI) is transforming numerous professions, not least various felds intimately relying on creativity, such as design To explore GAI’s adoption and appropriation in design, an interview-based study probed 10 specialists in user experience and industrial design, with varying tenure and GAI experience, for their adoption/application of GAI tools, reasons for not using them, problems with ownership and agency, speculations about the future of creative work, and GAI tools’ roles in design.

## Key Concepts
- **GAI adoption patterns in design practice**: Examination of how UX and industrial designers integrate generative AI tools into professional workflows, including motivations, barriers, and usage contexts
- **Clay metaphor for creative tools**: Conceptualizing GAI tools as malleable creative material that designers can shape and experiment with, contrasting with traditional rigid software tools
- **Ownership and agency tensions**: Professional concerns about authorship, intellectual property, and creative control when using AI-generated outputs in client-facing design work
- **Experience-based appropriation**: How designers with varying tenure and GAI familiarity develop different adoption strategies and tool appropriation practices
- **Creative work futures speculation**: Professional designers' projections about how GAI will reshape design labor, skill requirements, and value propositions in UX and industrial design fields

## Theoretical Framework
The study appears grounded in technology adoption and appropriation frameworks from HCI and design research, examining how professional designers selectively integrate, resist, or transform GAI tools within established creative practices. The "clay" metaphor suggests connections to material-centered design thinking and theories of creative media as malleable substances. The framework likely considers sociotechnical perspectives on how new technologies reshape professional identities, work processes, and creative agency.

## Methods
Interview study with N=10 specialists in UX and industrial design, with varying professional tenure and GAI experience levels. Semi-structured interviews explored GAI tool adoption/application patterns, reasons for non-use, concerns about ownership and agency, speculations about creative work futures, and perceived roles of GAI in design processes. Data analyzed through thematic analysis to identify patterns across experience levels and design domains.

## Main Arguments
- **GAI as experimental creative material**: Designers conceptualize generative AI tools as "clay to play with"—malleable creative material for exploration rather than deterministic production tools, emphasizing experimentation over finalized outputs
- **Selective appropriation based on task fit**: Professional designers strategically adopt GAI for specific tasks (ideation, variation generation, inspiration) while maintaining traditional methods for client deliverables, reflecting nuanced tool appropriation
- **Ownership creates professional hesitation**: Concerns about intellectual property, client expectations, and professional credibility create barriers to GAI adoption in client-facing work, even when designers find tools personally useful
- **Experience shapes adoption trajectories**: Designers with longer professional tenure show more cautious, critical GAI adoption compared to those earlier in careers, suggesting experience-based differences in technology integration
- **Uncertain futures drive mixed speculation**: Professionals express both excitement about creative possibilities and anxiety about skill devaluation, job displacement, and changing professional identities in AI-augmented design futures
- **Agency preservation as core concern**: Designers emphasize maintaining creative control and decision-making authority when using GAI, resisting tools that feel like they replace rather than augment human judgment

## Limitations & Critiques
- **Small sample size**: N=10 participants may not capture full diversity of UX and industrial design practices across different organizational contexts, geographic locations, or design specializations
- **Snapshot of rapidly evolving landscape**: Interview-based study captures attitudes at specific moment in time; GAI tools and professional adoption patterns evolving rapidly may quickly outdated findings
- **Self-selection bias**: Participants willing to discuss GAI likely more engaged (positively or negatively) with technology than designers who haven't formed strong opinions, potentially missing neutral perspectives
- **Limited observational data**: Reliance on interview self-reports rather than observational studies of actual GAI use in practice may not capture tacit behaviors, unconscious biases, or gaps between stated and enacted practices
- **Undefined tool specificity**: Unclear which specific GAI tools (Midjourney, DALL-E, ChatGPT, etc.) participants used; different tools with different capabilities may produce different adoption patterns
- **Missing organizational context**: Limited attention to how workplace policies, team dynamics, client expectations, or organizational cultures shape individual designers' GAI adoption decisions
- **Ownership framework underdeveloped**: While identifying ownership concerns, limited analysis of legal frameworks, industry standards, or practical strategies designers use to navigate intellectual property questions
- **Future speculation limitations**: Professional predictions about AI impact on creative work may reflect current anxieties more than accurate foresight; actual futures may diverge significantly from current speculation
