# Conceptions of Design Practice From Academy to Industry: Implications for HCI and Design Education

## Summary
This paper investigates the gap between how design practice is conceptualized in academic design education versus how it is enacted in professional industry contexts. Through interviews with practitioners and analysis of design education curricula, the authors find that academic design education tends to emphasize process, reflection, and conceptual development, while industry contexts emphasize speed, constraint satisfaction, and iterative user feedback. The paper argues for design education that explicitly bridges these different conceptions, preparing students for the transition without abandoning the intellectual rigor of academic design.

## Key Concepts
- Design practice conceptions (academic vs. industry)
- Design education-practice gap
- Tacit knowledge transfer from education to practice
- Professional socialization in design
- Studio learning vs. industry work contexts

## Theoretical Framework
Sociocultural learning theory; communities of practice (Lave & Wenger); occupational identity formation; draws on design education research and professional practice literature.

## Methods
Qualitative study; semi-structured interviews with design educators and design practitioners; comparative analysis of academic and industry design work expectations.

## Main Arguments
- Academic and industry conceptions of design practice emphasize different values and timelines, creating a transition gap for graduates
- Industry practitioners report that graduates need stronger skills in communicating design rationale and navigating client/stakeholder relationships
- Academic education's emphasis on reflection and process is valuable but requires complementary exposure to constraint-driven, collaborative practice
- Design programs that include authentic industry collaboration better prepare students for the transition

## Limitations & Critiques
Sample limited to a single national context; the academic-industry binary may overstate the homogeneity of each context; findings may be more applicable to product and interaction design than to other design disciplines.
