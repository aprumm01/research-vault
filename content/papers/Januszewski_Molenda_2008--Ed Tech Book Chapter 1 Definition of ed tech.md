# Definition (Chapter 1 from Educational Technology: A Definition with Commentary)

## Summary

This chapter presents the 2008 AECT definition of educational technology, building upon the organization's previous definitions from 1963 and 1994. The authors propose that educational technology is "the study and ethical practice of facilitating learning and improving performance by creating, using, and managing appropriate technological processes and resources." This definition represents a significant shift from earlier conceptions that emphasized instruction and control toward a view that centers on facilitating learning and recognizes the learner as an active constructor of knowledge.

The chapter systematically unpacks each element of the definition, explaining how terms like "study," "ethical practice," "facilitating," "learning," "improving," "performance," "creating," "using," "managing," "appropriate," "technological," "processes," and "resources" carry specific meanings within the field. The authors emphasize that the definition is tentative and subject to evolution, reflecting the dynamic nature of the field as it responds to new technologies and theoretical developments in learning theory.

A central theme is the shift from behaviorist to constructivist orientations in the field, moving from designing instruction to be "delivered" toward creating learning environments where learners actively participate in constructing their own understanding. The definition also makes an explicit commitment to ethical practice, recognizing that educational technologists must consider issues of inclusion, empowerment, and the broader societal good alongside traditional concerns of efficiency and effectiveness.

## Key Concepts

- **Educational Technology**: The study and ethical practice of facilitating learning and improving performance by creating, using, and managing appropriate technological processes and resources.
- **Study**: Encompasses research, reflective practice, and all forms of disciplined inquiry including quantitative/qualitative research, theorizing, philosophical analysis, and evaluation.
- **Ethical Practice**: A commitment to professional standards addressing concerns about individuals, society, and the profession; questioning assumptions about efficacy and power dynamics.
- **Facilitating**: A shift from controlling learning to creating environments where learning can more easily occur; recognizing the learner as constructor rather than recipient of knowledge.
- **Learning**: Distinguished from mere retention of information; emphasizes acquisition of knowledge, skills, and attitudes that transfer beyond the classroom.
- **Performance**: The learner's ability to use and apply new capabilities gained; distinguishes "inert knowledge" from usable capability.
- **Creating**: The generation of instructional materials, learning environments, and teaching-learning systems through various design approaches.
- **Using**: Bringing learners into contact with learning conditions and resources; includes selection, utilization, diffusion, integration, and institutionalization.
- **Managing**: Project management, personnel management, information management, and program evaluation within educational technology contexts.
- **Appropriate**: Suitability for intended purposes; ethically sound and likely to yield results; sustainability for local contexts.
- **Technological**: The systematic application of scientific or organized knowledge to practical tasks; distinguishes the field from general education.
- **Processes**: Series of activities directed toward specified results; includes systematic instructional development approaches.
- **Resources**: People, tools, technologies, and materials designed to help learners; includes digital media, community resources, and traditional materials.

## Theoretical Framework

The chapter reflects a paradigm shift from behaviorist to cognitive and constructivist learning theories. Earlier AECT definitions (1963, 1994) implied more direct cause-and-effect relationships between instruction and learning. The 2008 definition acknowledges constructivist influences that recognize learners as active constructors of knowledge who gain ownership through their own meaning-making processes. The field has shifted from designing instruction to be "delivered" toward creating learning environments that support exploration and meaningful understanding. The authors also situate educational technology within the broader context of human performance technology (HPT), noting that while HPT encompasses all workplace interventions, educational technology focuses specifically on learning-related solutions.

## Methods

This is a conceptual/definitional chapter rather than an empirical study. The methodology involves philosophical analysis, historical review of prior AECT definitions (1963, 1994), synthesis of theoretical developments in learning theory and instructional design, and consensus-building through the AECT Definition and Terminology Committee.

## Main Arguments

- Educational technology should be understood as both a concept (theoretical construct) and a field of practice, each with different validity criteria
- The shift from "instruction" to "learning" reflects fundamental theoretical changes in understanding how knowledge is acquired
- Ethical practice is not merely rule-following but an approach that questions assumptions about efficacy, power, inclusion, and social good
- Technology in this context refers to systematic application of knowledge, not just hardware or media
- The field must continually update its definition as technologies evolve and theoretical understanding deepens
- Educational technology is a subset of human performance technology, focusing specifically on learning interventions
- "Facilitating" rather than "causing" learning represents appropriate professional humility about the indirect relationship between instruction and learning outcomes
- Appropriateness includes both effectiveness criteria and ethical considerations of sustainability, cultural fit, and societal benefit

## Limitations & Critiques

The authors acknowledge that the definition is "tentative" and "subject to further reconsideration over time." There is an inherent tension between the systematic instructional development tradition (with its "plan-and-control" sensibility) and constructivist approaches (with more "free-form" orientations), which remains unresolved. The definition may be criticized for being broad enough to blur boundaries with general education and curriculum studies. Additionally, while the chapter addresses ethical dimensions, some critical theorists might argue it does not go far enough in questioning power structures embedded in educational technology practice. The focus remains primarily on North American contexts and AECT's organizational perspective on the field.
