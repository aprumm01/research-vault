## Summary
Baumer et al.'s paper is a foundational contribution to understanding how and why people resist, reject, or develop oppositional relationships with AI technologies. Moving beyond adoption/non-adoption binary, the paper develops a taxonomy of oppositional AI responses — from principled resistance rooted in values to pragmatic non-use based on perceived utility — and examines the conditions under which each type emerges. The work is important for HCI and AI ethics because it frames resistance as legitimate, potentially valuable, and worthy of design accommodation rather than a failure of adoption.

## Key Concepts
- AI resistance and non-use as legitimate responses
- Taxonomy of oppositional AI responses
- Principled resistance (value-based) vs. pragmatic non-use
- Design for resisters
- Agency and technology refusal

## Theoretical Framework
Technology non-use studies; science and technology studies (STS); critical AI studies; draws on Selwyn's work on technology non-use and Wyatt's technology refusal typology to develop AI-specific resistance framework.

## Methods
Conceptual framework development; interviews and case analysis of people who actively resist or avoid AI technologies; taxonomy construction from empirical and theoretical data.

## Main Arguments
- AI resistance is not simply adoption failure but a complex, often principled response worthy of analysis and design consideration
- Different types of resistance reflect different values and concerns: privacy resistance, autonomy resistance, identity resistance, and practical non-use are distinct phenomena
- Treating all resistance as a problem to be overcome misses the legitimate concerns that drive it
- HCI and AI design should create space for meaningful AI refusal and opt-out rather than designing systems that foreclose non-use options

## Limitations & Critiques
The taxonomy requires empirical validation across diverse AI contexts; the normative argument for designing for resisters may be in tension with organizational incentives; resistance itself may change as AI becomes more embedded in essential services.
