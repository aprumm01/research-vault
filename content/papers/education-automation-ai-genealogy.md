# Education, Automation and AI: A Genealogy of Alternative Imaginations

## Summary

This article employs Foucauldian genealogical analysis to examine how education has been conceptualized in relation to automation and artificial intelligence across different historical periods. Using Sweden as a case study, Rahm identifies and analyzes four distinct "educational imaginaries" from 1955 to 1997, revealing how expectations about technological unemployment and AI have shaped educational policy discourse.

The study identifies four imaginaries: (1) the "push-button imaginary" of the 1950s focused on leisure and liberation from work; (2) the "full employment imaginary" of the 1960s-70s emphasizing education for economic adaptation; (3) the "entrepreneurial imaginary" of the 1980s celebrating innovation and individual initiative; and (4) the "lifelong learning imaginary" of the 1990s positioning continuous education as essential for navigating technological change.

Rahm argues that these imaginaries are not simply neutral forecasts but performative discourses that shape policy and social expectations. Drawing on the concept of sociotechnical imaginaries from STS (Science and Technology Studies), the author shows how visions of automated futures have consistently positioned education as the primary solution to technological disruption—a pattern with significant implications for contemporary AI policy debates.

## Key Concepts

- **Foucauldian Genealogy**: Method of historical analysis that traces how present concepts emerged from contingent historical conditions rather than inevitable progress
- **Educational Imaginaries**: Collective visions of how education relates to technological and social futures
- **Sociotechnical Imaginaries**: Collectively held visions of desirable futures attainable through science and technology (Jasanoff & Kim)
- **Technological Unemployment**: The displacement of workers by automation and AI
- **Push-Button Society**: 1950s vision of automated abundance creating leisure time
- **Lifelong Learning**: Policy framework positioning continuous education as individual responsibility for employability

## Theoretical Framework

The study combines Foucauldian discourse analysis with the concept of sociotechnical imaginaries from Science and Technology Studies (STS). Rahm draws on Jasanoff and Kim's framework of nationally specific imaginaries while adapting it to examine education-automation relationships. The genealogical method emphasizes discontinuity and contingency, resisting teleological narratives of technological progress.

## Methods

- **Approach**: Foucauldian genealogical analysis
- **Data Sources**: Swedish policy documents, commission reports, and public discourse from 1955-1997
- **Case Selection**: Sweden chosen for its strong education-welfare state tradition and extensive documentation
- **Analysis**: Identification of ruptures, discontinuities, and emergent discursive formations across four historical periods

## Main Arguments

1. Education has consistently been positioned as the solution to technological unemployment across different historical periods
2. Educational imaginaries are performative—they shape policy and social arrangements, not just reflect them
3. The relationship between education and automation is not natural or inevitable but historically constructed
4. Contemporary AI discourse recycles many themes from earlier automation debates
5. National contexts (Sweden's welfare state tradition) shape how imaginaries develop and are institutionalized
6. The shift from collective to individual responsibility for adaptation to technological change represents a significant discursive transformation
7. Genealogical analysis reveals alternatives that were foreclosed, suggesting current arrangements are not inevitable

## Limitations & Critiques

- Single-country focus (Sweden) limits generalizability to other national contexts
- Heavy reliance on official policy documents may miss grassroots or counter-discourses
- The periodization into four imaginaries may impose artificial boundaries on more fluid discursive changes
- Limited attention to how imaginaries were contested or resisted by educators, workers, or other groups
- Does not extend analysis to post-1997 period when AI discourse accelerated significantly
- The Foucauldian framework, while generative, may prioritize discourse over material conditions and political economy
- Connection between Swedish imaginaries and actual policy implementation could be further developed
