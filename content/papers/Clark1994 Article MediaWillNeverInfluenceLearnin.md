# Clark1994 Article MediaWillNeverInfluenceLearnin

## Overview of the Document

This paper was authored by Richard E. Clark, Professor and Chair of the Division of Educational Psychology and Technology and Director of Professional Studies in the School of Education at the University of Southern California. The document was published in 1994 in Educational Technology Research & Development (ETR&D), Volume 42, Number 2, pages 21-29. This is a theoretical position paper representing a continuation and defense of Clark's controversial 1983 claim that media do not influence learning. The paper responds to Robert Kozma's critique and broader reactions to Clark's arguments, situating itself within seven decades of educational media research.

The paper matters because it challenges fundamental assumptions about educational technology investment and research. As Clark notes, educators and business trainers continue to "invest heavily in expensive media in the hope that they will produce gains in learning" (Clark, 1994, p. 486), and when gains occur, they attribute them to delivery medium rather than instructional method. If Clark is correct that media are merely delivery vehicles while instructional methods cause learning, this has profound implications for research priorities, technology purchasing decisions, curriculum design, and how we theorize about educational effectiveness. The stakes are particularly high given the substantial financial and institutional commitments to educational technology.

The document represents Clark's attempt to clarify and defend his position against four types of counter-arguments: usual uses (the McLuhan view that media and method are inseparable), meta-analytic evidence showing learning benefits from certain media, critiques from non-empiricist philosophical positions, and arguments about necessary media attributes from scholars like Gavriel Salomon. Clark's central metaphor—that media are like the truck delivering groceries, which doesn't influence nutrition any more than the food itself—captures his core claim that research confounds delivery technology with instructional design technology, leading to misattribution of causal factors in learning outcomes.

## Research Overview

The central research question asks: Do media or media attributes cause learning gains, or are observed learning differences attributable to instructional methods that happen to be embedded in media presentations? More specifically, Clark investigates whether any medium or media attribute cannot be replaced by a different medium or set of attributes to achieve similar learning results for any given student and learning task. The research was conducted by Richard E. Clark at USC, building on his earlier work from the 1980s and responding to critiques through the early 1990s.

The study employs theoretical analysis and literature review rather than empirical data collection. Clark summarizes his methodology as presenting "evidence in support of the hypothesis that instructional methods had been confounded with media and that it is methods which influence learning" (Clark, 1994, p. 76). The argumentative strategy centers on what Clark calls the "replaceability challenge": "We need to ask whether there are other media or another set of media attributes that would yield similar learning gains" (p. 107). If different media produce similar results, then the cause must lie in some shared property—which Clark identifies as instructional method—rather than the medium itself.

Key research concepts include instructional method ("any way to shape information that activates, supplants or compensates for the cognitive processes necessary for achievement or motivation," Salomon, 1979, cited in Clark, 1994, p. 164), delivery technology (formats and packages essential instructional methods based on available resources and cost-effectiveness), media attributes (capacities like television's ability to "zoom" or "unwrap" three-dimensional objects), and confounding (failure to control for instructional method when comparing media, making it impossible to isolate causal factors).

Clark draws heavily on historical media research, citing Lumsdaine (1963), who "concluded that the benefits of media were primarily economic and that their use was 'to develop the technology of instructional method'" (Clark, 1994, p. 30). He references Mielke (1968), who "predicted that adequately designed research on the learning benefits of various media would yield no significant differences between treatments" (p. 37), and Schramm (1977), "who claimed that learning is influenced more by the content and instructional strategy in a medium than by the type of medium" (p. 42). Clark emphasizes that his 1983 restatement received attention not because the claim was new, but because "previous discussions of this argument have put the claim in very tentative terms... and left the door open to media effects on learning. I made the explicit and clear claim that there were no learning benefits possible" (p. 56).

The paper's most controversial claim appears early: "media are 'mere vehicles that deliver instruction but do not influence student achievement any more than the truck that delivers our groceries causes changes in our nutrition'" (Clark, 1983, cited in Clark, 1994, p. 72). Clark later extends this to motivation, agreeing with Salomon (1984) that motivation stems from "learners' beliefs and expectations about their reactions to external events—not to external events alone" (Clark, 1994, p. 197).

## Theories of Knowledge

The paper engages multiple theoretical frameworks, primarily from cognitive psychology and philosophy of science. **Salomon's cognitive effects of media attributes theory** appears as the primary target of Clark's critique. Salomon (1979) argued that specific media attributes can "model" cognitive processes and "shape the development of unique 'cognitive processes'" (cited in Clark, 1994, p. 81). Examples include television's capacity to "zoom" into detail or "unwrap" three-dimensional objects. Clark challenges this by noting that "in every attempt to replicate the published media attribute studies... a number of very different media attributes served the same or similar cognitive functions" (p. 94). This suggests attributes are "proxies for some other variables that are instrumental in learning gains" (p. 99) rather than uniquely causal.

**Cognitive theories of motivation** underpin Clark's argument about motivational effects. Drawing on Salomon (1984) and "new cognitive theories," Clark argues that motivation derives from "learners' beliefs and expectations about their reactions to external events" (Clark, 1994, p. 197). The evidence shows "students' beliefs about their chances to learn from any given media are different for different students and for the same students at different times" (p. 200), suggesting media effects on motivation are mediated by learner perceptions rather than direct.

**Philosophy of science frameworks about necessary versus sufficient conditions** structure Clark's debate with Kozma. Kozma (1994) argued that "scientists interested in the production of something desirable, such as learning, are concerned with establishing conditions that are sufficient to bring it about" (cited in Clark, 1994, p. 356), suggesting that media attribute treatments sufficient for learning demonstrate their value. Clark counters that "when a study demonstrates that media attributes are sufficient to cause learning, the study has failed to control for instructional method and is therefore confounded" (Clark, 1994, p. 367). He argues that "any treatment that is sufficient for learning must embody whatever is necessary to cause learning" (p. 384), and that necessary condition is instructional method, not medium.

**Structural versus surface features theory** from problem-solving research provides an analogy Clark uses extensively. Drawing on Gick and Holyoak (1987), Clark explains that "surface features of analogies are those whose characteristics are of only limited and domain-specific importance," while "structural (necessary) features" are essential across contexts (Clark, 1994, p. 392). Clark argues "media attributes are surface features of learning systems. Those surface features may affect the economics but not the learning effectiveness of instruction. Instructional methods are structural (necessary) features" (p. 408). This framework allows Clark to distinguish economically relevant from causally necessary variables.

**Cognitive processing theories** implicitly undergird Clark's definition of method. He states that "method is the inclusion of one of a number of possible representations of a cognitive process or strategy that is necessary for learning but which students cannot or will not provide for themselves" (Clark, 1994, p. 418). This assumes learning requires specific cognitive operations (connecting new information to prior knowledge, activating schemas, elaboration, etc.) and that instruction must support these when learners can't self-generate them. The theory treats cognition as computational processes that can be externally supported through various means.

**Meta-analysis methodology** appears as evidence Clark must address. Kulik's meta-analyses showed "an approximate 20 percent increase in final exam scores following computer-based instruction (CBI) when it is compared to traditional forms of instruction" (Clark, 1994, p. 254). However, Clark's reanalysis found that "when the same instructional design group produces CBT and presents the live instruction with which it is compared in many studies, there is no achievement difference between the CBT and live conditions" (p. 269). This suggests the meta-analytic evidence reflects confounding rather than media effects.

**Distinction between instructional and delivery technology** represents Clark's theoretical contribution. He argues that "instructional or training design technologies draw on psychological and social-psychological research to select necessary information and objectives (as a result of task analysis) and design instructional methods and environments that enhance achievement. A very different technology—delivery technology—is necessary to provide efficient and timely access to those methods and environments" (Clark, 1994, p. 183). This framework treats instructional effectiveness and delivery efficiency as separate domains requiring different research approaches.

## Central Arguments

Clark advances a central argument that media and media attributes do not cause learning; instead, instructional methods embedded in media presentations are the active ingredients producing learning gains, and research confounding method with medium has led to systematic misattribution of causal factors. He states: "it is method which is the 'active ingredient' or active independent variable that may or may not be delivered by the medium to influence learning" (Clark, 1994, p. 431).

The first major sub-argument concerns replaceability as a test of causation. Clark proposes: "We need to ask whether there are other media or another set of media attributes that would yield similar learning gains" (Clark, 1994, p. 107). He challenges Kozma and colleagues "to find evidence, in a well designed study, of any instance of a medium or media attributes that are not replaceable by a different set of media and attributes to achieve similar learning results for any given student and learning task" (p. 120). The logic is that "if a treatment can be replaced by another treatment with similar results, the cause of the results is in some shared (and uncontrolled) properties of both treatments" (p. 127). Since "absolutely any necessary teaching method can be delivered to students by many media or a variety of mixtures of media attributes—with similar learning results" (p. 563), media cannot be the causal factor.

The second argument addresses the confusion between delivery and instructional technology. Clark contends that "delivery technologies influence the cost and access of instruction and information. Design technologies make it possible to influence student achievement. In my view, there is a long history of a basic confusion between these two technologies that strangles our study of the contributions of media" (Clark, 1994, p. 154). This distinction means "media influence cost or speed (efficiency) of learning but methods are causal in learning" (p. 442). The practical consequence is that "it is important for instructional designers to know that there are a variety of treatments that will produce a desired learning goal. However, the utility of this knowledge is largely economic. The designer can and must choose the less expensive and most cognitively efficient way to represent and deliver instruction" (p. 132).

The third argument concerns necessary versus sufficient conditions and structural versus surface features. Responding to Kozma's claim that sufficient conditions matter for design science, Clark argues that "when a study demonstrates that media attributes are sufficient to cause learning, the study has failed to control for instructional method and is therefore confounded" (Clark, 1994, p. 367). He explains: "It is true that in some cases instructional treatments containing media attributes are sufficient to cause learning. When this happens, the necessary condition to cause learning is embedded in the sufficient treatment" (p. 370). Clark uses the medical analogy: "Different forms of a medicine might help us provided that they all contain the same method or active ingredient. The different forms of medicine are similar to different media" (p. 452). Just as tablets, liquid suspensions, and injections differ in efficiency but achieve similar health outcomes when containing the same active ingredient, different media differ in cost/speed but achieve similar learning when embedding the same instructional method.

The fourth argument addresses the "usual uses" misconception. Clark notes that many respondents took "Marshall McLuhan's view that media and method were identical and inseparable" (Clark, 1994, p. 215), believing that television naturally conveys realistic visual content while computers provide simulations. Clark counters: "the usual uses of a medium do not limit the methods or content it is capable of presenting. Computers can present realistic visual, real-time documentary information, and television can present semantically dense simulations" (p. 229). He cites Suppes' computer drill-and-practice study where a control district accidentally delivered the same method via teachers, producing identical achievement gains at similar cost, demonstrating that "it was not the medium but the drill and practice method that influenced achievement" (p. 241).

The fifth argument concerns meta-analytic evidence and its interpretation. Clark acknowledges that meta-analyses show learning benefits from computer-based instruction but argues this reflects method confounding. He notes that Kulik "agreed that it is not the computer but the teaching method built into CBI that accounts for the learning gains in those studies" and that "the methods used in CBI can be and are used by teachers in live instruction" (Clark, 1994, p. 260). Kulik's phrase about "diffusion of the innovative treatment to the control condition" (p. 275) acknowledges that most studies fail to control method, making it impossible to isolate media effects.

The sixth argument addresses motivation. Clark agrees with Salomon that "students' beliefs about their chances to learn from any given media are different for different students and for the same students at different times" (Clark, 1994, p. 200), meaning motivational effects are mediated by learner perceptions rather than caused directly by media. This cognitive framing treats motivation as attribution and expectation rather than stimulus response.

The final argument concerns research priorities and theory development. Clark argues that failing to separate medium from method "has caused enormous confounding and waste in a very important and expensive research area" (Clark, 1994, p. 478). He contends that "when learning gains are found, we attribute them to the delivery medium, not to the active ingredient in instruction. When learning gains are absent, we assume we have chosen the wrong mix of media" (p. 488), leading to continued expensive investments based on misattributed causation. Clark advocates shifting focus to "a concern with instructional methods that support the structural elements of cognitive processing during learning and transfer" (p. 151).

## Evidence

The evidence supporting Clark's arguments comes primarily from historical literature review, reanalysis of existing studies, and logical argumentation rather than new empirical data. Clark cites a 70-year history of similar conclusions, beginning with Lumsdaine (1963) in the first Handbook of Research on Teaching, who concluded media benefits were "primarily economic" (Clark, 1994, p. 30). Mielke (1968) "predicted that adequately designed research on the learning benefits of various media would yield no significant differences between treatments" (p. 37). Schramm (1977), described as "this century's most prolific media research reviewer," claimed "learning is influenced more by the content and instructional strategy in a medium than by the type of medium" (p. 42). Levie and Dickie (1973) made the same point in the Second Handbook, and Clark reached the same conclusion with Salomon in the Third Handbook (1986).

The Suppes drill-and-practice study provides concrete evidence for replaceability. As Clark describes: "In a study of computers versus teachers using drill and practice in mathematics, Suppes found that one of his control school districts had messed up the data collection by delivering more drill and practice in mathematics than was permitted by the study—using teachers and not computers. The result was that in that school district, mathematics achievement increased at exactly the same rate as it did in districts where computers were giving drill and practice" (Clark, 1994, p. 236). Suppes concluded "it was not the medium but the drill and practice method that influenced achievement but he noted that the cost of the intervention might have been less with computers" (p. 242), demonstrating both replaceability and the economic rather than learning distinction.

Clark's reanalysis of meta-analytic studies provides quantitative evidence. He examined "a 30 percent sample of the studies [Kulik] used and found that when the same instructional design group produces CBT and presents the live instruction with which it is compared in many studies, there is no achievement difference between the CBT and live conditions" (Clark, 1994, p. 269). This suggests that when method is controlled, media effects disappear, supporting the confounding hypothesis.

Evidence about media attributes comes from replication failures. Clark notes that "in every attempt to replicate the published media attribute studies... a number of very different media attributes served the same or similar cognitive functions" (Clark, 1994, p. 94). He gives the example that "there are a variety of equally effective ways to highlight details other than zooming" (p. 91), suggesting that if multiple different attributes accomplish the same learning goal, the attribute itself cannot be uniquely causal. When Petkovich and Tennyson (1984) cited a computer simulation teaching flying skills as evidence for necessary media attributes, Clark responded that "people learned to fly planes before computers were developed and therefore the media attributes required to learn were obviously neither exclusive to computers nor necessary for learning to fly" (p. 324).

Regarding Kozma's evidence from ThinkerTools and Jasper Woodbury Series, Clark argues these studies "were not designed so that their results would provide evidence about the claims being made in this dispute. The research conducted to validate these very creative instructional programs did not control for the sources of confounding that lie at the root of the argument" (Clark, 1994, p. 505). The ThinkerTools computer program was "compared with a standard curriculum for teaching force and motion. It is not clear whether the standard curriculum used similar instructional methods but it is very doubtful" (p. 511). The Jasper program control group "did not receive instruction in 'decomposition and solution strategies'" (p. 514), making it impossible to determine whether other media could deliver these methods with similar results.

The research method carries inherent limitations. As theoretical argument supported by literature review, it does not provide direct experimental evidence testing the replaceability hypothesis Clark advocates. While Clark challenges others to find counter-examples, the burden of proof question remains contested—should media advocates demonstrate unique effects, or should Clark demonstrate perfect replaceability across all possible cases?

The definition of "instructional method" carries some circularity. Clark defines it as cognitive support that students "cannot or will not provide for themselves" (Clark, 1994, p. 419), but identifying what counts as the "same method" delivered through different media requires judgment. When is a zoom function versus verbal highlighting versus animated pointing delivering the "same" method of directing attention? The granularity at which we identify methods affects replaceability claims.

Clark acknowledges that "whenever learning occurs, some medium or mix of media must be present to deliver instruction" (Clark, 1994, p. 415), which slightly complicates the causal story. If medium is always necessary (though not uniquely so), does this make it a background condition rather than irrelevant? The philosophical distinction between necessary-but-not-sufficient conditions and causally irrelevant factors deserves more elaboration.

The paper's focus on learning effectiveness while bracketing cost/efficiency means economic factors get treated as secondary. Yet for practitioners, cost-effectiveness is often the primary decision criterion. Clark's framework helps with this ("choose the less expensive delivery"), but doesn't address cases where only expensive media can practically deliver certain methods to scale, making the theoretical distinction less actionable.

The historical evidence showing researchers repeatedly reaching similar conclusions over 70 years could support either interpretation: that Clark is correct and media researchers keep relearning the same lesson, or that the claim is too strong and researchers keep finding exceptions that prevent consensus.

## Conclusion

This research contributes a clarifying framework for understanding educational technology effectiveness by rigorously distinguishing between delivery systems (media) and learning mechanisms (instructional methods). Clark's replaceability test provides a practical heuristic: if different media achieve similar learning outcomes for a given method and student population, then the medium is not the active causal ingredient. The implications are profound—billions spent on educational technology may reflect confusion about what actually drives learning, with investments focused on delivery vehicles rather than instructional design.

The immediate practical contribution is redirecting attention and resources. As Clark argues, "we continue to invest heavily in expensive media in the hope that they will produce gains in learning" (Clark, 1994, p. 485), but if methods are causal, investment should prioritize instructional design expertise over delivery technology. This doesn't mean technology is unimportant—cost and access matter enormously—but it does mean we should evaluate technology primarily on economic rather than learning effectiveness grounds, assuming adequate instructional methods are embedded.

The theoretical contribution distinguishes instructional technology (design of cognitive supports) from delivery technology (efficient packaging and transmission). This conceptual split allows researchers to ask clearer questions: What cognitive processes does this learner need support for? What methods can provide that support? Which delivery systems can present those methods most cost-effectively? Current research often conflates these, attributing learning gains to delivery medium when the method delivered is the causal factor.

The paper's challenge to media attribute research suggests a research reorientation. Rather than studying whether particular media attributes (zooming, animation, interactivity) improve learning, researchers should study what cognitive supports students need, then investigate which combinations of presentational features (deliverable through many media) provide those supports most efficiently. This shifts from medium-centric to cognition-centric research.

What remains open are boundary questions about when media characteristics might genuinely constrain instructional possibilities. Clark claims "absolutely any necessary teaching method can be delivered to students by many media" (Clark, 1994, p. 563), but are there edge cases? Can all methods truly be delivered via text, audio, static images, and human speech given sufficient ingenuity? Or do some learning goals require affordances that only certain media provide practically, even if theoretically replaceable?

Also unresolved is how to operationalize "same method" at appropriate grain size. When a computer simulation allows manipulation of variables while a physical demonstration shows the same phenomena, are these the "same" method (both provide concrete instantiation of abstract principles) or different methods (interactivity versus observation)? The framework's utility depends on having principled ways to identify method similarity and difference.

The motivation question deserves further investigation. If learner beliefs mediate motivational effects of media, as Clark argues, this suggests media can affect motivation indirectly through their influence on expectations and attributions. The relationship between direct causal effects and mediated effects through perception warrants more theoretical development.

Finally, the historical pattern of researchers repeatedly reaching similar conclusions yet media research continuing unabated suggests sociological and institutional factors deserve attention. Why do intuitions about media effects persist despite contrary evidence? What incentive structures in educational technology research, development, and policy sustain investment patterns that Clark's analysis suggests are misguided? The disconnect between research consensus and practice demands explanation beyond individual cognitive error.

## Discussion Questions

1. Clark argues that any necessary teaching method can be delivered through multiple media, making the medium replaceable and therefore not causal. But might different media make certain methods more or less likely to be used well in practice, even if theoretically equivalent? If teachers consistently implement methods more faithfully when using certain media due to cognitive load, interface design, or professional norms, does this practical difference matter more than theoretical replaceability for design decisions?

2. The paper treats cost-effectiveness as secondary to causal questions about learning, arguing we should identify effective methods first, then choose cheapest delivery. But in resource-constrained contexts, might an "good enough" method delivered via inexpensive medium outperform a theoretically superior method that's too expensive to implement at scale? Does Clark's framework inadvertently privilege contexts with sufficient resources to optimize both method and delivery?

3. Clark uses the medical analogy that tablets, injections, and liquid suspensions are different delivery forms for the same active ingredient. But medicine also recognizes that delivery mechanism affects bioavailability, absorption rate, and patient compliance, sometimes making delivery choices clinically significant despite same active ingredient. Might educational media similarly affect cognitive "bioavailability" or learner engagement in ways that make delivery mechanisms more consequential than Clark acknowledges?

4. The paper argues that 70 years of negative evidence should convince researchers to stop investigating media effects. Yet scientific history includes cases where persistent negative findings eventually yielded to positive results under refined conditions or new theoretical frameworks. How should we balance respecting accumulated evidence against remaining open to the possibility that media might matter in ways current research designs cannot detect? Is Clark's position appropriately conservative or prematurely foreclosing productive research directions?
