# To Engage or Not to Engage with AI for Critical Judgments: How Professionals Deal with Opacity When Using AI for Medical Diagnosis

## Summary

This paper investigates how professionals integrate AI tools into their critical decision-making processes, specifically examining how radiologists at a major U.S. hospital use AI for medical diagnoses. The study focuses on the challenge of "opacity" - the inability to understand the underlying reasoning behind AI outputs from deep learning systems that function as "black boxes." The authors conducted an in-depth ethnographic field study across three radiology departments (lung cancer, breast cancer, and bone age determination), observing over 1,000 diagnostic cases and conducting 33 interviews over ten months.

The central finding is that AI opacity increased uncertainty for professionals in all three departments, as AI results frequently diverged from their initial judgments without providing underlying reasoning. However, the departments showed divergent patterns in how they dealt with this opacity. Only in the lung cancer department did radiologists consistently incorporate AI results into their final judgments through what the authors call "engaged augmentation." These professionals enacted "AI interrogation practices" - actively probing, questioning, and relating AI knowledge claims to their own expertise. In contrast, breast cancer and bone age radiologists enacted "unengaged augmentation," either ignoring or blindly accepting AI inputs without deep engagement.

The study contributes to literature on AI adoption in knowledge work by identifying the conditions under which professionals can successfully integrate opaque AI tools into their practice. Key factors include the AI tool's ability to reduce uncertainty, time pressure constraints, and the richness of complementary technologies available for interrogation.

## Key Concepts

- **AI Opacity**: The difficulty or impossibility of understanding the reasoning behind a given AI output, particularly with deep learning algorithms that function as "black boxes." Users cannot examine how the algorithm arrived at a particular result.

- **Engaged Augmentation**: A pattern of human-AI interaction where professionals regularly integrate AI knowledge claims with their own expertise by enacting AI interrogation practices. This involves overruling AI claims, reflectively agreeing with them, or synthesizing AI and expert claims to form new judgments.

- **Unengaged Augmentation**: A pattern where professionals do not relate AI knowledge claims to their own expertise, instead either blindly accepting AI claims or ignoring them without meaningful reflection.

- **AI Interrogation Practices**: Practices enacted by human experts to relate their own knowledge claims to AI knowledge claims. These involve building an understanding of the AI result, reconciling divergent viewpoints, and integrating the claims into final judgments.

- **Epistemic Uncertainty**: Uncertainty experienced by professionals when AI results diverge from their initial judgment without explanation, leaving them unable to understand or validate the AI's reasoning.

## Theoretical Framework

The study adopts a sociomaterial perspective that treats technologies-in-use as part of specific organizational configurations rather than as having inherent fixed features. Drawing on Suchman (2007) and Orlikowski (2007), the authors view opacity not as an inherent property of AI tools but as something produced and enacted through practices situated in particular social and historical contexts. This lens enables examination of how opacity is experienced and dealt with when professionals use AI, focusing on the "categorical debates" around AI and opacity through empirical investigations of concrete practices where individuals and technologies act together.

The theoretical contribution centers on distinguishing "engaged augmentation" from "unengaged augmentation" - challenging the taken-for-granted assumption in literature that human-AI augmentation automatically involves humans and machines "collaborating" to "multiply" and "combine their complementary strengths."

## Methods

The authors conducted an in-depth ethnographic field study within three departments of a large diagnostic radiology organization at a major U.S. teaching hospital ("Urbanside"). Data collection began in late 2018 and ethnographic fieldwork ran from January 2019, studying 40 radiologists across three departments using AI tools for breast imaging, chest imaging (lung cancer), and pediatric imaging (bone age).

**Data sources included:**
- **Observation**: Over 500 hours of ethnographic observation documenting over 1,000 diagnostic cases with detailed written observational notes
- **Interviews**: 33 semi-structured interviews (12 formal, 21 informal) covering diagnostic practices, perceptions of AI tools, and uncertainty experiences
- **Documentation and Artifacts**: Medical notes, photographs, technical research papers, regulatory filings, and vendor documentation

**Analysis**: Following grounded theory principles, the researchers engaged in iterative rounds of data analysis with open coding to capture emerging themes. They focused on how radiologists integrated AI results using "AI interrogation practices" and developed their conceptualization through iteration with literatures on epistemic uncertainty and opacity.

## Main Arguments

- AI opacity is not merely a technical property but emerges through sociomaterial practice - how professionals experience and deal with opacity depends on the specific context of AI-in-use

- Using AI tools for critical judgments increases professionals' uncertainty because AI results often diverge from initial judgments without explanation, heightening epistemic uncertainty

- Explainable or interpretable AI may enable but not guarantee that professionals will integrate AI knowledge (engaged augmentation) - professionals must also have the motivation and willingness to invest time in AI interrogation practices

- Three factors influence whether professionals enact AI interrogation practices: (1) the AI tool's ability to reduce professionals' uncertainty, (2) time pressure and resource constraints, and (3) the richness of complementary technologies-in-use for interrogation

- Human-in-the-loop scenarios of "unengaged augmentation" - where professionals cannot relate AI claims to their own expertise - are essentially cases of automation without the learning and knowledge benefits typically associated with augmentation

- The assumption that augmentation will help organizations achieve positive outcomes through improved human knowledge should be questioned - unengaged augmentation may lead to declining quality without the benefits of knowledge transformation

## Limitations & Critiques

- **Single organization**: The study was conducted within one highly resourced U.S. teaching hospital with world-leading experts and strong professional accountability. Findings may differ in hospitals with fewer resources and lower standards of care, where unengaged augmentation may be more common.

- **U.S. regulatory context**: The U.S. legal system requires hospitals to adhere to strict regulation and oversight, which is not the case in many other countries. The role of regulation on AI engagement deserves further research.

- **Profession-specific**: This is a study of physicians within a specific medical field. How other professional contexts (judges, HR managers, military experts) experience opacity and enact interrogation practices requires further investigation.

- **Similar degree of opacity**: All three AI tools studied had similar degrees of opacity. Future studies could explore how different degrees of opacity lead to varying interrogation practices.

- **Time dimension**: The study represents a snapshot; it is unclear whether professionals may develop AI interrogation practices over longer periods of time, or whether engaged professionals may eventually start reproducing AI biases through routine endorsement of AI results.

- **Efficiency assumptions challenged**: In all cases studied, experts using AI tools spent additional time coping with heightened uncertainty, questioning the common assumption that AI augmentation increases efficiency. Healthcare systems' willingness to invest this additional time remains unclear.
