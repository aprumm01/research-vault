# Comparing Student Designers' Metacognition and Design Processes With and Without Generative AI (Chen et al., 2025)


## Abstract

## Summary
Chen et al.'s 2025 study compares student designers' metacognitive processes and design outcomes when working with versus without generative AI tools. Using protocol analysis and design artifact comparison, the study finds that AI-assisted designers show different metacognitive patterns: they spend less time in divergent ideation and more time evaluating and directing AI outputs. While AI-assisted students produce more visually diverse initial concepts, they show less evidence of deep design reasoning. The paper raises important questions about what design cognition AI tools enhance versus potentially atrophy.

## Key Concepts
- Student designer metacognition
- Protocol analysis of AI-assisted vs. non-AI design
- Divergent vs. convergent thinking with AI
- Design reasoning depth
- Cognitive offloading in design education

## Theoretical Framework
Design cognition research; metacognition theory; human-AI collaboration; draws on protocol analysis methodology from design cognition research tradition (Cross, Schön).

## Methods
Controlled comparison study; concurrent think-aloud protocol analysis with student designers using AI tools vs. without; coding of metacognitive moves and design reasoning episodes; design artifact quality assessment.

## Main Arguments
- AI-assisted designers engage in different metacognitive patterns: more evaluation and curation, less initial generation and exploration
- Without AI, students show more evidence of deep design reasoning during ideation; with AI, they produce more quantity but potentially less depth
- The shift to AI-assisted design requires new metacognitive skills (directing, evaluating, iterating on AI outputs) that must be explicitly taught
- Design education should cultivate both AI-augmented and unaugmented design cognition to ensure students develop full metacognitive repertoires

## Limitations & Critiques
Laboratory-like protocol study may not capture real design studio practice; student participants are novice designers, limiting generalizability to professional practice; the quality assessment of design artifacts involves subjective judgment.
