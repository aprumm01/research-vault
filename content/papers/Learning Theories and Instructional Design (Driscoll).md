# Learning Theories and Instructional Design (Driscoll)

## Summary
Driscoll and Burner provide an overview of the major psychological theories of learning and their foundational implications for instructional design. The chapter distinguishes learning from development and maturation, defines learning as a persisting change in performance or performance potential through experience, and then examines how behavioral, cognitive, constructivist, and sociocultural perspectives each generate different instructional implications. The chapter also introduces connectivism as an emerging framework for understanding networked, technologically mediated learning.

## Key Concepts
- Learning defined as persisting change in performance potential through experience
- Behavioral learning theory and reinforcement principles
- Cognitive learning theory: schemas, information processing, memory
- Constructivism: knowledge built through active experience
- Sociocultural and connectivist perspectives on learning
- Implications of each theory for instructional design decisions

## Theoretical Framework
Psychological foundations of instructional design; draws on Skinner's behaviorism, cognitive information processing theory, constructivism, Vygotsky's sociocultural framework, and Siemens's connectivism; situates each within contemporary instructional design practice.

## Methods
Conceptual review and synthesis chapter; examines major learning theories and derives instructional implications for each; designed as a foundational reference for instructional designers rather than as primary empirical research.

## Main Arguments
- Different theories of learning lead to meaningfully different instructional strategies; designers must understand the theoretical basis for their choices
- Behavioral theory supports structured practice, reinforcement, and observable performance objectives
- Cognitive theory supports schema activation, worked examples, and strategies that reduce extraneous cognitive load
- Constructivism and sociocultural perspectives support collaborative, problem-based, and authentic learning environments
- Connectivism challenges individually-focused models by framing learning as network-distributed knowledge building enabled by technology

## Limitations & Critiques
As a survey chapter in an edited handbook, the treatment of each theory is necessarily brief and introductory. The chapter presents theories in relatively discrete categories, which may obscure the practical overlap and theoretical tensions among them. Connectivism, while included, remains contested as a formal learning theory.
