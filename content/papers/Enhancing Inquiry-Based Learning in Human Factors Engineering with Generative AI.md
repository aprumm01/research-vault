# Enhancing Inquiry-Based Learning in Human Factors Engineering with Generative AI

## Overview of the Document

The authors of this work are Tzu-No Tseng, Tung-Ming Lee, and Jo-Yu Kuo, representing three distinct institutions with complementary expertise. Tzu-No Tseng comes from the Department of Industrial Design at the National Taipei University of Technology in Taiwan, bringing a design education perspective. Tung-Ming Lee is affiliated with the Institute of Allied Health Sciences at National Cheng Kung University, also in Taiwan, contributing a health sciences and human factors lens. Jo-Yu Kuo rounds out the team from the Department of Creative Design at the National Taipei University of Technology, adding another layer of design pedagogy expertise. This interdisciplinary combination positions them well to examine the intersection of design education, human factors engineering, and emerging AI technologies.

This document is an empirical research study published in the proceedings of the 27th International Conference on Human-Computer Interaction (HCII 2025), specifically within the Distributed, Ambient and Pervasive Interactions track. It appears as part of the Lecture Notes in Computer Science series (volume 15808, pages 402-414), published by Springer Nature Switzerland AG. The paper sits at the intersection of several academic fields: design education, human factors engineering, educational technology, and human-computer interaction. It matters because it provides empirical evidence about how generative AI tools are actually being used by design students in authentic learning contexts, moving beyond speculation to documented observation of a real workshop with 54 participants. The study addresses a timely question for design educators who are rapidly integrating AI tools into their curricula without clear evidence about how these tools affect inquiry-based learning processes or design thinking stages.

The research examines whether and how generative AI influences the inquiry-based learning process when students engage in human factors engineering design challenges. The authors situate their work within broader concerns about whether AI might short-circuit the critical questioning and exploration phases that are central to design education. By documenting actual usage patterns, question generation, and learning outcomes, the study provides empirical grounding for ongoing debates about AI's role in design pedagogy.

## Research Overview

The central research question asks: How does the integration of generative AI tools influence inquiry-based learning (IBL) and design thinking processes when industrial design students tackle human factors engineering challenges? More specifically, the researchers wanted to understand which stages of the design thinking process students actually use AI for, whether AI usage affects the quantity and quality of questions students generate, and whether reliance on AI impacts their overall learning performance as measured by IBL rubrics.

The study was conducted by the three authors named above, who organized and facilitated a two-day workshop at their institution in Taiwan. They worked with 54 industrial design students who were tasked with redesigning toys for children with special needs, specifically targeting toy designs for children with cerebral palsy. Of the 54 participants, 44 provided valid data that could be analyzed (the reasons for the 10-participant attrition are not specified in the paper).

The researchers employed a mixed-methods approach combining quantitative survey data with qualitative analysis of student-generated artifacts. Students worked through the Stanford d.school design thinking framework, which consists of five stages: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, and Test. The authors explain that this framework "has been widely adopted in design education, guiding students to explore complex problems systematically through iterative processes" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 403). Throughout the workshop, students had access to generative AI tools (specifically Claude AI and ChatGPT) and were free to use them at any stage of their work.

Data collection included three key components. First, students completed questionnaires that captured demographic information, their frequency of AI tool usage across the five design thinking stages, and their self-reported dependency on AI. Second, researchers collected all design artifacts produced during the workshop, including sketches, prototypes, and written documentation. Third, students' work was evaluated using an inquiry-based learning rubric that assessed their ability to generate questions, explore problems systematically, and develop well-reasoned solutions.

Key research concepts require some unpacking. Inquiry-based learning refers to a pedagogical approach where learning occurs primarily through student-generated questions and exploratory investigation rather than through direct instruction. The authors note that "IBL encourages learners to ask questions, explore information, and develop explanations based on evidence" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 404). Design thinking, as operationalized here through the Stanford d.school model, represents a structured approach to creative problem-solving that emphasizes empathy with users, clear problem definition, divergent ideation, rapid prototyping, and iterative testing. Human factors engineering, the domain context for the design challenge, focuses on designing products and systems that account for human capabilities, limitations, and needs. In this case, students needed to understand the specific motor control challenges, sensory processing differences, and play patterns of children with cerebral palsy.

## Theories of Knowledge

The primary theoretical framework organizing this research is inquiry-based learning (IBL), which the authors trace to constructivist educational theory. IBL rests on the premise that deep learning occurs when students actively construct knowledge through questioning and investigation rather than passively receiving information. The authors explain that "inquiry-based learning is grounded in the constructivist learning theory, which emphasizes that learners construct knowledge through active engagement with problems" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 404). This connects to broader constructivist epistemology articulated by scholars like Jean Piaget and Lev Vygotsky, who argued that knowledge is built through interaction with the environment and social mediation rather than transmitted directly from teacher to student.

The second major theoretical structure is design thinking, specifically the Stanford d.school five-stage model. While the paper doesn't cite a specific originator for this framework (it has been collectively developed and popularized by Stanford's Hasso Plattner Institute of Design), the authors reference it as a structured methodology that guides students through problem exploration. The stages progress from empathizing with users, to defining clear problem statements, to generating multiple solution ideas, to building testable prototypes, to gathering feedback through testing. This framework embeds assumptions about how creative problem-solving works best: through iterative cycles, divergent and convergent thinking phases, and maintaining focus on human needs throughout the process.

A third theoretical thread, though less explicitly developed, involves cognitive load theory and attention management. The authors cite research on the HIDO system, an adaptive learning platform they developed for teaching programming. As they describe it, "the HIDO system aims to reduce visual distraction by presenting practice questions one at a time, limiting the visible question queue to three to prevent learners from searching for easy questions to solve" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 405). This draws on cognitive load theory's principle that working memory has limited capacity, and that learning environments should be designed to manage cognitive demands carefully. By mentioning this work, the authors situate their current study within a broader research program examining how educational technology can support or hinder cognitive processes essential for learning.

The paper also implicitly invokes theories of technological determinism versus social construction of technology. The research question itself challenges deterministic assumptions that AI will automatically improve or degrade learning outcomes. Instead, by examining actual usage patterns and their varied effects, the study takes a more nuanced socio-technical perspective where the relationship between technology and learning is mediated by how students choose to deploy AI tools within their design processes.

## Central Arguments

The central argument the authors advance is that generative AI's influence on inquiry-based learning in design education is more nuanced and less deterministic than either enthusiastic or pessimistic commentators suggest. They contend that AI tools are not uniformly helpful or harmful across all stages of the design process, but rather are selectively employed by students at specific moments, primarily during the ideation phase. As the authors state, "the results showed that Gen AI was most actively utilized during the Ideate stage, suggesting students primarily relied on it for generating creative solutions" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 402).

This leads to their second key claim: that students' inquiry behaviors remain concentrated in the early problem-understanding phases regardless of AI availability. The data showed that "students exhibited the highest levels of inquiry behavior during the Define and Empathize stages, which aligns with the nature of these stages requiring extensive problem exploration and research" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 410). This finding challenges concerns that AI might cause students to skip crucial questioning and exploration work in favor of jumping straight to AI-generated solutions.

The third major argument addresses the relationship between AI dependency and learning outcomes. The researchers found that "AI dependency did not have a substantial impact on the total IBL rubric scores, indicating that frequent AI usage does not inherently lead to learning challenges" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 411). However, they qualify this by noting that AI dependency did show a slight negative correlation with question generation, suggesting some nuanced effects worth monitoring.

A fourth claim concerns the continued importance of traditional design activities even in AI-augmented environments. The study found that "the number of sketches demonstrated a significant positive correlation with the total IBL rubric scores, indicating that active engagement in sketching positively impacts inquiry-based learning outcomes" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 411). This suggests that hands-on, embodied design activities retain their pedagogical value and may even be essential for successful AI-augmented design work.

Finally, the authors argue for a process-centric rather than outcome-centric view of AI integration in design education. They emphasize that understanding when and how students use AI matters more than simply measuring whether AI is present or absent. This argument positions them against both uncritical AI adoption and blanket AI prohibition, instead advocating for thoughtful integration that preserves essential learning processes while leveraging AI's capabilities where appropriate.

## Evidence

The quantitative evidence supporting these arguments comes from survey data and rubric assessments of 44 students. Regarding AI usage patterns across design stages, the researchers collected self-reported frequency data showing that 68.2% of students used AI during the Ideate phase, compared to lower percentages in other stages. The authors present this finding: "Among the design thinking stages, the Ideate phase exhibited the highest AI usage frequency, with 68.2% of students engaging with AI tools during this stage" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 409). This was followed by the Prototype stage (54.5%), Define stage (50%), Empathize stage (45.5%), and Test stage (34.1%).

For inquiry behavior patterns, the evidence comes from analyzing when and how many questions students generated during the workshop. The data showed distinct clustering in specific stages: "The findings showed that students exhibited the highest levels of inquiry behavior during the Define and Empathize stages, accounting for 40.9% and 29.5% of total questions, respectively" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 410). In contrast, questions during Ideate, Prototype, and Test stages represented only 15.9%, 9.1%, and 4.5% respectively.

The relationship between AI dependency and learning outcomes was assessed through correlation analysis. Students self-reported their AI dependency on a scale, and researchers correlated this with IBL rubric scores. The results showed "AI dependency did not exhibit a significant correlation with the total IBL rubric score" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 411), though the specific correlation coefficient is not provided in the excerpted text. This was interpreted as evidence that AI usage itself doesn't inherently undermine learning outcomes.

However, the relationship between AI dependency and question generation showed more complex patterns. The authors note that "AI dependency showed a slight negative correlation with question generation (r = −0.188), though this relationship was not statistically significant" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 411). While not reaching statistical significance, this trend suggests a potential area for concern that merits continued investigation.

The strongest positive finding concerned sketching activity. Correlation analysis revealed that "the number of sketches demonstrated a significant positive correlation with the total IBL rubric scores (r = 0.382, p < 0.05)" (Tseng et al., 2025, p. 411). This provides quantitative support for the continued importance of traditional hands-on design activities.

The study's limitations are both methodological and contextual. First, the research relies heavily on self-reported data about AI usage rather than logged or observed behavior. Students may not accurately recall when or how they used AI tools, or may over- or under-report usage based on perceived expectations. Second, the sample consists entirely of industrial design students at one institution in Taiwan, limiting generalizability to other contexts, disciplines, or cultural settings. Third, the two-day workshop format, while providing intensive focused time, doesn't capture how students might use AI differently over a full semester-long course where habits and dependencies could develop differently.

Fourth, the study doesn't examine the quality of AI interactions or outputs, only their frequency and self-reported dependency. Students using AI effectively to enhance their thinking would be grouped with students using AI ineffectively to avoid thinking, as long as their frequency and dependency levels were similar. Fifth, the IBL rubric used to assess learning outcomes is not fully described in the paper, making it difficult to assess construct validity or whether the rubric adequately captures the learning goals the researchers care about.

Finally, the timing of this study (workshop conducted in what appears to be 2024, published in 2025) means it captures a snapshot of AI integration very early in the generative AI era. Both the capabilities of AI tools and students' sophistication in using them are rapidly evolving, potentially dating these findings quickly.

## Conclusion

This research contributes empirical evidence to debates about generative AI's role in design education at a moment when such evidence is scarce but urgently needed. The study's core finding that AI usage concentrates in the ideation phase while inquiry behaviors remain strongest in problem-understanding phases offers some reassurance to educators worried that AI might cause students to skip foundational learning processes. Students appear to be using AI strategically as a tool for generating solution options rather than as a replacement for the hard work of understanding problems deeply.

The finding that AI dependency doesn't significantly correlate with overall learning outcomes, while sketching activity shows strong positive correlation with IBL scores, suggests that traditional hands-on design activities continue to drive learning even in AI-augmented environments. This has important implications for curriculum design: rather than choosing between AI integration and traditional methods, educators might focus on ensuring students remain actively engaged in core design activities like sketching while also learning to leverage AI tools appropriately.

However, the slight negative correlation between AI dependency and question generation, though not statistically significant here, points to an area requiring continued attention. If future studies with larger samples or longer timeframes confirm this trend, it would suggest that educators need to actively cultivate questioning skills and guard against AI dependency patterns that might short-circuit inquiry processes.

What remains open from this work is substantial. First, the study doesn't examine the quality of design outcomes, only the process and IBL scores. Whether AI-augmented work produces better, worse, or simply different design solutions remains unknown. Second, the mechanisms behind the observed patterns are unclear. Why do students gravitate toward using AI during ideation? Is it because AI tools are particularly well-suited to generating options, because students perceive ideation as less critical than problem understanding, or for other reasons entirely? Third, the longer-term developmental trajectory is unexamined. How do AI usage patterns and their effects change as students gain experience with both design and AI tools over months or years?

Fourth, individual differences among students are largely invisible in this analysis. The aggregated correlations might hide important subgroups: perhaps some students benefit greatly from AI while others are harmed, with these effects canceling out in group-level statistics. Finally, this study captures a single design challenge in one domain (toys for children with special needs). How AI integration patterns and effects vary across different types of design problems, different domains, and different educational levels remains to be investigated.

For a PhD student returning to this paper six months from now, the key takeaway is this: when debates about AI in education become polarized between enthusiasm and alarm, this study offers evidence that the reality is more textured. AI tools are being woven into design practice in specific, selective ways rather than transforming everything uniformly. The pedagogical challenge is not whether to use AI, but how to integrate it in ways that preserve and enhance the inquiry-based learning processes that drive deep understanding.

## Discussion Questions

1. The study finds that students concentrate their AI usage in the Ideate phase while maintaining inquiry behaviors in the Empathize and Define phases. Does this pattern represent appropriate strategic use of AI tools, or does it reveal a problematic assumption that ideation requires less critical thinking than problem understanding? How might this usage pattern shape what students learn about the nature of creative work?

2. Sketching activity showed the strongest correlation with learning outcomes in this study, even in an AI-augmented context. What does this tell us about the relationship between embodied, hands-on activities and cognitive processes in design learning? Should design educators double down on traditional methods, or does this finding simply reflect that current AI tools aren't yet good at supporting the kinds of thinking that sketching enables?

3. The paper mentions the HIDO system, which limits visible practice questions to reduce distraction and prevent students from cherry-picking easy problems. How does this design philosophy relate to the affordances of generative AI tools, which make it easy to rapidly generate multiple solutions? Should educators designing AI-augmented learning environments build in similar constraints?

4. The authors studied a two-day intensive workshop with a specific design challenge. How might AI usage patterns and effects differ in a semester-long course where students work on multiple projects, develop deeper expertise, and have time for habits and dependencies to form? What research designs would help us understand longer-term developmental trajectories?
