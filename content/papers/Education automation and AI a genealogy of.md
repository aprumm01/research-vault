## Key Concepts
- **Educational Imaginaries**: Collectively held visions of desirable futures attainable through education about science and technology
- **Genealogical Approach**: Foucauldian method revealing power differentials and contingent histories rather than linear progressions
- **Sociotechnical Imaginaries**: Shared understandings of social life and order achievable through technological advances
- **Automation Anticipations**: Historical shifts from 1950s leisure-time utopias to 1970s surveillance dystopias to present AI literacy imperatives
- **Governance Through Education**: Broad education efforts as tools for shaping desired sociotechnical futures
- **Datafication of Talent**: 1950s aptitude testing and educational reserve concepts applying statistical techniques to identify "gifted boys"
- **Computer Literacy**: Evolving from 1970s public control emphasis to 1990s skills-for-employability to 2020s AI fluency requirements
- **Unnecessariat**: Growing surplus of people lacking necessary digital skills, rendered superfluous by automation
- **Explainable and Trustworthy AI**: Transparency dependent on citizenry's education and skill sets to understand AI explanations

## Theoretical Framework
Foucauldian genealogy combined with Jasanoff's sociotechnical imaginaries theory, elaborated through educational lens. Examines how education policies function as public pedagogy and modes of intervention to actualize desired futures. Analyzes problematizations, negotiations, and regimes of dominance through historical-genealogical mapping from 1950s-present.

## Methods
Genealogical analysis of Swedish computerization discourses (1955-1997) with transnational comparisons. Analyzed policies, political speeches, educational materials (broadcast and print), public reports, courses, films, and radio broadcasts. Isolated different contexts where events occur while remaining sensitive to missing/unrealized events. Swedish vantage point chosen for interventionist state approach and rich history of state-funded computerization efforts.

## Main Arguments
- **Education repeatedly mobilized for governance**: Broad education efforts serve as primary tools for creating social preconditions for automation and ensuring citizen leverage over technological futures
- **Four educational imaginaries emerge historically**: (1) 1950s adaptation to leisure-time explosion, (2) 1970s broad knowledge against computing threats, (3) 1980s-90s skills for employment, (4) 2000s-present AI literacy and futures competency
- **Intersectional structures embedded from origin**: 1950s educational reserve already datafied through aptitude testing, primarily targeting able-bodied males while conditionally including women
- **Control oscillates between elites and publics**: 1950s government/market control shifts to 1970s emphasis on democratic public utility computing, then back to market logics
- **Universities positioned as alternative developers**: 1960s-70s academic computing emphasizes simplicity, user needs, openness, and democracy over military-industrial efficiency
- **Education cannot solve structural unemployment**: Despite decades of upskilling responses, technological displacement outpaces educational solutions
- **Contemporary AI literacy imperative mirrors past patterns**: Digital competencies, computational thinking, and "powerful knowledges" replicate historical governance-through-education strategies
- **Alternative futures remain unexplored**: Genealogy reveals paths not taken—stopping computerization, public ownership, worker control—suggesting current trajectories are contingent not inevitable

## Limitations & Critiques
- **Swedish specificity limits generalizability**: Focus on Scandinavian welfare state model may not transfer to other political-economic contexts
- **Non-formal education underexplored**: Historical focus primarily on enlightenment ambitions rather than practical computer operation training
- **Resistance insufficiently examined**: Limited attention to actual worker organizing, strikes, or successful refusals of computerization
- **Gender analysis underdeveloped**: Brief mentions of women's conditional inclusion lack deeper feminist critique of technological change
- **Post-1997 period truncated**: Analysis ends before smartphone era, social media, and contemporary AI developments fully emerged
- **Outcome measures absent**: No assessment of whether educational interventions achieved stated goals of citizen control or employment
- **Global South perspectives missing**: Analysis centers Western/Nordic contexts with insufficient attention to differential global impacts
- **Alternative imaginaries undertheorized**: While identifying paths not taken, limited analysis of why certain alternatives failed to materialize
