# Automating Teacher Work A History of the Politics

## Summary
This historical study examines how automation in Swedish education (1957-present) has been framed in relation to teachers’ work. Drawing on archival and digital materials, the authors reveal how promises of labor-saving automation have been repeatedly promoted while research shows automation always depends on extensive human labor. By contrasting policy elements across decades, they demonstrate how debates and technologies are dynamically established and naturalized over time, potentially silencing critical discussion about what automation and AI mean for teachers’ work and public education. The study shows automation debates align with familiar ‘techno-solutionist’ EdTech histories, but scale and impact are shifting as global platform infrastructures integrate into education, raising questions about how critical debate remains possible under such circumstances.

## Key Concepts
- **Automation of Teacher Work**: Technologies aimed at replacing or streamlining teachers’ labor, historically framed as efficient and labor-saving but consistently shown to increase workload
- **Techno-Solutionism**: Assumption that technological solutions are superior to other interventions for educational challenges
- **Policy Assemblage**: Gathering of political imaginations, rationalities, technologies, infrastructures toward steering individuals in particular directions
- **Fauxtomation**: Human labor covering up for automation shortcomings, creating illusion of machine autonomy
- **Digital Labor**: Often voluntary and unpaid work teachers do on platforms beyond official working hours
- **The Exceptional vs The Mundane**: Extraordinary hopes/fears about AI versus taken-for-granted automation as installed infrastructure

## Theoretical Framework
- **Foucauldian Power Analysis**: How powers operate contingently over time through arrangements
- **Science and Technology Studies (STS)**: Analyzing how technologies appear, reappear, or disappear over time
- **Feminist Technology Studies**: Teachers as gendered, feminized workforce experiencing rearranged labor
- **Platform Capitalism**: Private global platform industries transforming public education institutions

## Methods
Historical document analysis using Swedish context (1957-present). Materials include government proposals, news, teacher-press debates, popular science reviews. Analytical framing contrasts "the exceptional" (extraordinary hopes/fears) versus "the mundane" (taken-for-granted automation). Three dimensions: discourses, governing rationalities, inscriptions of automation technologies.

## Main Arguments
1. **Labor Paradox**: Despite promises since 1950s, automation rarely reduces work; increases workload and rearranges how work is performed
2. **Machine-Centered Work**: Semi-automated work becomes centered on machines demanding adaptable, flexible workforce
3. **Historical Repetition with Escalation**: Debates align with techno-solutionist patterns but scale shifting with global platform infrastructures
4. **Hidden Human Dependencies**: AI automation makes inexplicable errors requiring extensive human judgment
5. **Platform Labor Extraction**: Teachers’ digital labor extends beyond official hours, aligning with platform capitalism’s unpaid labor model

## Limitations & Critiques
- **Geographic Scope**: Swedish context; generalizability to other systems unclear
- **Material Selection**: May over-represent policy voices, underrepresent teachers’ lived experiences
- **Missing Empirical Work Data**: Analyzes discourses but doesn’t directly measure actual workload changes
- **Resistance Practices**: Mentions resistance but doesn’t deeply analyze teacher organizing or alternatives
