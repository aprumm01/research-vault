# Artificial intelligence and work transformations - integrating sensemaking and workplace learning perspectives

## Overview of the Document

This paper was authored by Annika Engström from Jönköping University's School of Engineering, Daniel Pittino from Jönköping International Business School and University of Udine, and Alice Mohlin, Anette Johansson, and Nina Edh Mirzaei from Jönköping University's School of Engineering. Published in 2024 in Information Technology & People (Volume 37, Number 7, pages 2441-2461), this empirical qualitative study forms part of a special issue on "Sharing Work with AI." The research was funded by the Knowledge Foundation and conducted through the AFAIR project examining Knowledge Intensive Product Realization (SPARK) at Jönköping University between 2021-2024. The authors represent an interdisciplinary team combining engineering, business, and organizational learning expertise to study AI adoption in Swedish manufacturing contexts.

The document matters because it addresses a critical gap in AI adoption research: understanding how organizational members initially make sense of AI before implementation occurs, and how this prospective sensemaking shapes subsequent learning pathways. As the authors note, "AI's unique characteristics question the traditional assumptions regarding digital innovation" and "introduce novel uncertainties about what demands will arise related to enhanced organizational sensemaking" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 97). Given that many organizations stand at the threshold of AI adoption without clear roadmaps, understanding these initial cognitive processes becomes crucial for successful integration. The research challenges assumptions that AI adoption follows predictable technology acceptance patterns, revealing instead that how people perceive AI features—as abstract versus concrete—fundamentally shapes whether organizations pursue exploitative (top-down, adaptive) or explorative (bottom-up, developmental) learning modes.

The paper contributes an integrated theoretical framework combining sensemaking theory (Weick, Griffith) with organizational learning theory (Crossan, March, Ellström) applied specifically to AI adoption contexts. This integration reveals tensions between passive "waiting for directions" mindsets triggered by abstract AI perceptions and proactive "envisioning challenges and potentials" approaches triggered by concrete AI features. The socio-technical implications are significant: when AI remains abstract, organizations risk stagnation and missed learning opportunities; when AI becomes concrete through direct engagement, collaborative human-AI systems emerge that support exploration and innovation.

## Research Overview

The central research questions ask: (RQ1) How do features of AI technology affect the initial sensemaking that organizational members activate when anticipating AI adoption in their workplace? and (RQ2) What are the possible consequences of the initial sensemaking process of AI adoption on workplace learning paths? The research was conducted by an interdisciplinary team at Jönköping University in Sweden between March and June 2021 across four large Swedish manufacturing companies during the early stages of their AI transformation journeys.

The study employed an explorative qualitative methodology using focus groups as the primary data collection method. As the authors explain, "We adopted an explorative qualitative and interactive approach to capture free fantasies and imaginative ideas of AI among people within the industry" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 36). The methodological design involved 23 focus groups held at four Swedish manufacturing companies (Bravo, Charlie, Foxtrot, Sierra) with 114 total participants. Each focus group included four to six employees and lasted 45-60 minutes conducted online via Microsoft Teams due to COVID-19 restrictions.

The research design used a "pyramid" approach where the first four focus groups (one per company) were facilitated by project researchers and included company leaders involved in digitalization initiatives. Following each session, researchers conducted 30-minute training sessions teaching participants how to moderate focus groups themselves. These trained moderators then conducted additional focus groups within their organizations using identical questions and structures, creating a cascading data collection process that captured diverse organizational voices while maintaining methodological consistency.

Focus group questions included: What is your spontaneous reaction when you hear the concept of AI? What do you think AI is in practice? In what applications can AI be applied within your organization? What can AI be used for near your own work area? What consequences could AI have for your organization or work? How can your organization prepare you in the best way for the application of AI? These open-ended questions allowed participants to freely reflect and share ideas without being steered by predetermined frameworks.

Data analysis followed the Gioia methodology's three-step coding procedure. First-order coding generated 164 concepts from participants' own terminology. Second-order coding grouped these into nine themes based on similarities and differences. Third-order aggregation produced two dimensions: (1) "Waiting for Directions to Determine How We Adopt AI in the Workplace" (triggered by abstract AI features) and (2) "Envisioning Challenges and Potentials of AI in the Workplace" (triggered by concrete AI features). As the authors note, "This analysis procedure was not linear but dynamic and iterative, inspired by Gioia et al. (2013). First-order concepts and second-order themes were constantly renegotiated according to the research process and discoveries of central meaning" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 529).

Key research concepts include initial sensemaking (early-stage cognitive processes when encountering novel phenomena), prospective sensemaking (contemplating future possibilities rather than interpreting past events), abstract features of AI (underlying principles, algorithms, machine learning processes, ethical considerations, predictive analytics not directly observable), concrete features of AI (tangible, directly observable components like user interfaces, chatbots, specific outcomes, physical robots, automation of specific tasks), feed-forward learning flows (explorative, bottom-up developmental learning from individuals to organizations), and feed-back learning flows (exploitative, top-down adaptive learning from organizational systems to individuals).

The theoretical lens integrates Griffith's (1999) work on technology features as sensemaking triggers, Weick's (1995) foundational sensemaking theory extended through Goto's (2022) prospective sensemaking, Crossan et al.'s (1999) organizational learning framework distinguishing feed-forward and feed-back flows, and March's (1991) exploration-exploitation distinction applied to workplace learning contexts.

## Theories of Knowledge

**Weick's sensemaking theory** provides the foundational framework, emphasizing "the cognitive processes through which individuals within organizations grasp and interpret complex and ambiguous situations" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 206). Originally conceived as retrospective (interpreting past actions), the paper extends this through **prospective sensemaking**: "Recent research has... extended this perspective by acknowledging that sensemaking can also involve contemplating the future by considering how prospective actions or inactions might influence the way both oneself and others interpret events" (p. 237, citing Goto, 2022; Stigliani & Ravasi, 2012). This future-oriented sensemaking becomes crucial when organizations face novel technologies where past experience provides limited guidance.

**Griffith's (1999) theory of technology features as sensemaking triggers** structures the core analytical distinction. Griffith argued that concrete features "can be directly and specifically described and verified (literally on the floor), while abstract features are described more indirectly or generally and tend to take on imaginative forms such as visions or fantasies" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 211). Applied to AI, abstract features include complex algorithms, machine learning methodologies, predictive analytics, conceptual understanding of AI cognitive capabilities, and ethical implications around data privacy and AI principles. Concrete features include graphical user interfaces, chatbots providing real-time communication, specific visible outcomes like image recognition accuracy, physical robots in workplaces, automation of repetitive tasks, and AI-driven productivity tools. This distinction matters because "the perceived features of AI technologies can be viewed as being concrete... or so-called abstract features [that] are not directly observable or actionable" (p. 110).

**Crossan, Lane, and White's (1999) organizational learning framework** provides the structure for understanding learning pathways. The framework conceptualizes "two completely different learning logics, contradictory in their character: (1) Learning from instructions, based on cognitive processes in the organization, document structures, and things that have been thought out and decided already (thinking before acting); and (2) Learning from experiences and actions and learning from disturbances of routinized work (acting before thinking)" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 166, citing Ellström, 2006). These manifest as "feed-forward" flows (explorative, bottom-up, from individual insights to organizational knowledge) and "feed-back" flows (exploitative, top-down, from organizational systems to individual application). The authors note these "might interfere and compete for focus and resources while simultaneously being interdependent and equally important" (p. 183).

**March's (1991) exploration-exploitation theory** underpins the learning mode distinction. Exploration "in which discovery and innovation are key, is characterized by the unknown, uncertainty, and experimentation," while exploitation "is characterized by what is already known and executed; in exploitation, rules and agreed-upon procedures must be followed and adopted, and stability, efficiency, and repetition are key" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 179). The authors treat these as dualities rather than dichotomies, meaning they coexist and interact rather than being mutually exclusive choices.

**Ellström's (2006) workplace learning theory** distinguishes adaptive learning (following established procedures, top-down instruction) from developmental learning (reflective, critical thinking, exploring new knowledge). As the authors explain, developmental learning "reflects on current practices, supports critical thinking, and explores new knowledge in the workplace," while adaptive learning focuses on "exploitation of current and existing knowledge" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 177). This framework helps interpret how AI perception shapes which learning mode becomes activated.

**Socio-Technical Systems (STS) theory** from Trist and Bamforth (1951) appears as the framework needing expansion. Traditional STS emphasizes "the interaction between technology and human systems to optimize organizational outcomes," but the authors argue "AI technologies present a unique challenge as they can act as agents within these systems, not just as tools" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 1024). The paper proposes viewing AI as "not merely embedded in social systems but are active participants that reshape these systems," requiring "an expanded view of Socio-Technical Systems (STS) theory... to account for AI's role not merely as a tool but as an active participant within organizational ecosystems" (p. 147).

**Cognitive framing theory** from Nadkarni and Narayanan (2007) explains how sensemaking influences learning: "cognitive framing also guides its interpretation. This is critical for workplace learning because it affects the meaning that is ascribed to new information or experiences. It guides the formation of beliefs and heuristics regarding cause-effect relationships and 'successful' paths in decision-making" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 252). Frames either "highlight or obscure important information" (p. 250), making initial sensemaking consequential for what learning opportunities get noticed and pursued.

**Technology adoption models** (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al., 2003) appear as frameworks requiring revision. The paper argues these models "primarily focus on perceived usefulness and ease of use," but "our findings suggest that the initial sensemaking about technology—whether it is perceived as an abstract or concrete entity—can significantly influence these perceptions" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 1041). This suggests traditional adoption models miss a crucial antecedent stage where cognitive reactions to technology features shape subsequent perceived usefulness and ease evaluations.

## Central Arguments

The authors advance a central argument that perceived features of AI technology—whether abstract or concrete—trigger different initial sensemaking processes that in turn activate either exploitative or explorative workplace learning modes, with significant consequences for organizational AI adoption success. As they state: "sensemaking triggered by abstract features of AI technology mainly pointed to an exploitative learning path... sensemaking triggered by concrete features of the technology mainly pointed to explorative paths, where socio-technical processes appear to be crucial" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 43).

The first major sub-argument concerns how abstract AI features trigger passive, top-down learning orientations. The authors found that "themes of the first dimension, revolving around the abstract features of AI in the workplace, can be interpreted as instances of sensemaking fostering a feed-back flow and an exploitative mode of learning, revealing a pattern where employees await directions rather than seeking out learning opportunities themselves" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 793). Specifically, when participants discussed AI in abstract terms—complexity of algorithms, machine learning methodologies, predictive analytics, ethical implications—they expressed sentiments like "AI is interesting, but it is not really for us yet" and "The idea of AI is overwhelming for many" (p. 602). This abstraction fostered four sub-themes: ambiguous signs of resistance (diffuse unpleasant feelings about AI adoption happening "somewhere else"), missing data infrastructure (perception that current systems aren't ready for AI), still on the starting blocks (organizations haven't established methods for implementation), and unimaginable and remote (AI as incomprehensible future transformation). The consequence is that "rather than engaging proactively with AI, there is a tendency among employees to wait for organizational leaders to demarcate the boundaries of its use" (p. 806).

The second argument addresses how concrete AI features activate explorative, bottom-up learning. When participants engaged with tangible AI manifestations—chatbots, automation of specific tasks, physical robots, visible outcomes like image recognition—they expressed curiosity and initiative: "AI can process data for me" and "AI can relieve us from boring parts of our work" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 690). This concreteness generated five sub-themes: AI assisting humans (subordinate tool performing repetitive tasks), AI independent of humans (autonomous physical robots from movies/media), superhuman capabilities (handling data and making unbiased decisions better than humans), humans as barriers (human limitations obstructing AI potential), and trust and control in the relationship (need for mutual understanding between humans and machines). The authors argue that "the visibility of AI in tasks such as automating repetitive processes, enhancing decision-making through data analytics, and even acting independently in certain operations, transforms it from a nebulous concept into a tangible asset that employees can interact with, learn from, and influence" (p. 863).

The third argument concerns socio-technical integration as essential for explorative learning. The authors emphasize that "the focus groups clearly point out the socio-technical system as crucial for explorative processes" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 885). When AI becomes concrete, "participants do not perceive themselves as merely passive recipients of predetermined instructions but become 'active explorers,' seeking to understand how AI's capabilities can be harnessed in their specific work contexts" (p. 859). This requires "the integration of the social and technical systems in AI adoption" (p. 138), where "AI and human actions are intricately linked, thus requiring opportunities for experimentation and exploratory learning" (p. 1100). The implication is that successful AI adoption depends on creating conditions where employees directly engage with AI functionalities rather than waiting for abstract concepts to be translated into procedures.

The fourth argument addresses consequences for learning activation. The authors contend that "the role of (prospective) sensemaking is pivotal in activating learning flows, as it entails the activation of cognitive frames, which in turn determine what information members of an organization pay attention to and how they prioritize it" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 243). When abstract features dominate, "the members of the focus groups do not see their own role and responsibility to prioritize learning for change," leading to "inaction and stagnation" (p. 824). Conversely, concrete features "feeds into an exploratory learning mode" where "insights gained from hands-on experience with AI applications inform broader workplace learning and adaptation strategies" (p. 887).

The fifth argument proposes revising traditional frameworks. The authors argue that "traditional workplace technology adoption models need expansion to incorporate sensemaking processes, as these initial cognitive reactions to technology crucially predict adoption success" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 151). They advocate for "an expanded view of Socio-Technical Systems (STS) theory... emphasizing the co-evolution of human and AI agents" (p. 148) and suggest that "STS theory needs to consider not just the coexistence but also the co-evolution of human and technological agents within organizations" (p. 1034). This positions AI as communicative agents "that actively participate in social interactions, thereby fostering a network of connectivity" (p. 984).

The sixth argument addresses practical implications for managing adoption. The authors contend that "our findings make a case for allowing exploratory sensemaking during AI adoption rather than viewing the absence of ambiguity as an unequivocal good" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 1070). They recommend "open dialogues with employees about the potential and challenges of AI technologies" conducted "within a psychologically safe environment... crucial for fostering an innovative and healthy organizational culture" (p. 1062). The practical guidance is that "adopting AI should be approached as the creation of a new socio-technical system where AI collaborates with human efforts, transforming it from a mere technological addition into a fundamental part of the organizational workflow" (p. 1067).

## Evidence

The evidence comes from 23 focus groups involving 114 participants across four Swedish manufacturing companies (Bravo, Charlie, Foxtrot, Sierra) conducted during March-June 2021. The Gioia methodology analysis generated 164 first-order concepts, nine second-order themes, and two aggregated dimensions from transcribed focus group discussions. Table 2 in the paper shows the distribution of evidence: "ambiguous signs of resistance" generated from 60 references across 10 focus groups; "missing data infrastructure" from 15 references across 9 focus groups; "still on the starting blocks" from 207 references across all 23 groups; "unimaginable and remote" from 142 references across all 23 groups; "AI assisting humans" from 325 references across all 23 groups (the most frequently discussed theme); "AI independent of humans" from 84 references across 23 groups; "superhuman capabilities" from 158 references across 23 groups; "humans as barriers" from 92 references across 21 groups; and "trust and control" from 88 references across 22 groups (Engström et al., 2024, p. 561-573).

Specific quotes provide concrete evidence for abstract feature themes. For ambiguous resistance, participants said: "It is a very big concept that you throw around, and I can imagine that people are perhaps afraid of the concept as such" and "Maybe not us, who think this is exciting, but many other people" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 601-602). For missing infrastructure: "We cannot start with AI learning until our data is in order" and "The gaps in our data constitute an uphill battle" (p. 613-625). For being on starting blocks: "And so it is a journey, but at the same time you have to start the journey, otherwise you will never get anywhere" and "I do not think we are ready, at all, thinking about that" (p. 653-654). For unimaginable qualities: "Because I do not think we are there, at least my imagination is not good enough to see that" and "We are not capable, because we are not trained or we are not made to really think outside the box, on how AI will look like tomorrow" (p. 671-673).

Evidence for concrete feature themes shows different engagement patterns. For AI assisting humans: "Really, what I do today when processing data, it is like software, it is only numbers in and out. I think it will be very easy to construct algorithms so that AI can do that" and "If we could get AI that gets us out of the boring parts of our work, that would be nice, right?" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 698-704). For AI independence: "My spontaneous reaction is what you have seen in the movies, robots thinking for themselves" and "My original association was Terminator, I think, when the machines took control and went to war with the humans" (p. 712-714). For superhuman capabilities: "Like, making decisions about welfare payments or care queues and things like that, you can imagine many situations where a computer would probably make many adequate, correct, and statistically secure decisions" (p. 731-733). For humans as barriers: "The personal exchange and negotiations and all of that, it will never be replaced by AI" and "I do not think that 'business feeling' is possible to capture in an AI robot" (p. 750-752). For trust and control: "The faults could be severe if we give too much responsibility to an AI, and it goes off track of what is reasonable" and "We must build some kind of respect between each other here" (p. 784-787).

The research methods carry several important limitations. The sample consisted of four Swedish manufacturing companies, all in the introductory phase of AI adoption, limiting generalizability to other industries, cultural contexts, or adoption stages. As the authors acknowledge, "The results in this paper are based on findings from four of the participating companies" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 288), and companies were selected based on "their history of successful research collaborations" (p. 287), potentially introducing selection bias toward organizations already disposed to reflexive learning practices.

The timing during COVID-19 restrictions meant all focus groups occurred online via Microsoft Teams rather than in-person, potentially affecting group dynamics and depth of interaction. The "pyramid" data collection approach, while innovative for scaling data collection, introduced variability as company representatives received only 30-minute training before moderating their own focus groups. While this captured more voices, it sacrificed some methodological control compared to researcher-facilitated groups throughout.

The cross-sectional design captures initial prospective sensemaking but cannot track how these perceptions evolved during actual implementation. The authors acknowledge this limitation: "Future research should aim to uncover the actual learning processes that follow the initial/prospective sensemaking stage by collecting additional longitudinal data accounting for the during and post AI implementation phases" (Engström et al., 2024, p. 1107). Without longitudinal follow-up, causal claims about sensemaking leading to particular learning modes remain speculative rather than demonstrated.

The Gioia methodology, while rigorous for qualitative analysis, involves substantial interpretive judgment. The classification of features as "abstract" versus "concrete" required researchers to code participants' language into these categories, introducing potential for interpretive bias. Some AI characteristics might straddle both categories—for example, chatbots are concrete interfaces but embody abstract machine learning principles.

The focus group method elicits socially constructed accounts rather than capturing actual workplace behavior during AI interaction. Participants discussed imagined futures and fantasies about AI rather than reporting on direct experiences implementing specific AI systems. This prospective focus aligns with the research questions but means findings reflect anticipatory cognition rather than demonstrated learning outcomes.

## Conclusion

This research contributes an integrated framework demonstrating that how organizational members initially perceive and make sense of AI technology—as abstract versus concrete—fundamentally shapes whether exploitative or explorative workplace learning modes become activated, with significant consequences for AI adoption trajectories. The practical implication is clear: organizations that allow AI to remain abstract (complex algorithms, ethical considerations, future potential) while waiting for perfect conditions and top-down directives risk stagnation in exploitative patterns that miss learning opportunities. Conversely, organizations that make AI concrete through direct engagement with specific applications, tangible interfaces, and hands-on experimentation activate bottom-up explorative learning where socio-technical collaboration emerges organically.

The theoretical contribution lies in integrating sensemaking theory with organizational learning frameworks through the concept of prospective sensemaking. This integration reveals that cognitive reactions to technology features precede and shape traditional technology acceptance factors like perceived usefulness and ease of use. The framework also extends Socio-Technical Systems theory by positioning AI not merely as a tool embedded in social systems but as an active agent that reshapes organizational structures and workflows through co-evolution with human capabilities.

For practitioners, the findings suggest that managing AI adoption requires deliberate strategies to make AI concrete and experienceable rather than abstract and distant. This means creating opportunities for employees to directly interact with AI applications, experiment with possibilities, and develop trust through hands-on engagement. The research challenges the assumption that organizations should wait until infrastructure, data quality, and procedures are perfect before engaging with AI. Instead, the exploratory learning mode requires embracing ambiguity, allowing experimentation even with imperfect conditions, and fostering psychological safety for bottom-up innovation.

What remains open are questions about how these initial sensemaking patterns evolve through actual implementation. The cross-sectional design captures anticipatory cognition but cannot track whether explorative orientations persist through implementation challenges, whether abstract perceptions shift toward concrete understanding through experience, or whether organizations can deliberately influence which learning mode becomes dominant. Longitudinal research following organizations through full AI implementation cycles would reveal whether initial sensemaking proves durable or malleable.

Also unresolved is how to operationalize "making AI concrete" in contexts where AI capabilities genuinely are complex, ethically fraught, and difficult to specify precisely. The research suggests concreteness activates beneficial explorative learning, but some AI applications may resist easy concretization due to their inherent opacity, complexity, or ethical ambiguity. Balancing the need for tangible engagement against the reality that some AI features remain legitimately abstract presents practical challenges the framework doesn't fully address.

The socio-technical perspective requires further development regarding AI agency. The paper positions AI as an active participant in organizational systems, but the boundaries of this agency remain unclear. When does AI move from being a tool that humans control to being an agent with its own influence on learning processes and organizational outcomes? How should organizations design governance structures when both humans and AI systems participate in decision-making and learning? These questions about distributed agency in human-AI systems deserve continued theoretical and empirical attention.

## Discussion Questions

1. The study found that abstract AI features trigger exploitative learning while concrete features trigger explorative learning. But might this pattern reverse in some contexts—for example, could abstract ethical considerations about AI actually motivate explorative inquiry into alternative development approaches, while concrete automation of specific tasks leads to exploitative refinement of existing procedures? What determines which direction the relationship runs?

2. The authors recommend embracing ambiguity during AI adoption to foster explorative learning, yet organizations often face pressure from stakeholders for clear implementation plans and predictable outcomes. How can practitioners balance the exploratory experimentation the research advocates against legitimate organizational needs for planning, resource allocation, and accountability to investors or regulators?

3. The research treats exploration and exploitation as dualities that should be balanced, but the findings suggest they're triggered by different AI perceptions (abstract vs concrete). Does this mean organizations must somehow maintain simultaneous abstract and concrete perceptions of AI to balance learning modes? Or does successful adoption require sequencing—starting with concreteness to activate exploration, then abstracting to consolidate through exploitation?

4. The paper positions AI as an active agent in socio-technical systems rather than merely a tool, but this agency attribution itself emerged from participants' sensemaking. Is AI agency an objective property that organizations must accommodate, or is it a socially constructed interpretation that organizations can intentionally shape through how they frame and present AI capabilities? What are the ethical implications if AI agency is more constructed than inherent?
