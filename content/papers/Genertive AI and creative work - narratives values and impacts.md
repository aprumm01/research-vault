# Genertive AI and creative work - narratives, values, and impacts

## Summary
CY] 6 Feb 2025 Generative AI and Creative Work: Narratives, Values, and Impacts BAPTISTE CARAMIAUX, CNRS, Sorbonne Université, ISIR, France KATE CRAWFORD, Microsoft Research, USA Q VERA LIAO, Microsoft Research, Canada GONZALO RAMOS, Microsoft Research, USA JENNY WILLIAMS, Microsoft, USA Generative AI has gained a significant foothold in the creative and artistic sectors In this context, the concept of creative work is influenced by discourses originating from technological stakeholders and mainstream media.

## Key Concepts
- **Competing narratives of creative work**: Divergent discourses about creative labor and value originating from technology companies, media, and creative communities themselves, shaping how generative AI's role in creative sectors understood and contested
- **Technological framing of creativity**: How tech industry narratives position creativity as enhanceable through AI tools, often emphasizing efficiency, democratization, and augmentation while potentially obscuring labor displacement, devaluation, and power concentration concerns
- **Creative worker perspectives and resistances**: Actual creative professionals' experiences, values, and concerns about AI integration often differing from technological optimism narratives, including anxieties about authorship, craft devaluation, economic precarity, and loss of creative autonomy
- **Values embedded in AI creative tools**: Design choices, capabilities, limitations, and business models of generative AI systems reflect particular assumptions about what creativity is, who creative workers are, and what creative labor should entail—values often unmarked but consequential
- **Material impacts on creative labor**: Concrete economic, professional, and cultural effects of generative AI adoption in creative sectors including job restructuring, compensation changes, skill requirement shifts, and transformations in creative practice and professional identity

## Theoretical Framework
**Critical Discourse Analysis**: Examining how language, narratives, and framing devices shape understanding of generative AI in creative work, revealing power dynamics in whose perspectives legitimized, what questions asked/avoided, and how particular visions of AI-creative futures naturalized or contested.

**Political Economy of Creative Labor**: Analyzing generative AI within broader context of creative sector labor conditions—precarity, platform capitalism, intellectual property regimes, winner-take-all markets—revealing how AI tools reinforce or disrupt existing power structures and economic arrangements.

## Methods
**Narrative analysis methodology**: Systematic examination of diverse discourse sources (technology company communications, media coverage, creative worker testimonies, industry analyses) identifying recurring themes, contradictions, silences, and framings that construct meaning around generative AI and creative work.

**Multi-stakeholder perspective integration**: Deliberately incorporating voices from technology developers, creative professionals, industry analysts, and affected communities rather than privileging single perspective, revealing tensions and contested terrain in how generative AI's creative sector role understood.

## Main Arguments
- **Technological narratives systematically underplay creative labor disruption while overplaying democratization**: Tech industry and mainstream media discourse frequently frames generative AI as democratizing creativity—enabling non-professionals to create, reducing technical barriers, expanding creative access. However, this framing often obscures impacts on existing creative workers whose livelihoods depend on skills AI automates, concentrates creative power with platform owners controlling models, and potentially floods creative markets devaluing all creative work. "Democratization" narrative serves marketing and legitimation functions while sidelining labor implications.

- **Dominant narratives embed particular values about creativity often contradicting creative workers' values**: AI creative tool design reflects assumptions: creativity primarily about output generation (not process), speed and volume valuable (versus craft and contemplation), individual creation (versus collaboration), and commodifiable artifacts (versus meaning-making). These values contrast with many creative professionals' emphasis on iterative refinement, embodied practice, contextual appropriateness, and creative work as identity and community participation. Misalignment creates adoption resistance and professional identity threats beyond simple technological capability questions.

- **Creative workers experience AI integration as compound threat—economic, professional, and existential**: Material concerns about income loss, job restructuring, and market saturation intersect with professional anxieties about skill devaluation, credibility erosion when outputs AI-replicable, and existential questions about creative identity and purpose when machines handle tasks previously defining creative work. Dismissing concerns as Luddism or technophobia misses how AI fundamentally challenges what it means to be creative worker, not just efficiency of creative production.

- **Power concentration intensifies as platforms control creative infrastructure**: Generative AI shifts creative production infrastructure toward platform-owned models, creating dependencies where creative workers increasingly reliant on tools controlled by few technology companies. This concentrates power to set terms (pricing, access, copyright treatment of training data and outputs), shape creative possibilities (what models trained on, what outputs permitted), and extract value from creative labor. Platform power extends beyond tools to potentially reshape entire creative sector economics and cultural production.

- **Diverse creative sector experiences require nuanced rather than monolithic analysis**: Impacts vary substantially across creative domains (writing versus visual art versus music), work arrangements (employee versus freelance versus hobbyist), and positionalities (established versus emerging creators, Western versus Global South, commercial versus community-oriented). Generalizations about AI "helping" or "harming" creativity obscure how technology's effects mediated by existing inequalities, market structures, and cultural contexts—requiring attention to who benefits and who bears costs within heterogeneous creative sectors.

## Limitations & Critiques
**Temporal snapshot in evolving discourse**: Narrative analysis captures particular moment in rapidly shifting discourse around generative AI and creativity, but narratives, power dynamics, and material conditions continue evolving as technology develops, adoption patterns change, and cultural understanding deepens—limiting prescriptive validity.

**Potential overemphasis on discourse versus material conditions**: While revealing how narratives shape understanding, analysis risk focusing on talk about creative work at expense of examining actual labor transformations—employment data, income changes, practice modifications, market restructurings—that constitute AI's material impacts independent of discursive framing.

**Western-centric perspective**: Analysis predominantly reflects discourse and creative labor conditions in North American and European contexts, with insufficient attention to how generative AI narratives and impacts differ in other cultural contexts, creative traditions, economic arrangements, and technological development models.

**Limited creative worker voice diversity**: While claiming to center creative perspectives, unclear whether full range of creative worker experiences represented—potentially oversampling visible resistant voices while missing silently adapting workers, enthusiastic adopters, or marginalized creators with different priorities than established professionals.

**Underspecified recommendations**: While identifying problematic narratives and values, provides insufficient concrete guidance on alternative frameworks, policy interventions, or design approaches that would address identified problems—critical analysis not matched with constructive proposals.
