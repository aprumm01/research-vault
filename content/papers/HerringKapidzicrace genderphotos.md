# Herring.Kapidzic.race gender.photos

## Summary
This study analyzes how teens represent themselves through their profile photographs on a popular nonymous chat site Using visual content analysis methods, we analyzed 400 profile photographs, controlling for the self-reported gender and the apparent race of the photographic subject The analysis finds significant differences in gaze, posture, dress, and distance from the camera according to gender and race, although racial differences are stronger for boys than for girls.

## Key Concepts
- **Visual self-representation** by teens through profile photographs on nonymous chat sites
- **Gender differences** in visual presentation: gaze, posture, dress, camera distance
- **Racial differences** in self-presentation, stronger for boys than girls
- Visual content analysis of digital self-presentation practices
- Intersection of gender and race in online identity construction
- Profile photographs as performative identity work

## Theoretical Framework
- **Visual content analysis**: Systematic coding of visual elements in photographs
- **Gender performance theory**: Self-presentation as gendered performance
- **Race and identity**: Racialized patterns in visual self-representation
- Computer-Mediated Communication (CMC) theory applied to visual identity

## Methods
content analysis

## Main Arguments
- Significant differences exist in teen self-presentation by gender (gaze, posture, dress, distance)
- Racial differences in visual self-presentation are significant, particularly for boys
- Gender and race intersect to shape online visual identity construction
- Profile photographs reflect and reproduce social categories and norms
- Visual analysis reveals patterns not captured by text-based CMC research
- Racial differences show gendered patterns (stronger for boys than girls)

## Limitations & Critiques
- Sample limited to single nonymous chat site (400 profile photographs)
- Control for self-reported gender and apparent race may not capture full complexity
- Static analysis of photographs may miss temporal evolution of self-presentation
- Generalizability to other platforms and contexts requires further research
- Coding apparent race from photographs raises methodological and ethical considerations
