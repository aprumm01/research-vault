# Catalyst for Creativity or a Hollow Trend A Cross-Level Perspective on The Role of Generative AI in Design

## Summary
Generative AI image creation tools have the potential to transform design education and practice, but raise critical concerns for creativity and ownership We leverage the 2022 launch of tools like Midjourney and DALL E as a point dividing design enthusiasts into pre- and post-tool learners.

## Key Concepts
- **Cross-level comparative approach**: Methodology leveraging the 2022 launch of generative AI tools as a stratification point dividing designers into pre-tool learners (experienced designers and senior students) and post-tool learners (junior students)
- **Value system rift**: Growing divide between experienced designers who are circumspect about loss of traditional creativity/foundational skills and junior designers more enthusiastic about AI adoption
- **Sawyer's eight characteristics of creativity**: Iteration, ambiguity, exploration, emergence, failure, deliberate intentionality, conscious reflection, and constraints—framework for evaluating how generative AI shapes creative processes
- **Creativity support tools (CST)**: Digital-interactive tools for enhancing creativity, traditionally keeping people in control vs. generative AI potentially shifting this dynamic with autonomous output production
- **Ownership ambiguity**: Blurred lines between originality and plagiarism when using generative AI, with junior students viewing AI as collaborative tool while others worry about authenticity
- **Marketability vs. affordance tension**: Growing demand for AI-related design skills contrasts with limited practical affordances of AI-generated outputs for achieving meaningful designs

## Theoretical Framework
The paper integrates creativity theory with HCI perspectives on AI collaboration:

1. **Models of creativity**: Examines generative AI's impact through traditional creativity frameworks—from Wallas' four-stage model (1926) to Sawyer's eight characteristics (nonlinear, iterative, improvisational). Focuses on whether AI alters or reinforces traditional creativity stages in design workflows

2. **Creativity evaluation criteria**: Uses three assessment dimensions—novelty (innovation), quality (appeal/feasibility), and contextual relevance (social/emotional connection)—to evaluate how AI-generated artifacts compare to human-created designs

3. **Actor Network Theory (Thibault et al.)**: Framework for studying AI's impact on creative ecosystems, theorizing AI's role as expanding, cloning, replacing, or surpassing human agency across labor, professionalization, and management levels

4. **Human-AI co-creation ethics**: Addresses ownership, collaboration vs. leadership roles, accountability, data concerns, and personification of co-creative AI systems (Rezwana & Maher, 2024)

The framework positions generative AI not merely as a tool but as an agent that potentially restructures the fundamental characteristics and stages of creative design work.

## Methods
Artifact-based interviews with N=28 designers stratified into three groups: (1) junior students (first/second-year undergraduates who began design education after 2022 GenAI launch), (2) senior students (third/fourth-year undergraduates and graduate students for whom GenAI was introduced midway), and (3) design professionals (practicing before GenAI developments). Participants from diverse design fields including UI/UX, graphic design, game art, typography, animation, industrial design. Interviews explored: design practice changes, ownership navigation, and educational curriculum implications. Analysis examined cross-level differences in perceptions, usage patterns, and concerns.

## Main Arguments
- **Fundamental rift in designer value systems emerges**: Experienced professionals express concern over erosion of traditional creativity and foundational design skills, while junior designers embrace AI potential enthusiastically. This divide extends to curriculum debates—juniors advocate early AI integration, while seniors and professionals stress foundational skills first, even suggesting restricted early access

- **Ownership becomes contested territory**: Design artifacts' authorship creates debate—junior students view AI as collaborative tool (shared ownership), while experienced designers worry about blurred originality/plagiarism lines. This raises questions about what constitutes authentic creative work and who deserves credit for AI-mediated outputs

- **Marketability-affordance tension reveals hollow trend**: Despite growing demand for AI-related design skills in job market, limited affordances of AI-generated outputs create practical limitations in achieving meaningful designs. All experience levels align on this concern, questioning whether current generative AI is genuinely useful or merely trendy

- **Generative AI disrupts Sawyer's creativity characteristics**: Traditional characteristics—iteration, deliberate intentionality, conscious reflection, failure as learning—become distorted when AI autonomously produces outputs. The shift from creativity support (human-controlled) to autonomous generation challenges fundamental assumptions about creative process

- **Cross-level experiences reveal developmental concerns**: Pre-tool learners (who developed foundational skills before AI) express different concerns than post-tool learners (who may rely on AI as crutch). This temporal stratification suggests generative AI's introduction timing significantly shapes designers' relationships with technology and craft

- **Education must navigate competing demands**: Design curricula face tension between preparing students for AI-integrated industry while ensuring they develop fundamental skills. The cross-level divide suggests no consensus on optimal integration approach

## Limitations & Critiques
- **2022 temporal cutoff may be artificial**: While convenient for stratification, the "before/after GenAI" division oversimplifies gradual technology adoption and assumes sharp discontinuity that may not reflect lived experiences

- **Sample size and diversity constraints**: N=28 participants, while providing rich qualitative insights, may not capture full range of perspectives across different design subdisciplines, cultural contexts, or institutional settings. Geographic diversity not specified

- **Self-selection and recruitment biases**: Participants likely those interested in discussing AI, potentially overrepresenting either enthusiasts or critics. Those neutral or unaffected by AI may be underrepresented

- **Rapid technology evolution**: Findings based on 2022-era tools (Midjourney, DALL-E 2, Stable Diffusion) may quickly become outdated as capabilities improve. Current tools' limitations may not reflect future affordances

- **Missing longitudinal dimension**: Cross-sectional comparison at one time point cannot track how individual designers' perspectives evolve with continued AI use and skill development. Junior students may develop concerns as expertise grows

- **Artifact-based interview limitations**: While grounding discussions in concrete work, this method may not capture broader process experiences, failed attempts, or work not deemed interview-worthy

- **Industry vs. education gap**: Professional designers' workplace constraints, client expectations, and competitive pressures differ significantly from student contexts, potentially confounding experience-level comparisons

- **Ownership framework underdeveloped**: While identifying ownership tensions, the paper provides limited analysis of legal, ethical, or practical frameworks for resolving authorship questions in AI-mediated design

- **"Hollow trend" conclusion may be premature**: Dismissing current AI as potentially hollow based on affordance limitations doesn't account for learning curves, emerging best practices, or complementary tool development that might address current shortcomings
