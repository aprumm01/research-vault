# Brush1998 Article EmbeddingCooperativeLearningIn

## Summary
Embedding Cooperative Learning into the Design of Integrated Learning Systems: Rationale and Guidelines [] Thomas A Brush An Integrated Learning Systera (ILS) is an advanced computer-based instructional system, generally consisting of a set of computerized courseware covering several grade levels and content areas, and complex classroom management and reporting features Although ILSs have become increasingly popular in schools over the past five to ten years, they introduce several potential factors that could have negative effects on students' academic and social growth.

## Key Concepts
- **Integrated Learning Systems (ILS)**: Advanced computer-based instructional systems consisting of computerized courseware covering multiple grade levels and content areas, with complex classroom management and reporting features, designed primarily for individualized instruction
- **Cooperative learning**: Learning situations where students working in groups can achieve instructional activity goals only if other students in their group also achieve those goals (Deutsch, 1962), contrasted with individualistic learning
- **Positive interdependence**: The perception that students are linked so they cannot succeed unless their groupmates do; includes goal, reward, resource, role, identity, and environmental interdependence
- **Individual accountability**: Requirement that each cooperative group member must master the information for which the group is responsible, typically assessed through individual testing
- **Collaborative skills**: Communication abilities, trust-building, leadership, and conflict management skills necessary for effective group work, requiring explicit teaching and reinforcement
- **Free rider effect**: When less able group members allow others to complete the majority of activities; contrasted with "sucker effect" where more able members expend less effort to avoid doing all the work

## Theoretical Framework
The paper integrates two primary theoretical traditions:

1. **Individualized instruction theory (Behaviorist tradition)**: ILSs based on meeting unique needs of each individual through placement testing, tailored instruction and feedback, practice with immediate feedback, mastery testing, and remediation—perpetuating behaviorist approaches to learning

2. **Cooperative learning theory (Social constructivist tradition)**: Based on Deutsch's (1962) conceptualization and developed through models like Johnson & Johnson's Learning Together, Slavin's Student Team Learning, and Aronson's Jigsaw—emphasizing social interaction and dialogue as essential for learning

The theoretical contribution proposes integrating these traditionally opposing frameworks, arguing that cooperative learning can address ILS deficiencies while maintaining benefits of individualized pacing and assessment.

## Methods
Literature review and conceptual analysis. Examines research on ILSs, cooperative learning, and computer-assisted instruction (CAI) to develop design guidelines. Reviews meta-analyses (Becker 1992, 1994; Slavin 1983, 1987, 1995), empirical studies on ILS effectiveness, and cooperative learning research to synthesize principles for embedding cooperative learning into ILS design. Provides conceptual framework rather than empirical testing.

## Main Arguments
- **ILSs introduce problematic factors despite benefits**: While showing moderate positive effects on achievement and offering implementation ease, long-term individualized ILS use creates (a) de-emphasis of affective outcomes and student isolation, (b) lack of teacher involvement in curriculum planning/delivery, and (c) disparate effects based on academic level (helping low/high achievers but not middle achievers)

- **Current ILS design perpetuates isolation**: Most ILSs designed based on theory that learning is best facilitated by meeting individual needs rather than providing social interaction opportunities. This design includes placement testing, individualized instruction/feedback, practice activities, mastery testing, and remediation—all completed alone at workstations

- **Cooperative learning addresses ILS limitations**: Research demonstrates cooperative learning improves social interaction skills (Lloyd et al., 1988), promotes teacher involvement (Hertz-Lazarowitz & Schachar, 1990), and has positive academic effects for students of all ability levels (Slavin, 1991)—directly addressing ILS problems

- **Three key components must be embedded**: For effective integration, ILS design must incorporate (1) positive interdependence through goal/reward structures and role assignments, (2) individual accountability through testing and evaluation, and (3) explicit teaching of collaborative skills through pre-instructional activities and embedded prompts

- **Current ILSs lack cooperative learning support**: No ILS systems overtly embed cooperative learning opportunities into courseware, and few provide procedures or materials to assist teachers with integration. This absence creates implementation barriers even when benefits are recognized

- **Design guidelines can facilitate integration**: Specific strategies proposed include: structuring group goals/rewards based on individual achievement, dividing resources and roles among group members, incorporating peer tutoring opportunities, embedding collaborative skill prompts into courseware, and providing teacher guidance for managing cooperative ILS sessions

## Limitations & Critiques
- **Limited empirical validation**: As a conceptual paper proposing design guidelines, it lacks empirical testing of whether the suggested strategies actually improve ILS outcomes when implemented. Recommendations based on extrapolating from CAI research to ILS contexts

- **Implementation feasibility underexplored**: While noting that teachers face "logistical difficulties" with implementing cooperative learning in computer labs (Beyer, 1993), the paper provides limited guidance on overcoming practical constraints like physical space, group scheduling, and computer-to-student ratios

- **Tension between individualization and cooperation unresolved**: The fundamental contradiction between ILS's individualized pacing (students progressing at different rates through different content) and cooperative learning's group cohesion (students working together on shared tasks) is not fully addressed

- **Teacher role ambiguity**: While arguing for increased teacher involvement, the paper provides limited specifics on what teachers should do during cooperative ILS sessions beyond monitoring and providing general support

- **Technology constraints ignored**: 1998 publication predates modern collaborative technologies; design guidelines assume individual workstations connected via LAN without considering how technology itself might facilitate or constrain cooperative interaction

- **"Turnkey" implementation critique may be overstated**: The criticism of ILS ease-of-implementation assumes this necessarily leads to teacher disengagement, but doesn't fully consider whether structured cooperative learning approaches might also reduce teacher burden while maintaining involvement

- **Assessment complexities**: Combining individual accountability (measured through individual testing) with group rewards creates potential fairness issues and assessment validity concerns not thoroughly examined

- **Missing alternative explanations for disparate achievement effects**: The finding that ILSs benefit low/high achievers but not middle achievers attributed to pacing/level needs, but alternative explanations (e.g., motivation, content design, ceiling effects) not fully explored
