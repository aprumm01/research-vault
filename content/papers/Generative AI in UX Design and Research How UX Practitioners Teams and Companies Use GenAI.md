# Generative AI in User Experience Design and Research: How Do UX Practitioners, Teams, and Companies Use GenAI in Industry?

## Summary
This DIS 2024 paper presents findings from interviews with 24 UX practitioners across multiple countries and companies, examining how GenAI is actually used — or not used — in UX practice at individual, team, and company levels. The study reveals a significant policy vacuum (most companies lack formal GenAI guidelines), an individual-centered rather than team-centered adoption pattern, and a persistent gap between GenAI's usefulness for writing-based tasks and its limitations for core design activities like wireframing and prototyping.

## Key Concepts
- **UX GenAI adoption gap**: The disconnect between high individual adoption of GenAI for text-based tasks and low adoption for visual/design-specific activities (wireframing, prototyping, graphic design)
- **Company policy vacuum**: The finding that most companies lack formal GenAI use policies, instead informally advising caution or delegating responsibility to individual employees
- **Team-level GenAI practices**: The absence of shared, coordinated team approaches to GenAI — practitioners report using it individually and informally, without team-wide protocols
- **Prompt engineering as a skill gap**: UX practitioners' reported difficulty crafting effective prompts and evaluating output quality — a skills deficit they want addressed through training
- **Design for AI vs. design with AI**: The conceptual distinction between designing AI-powered products (design for AI) and using AI as a tool within the design process (design with AI) — this paper focuses on the latter
- **Output quality assessment**: The challenge UX practitioners face in evaluating whether GenAI-generated user research artifacts, personas, or content are accurate and usable

## Theoretical Framework
The paper is positioned within the tradition of practice-based UX research, using qualitative interviews to understand how technology adoption occurs in real professional contexts rather than in idealized or controlled conditions. It draws on prior AI-in-UX literature that has proposed tools and methods for AI-enhanced design, but extends that literature by asking how practitioners are actually using (or not using) GenAI in industry — an empirical grounding that the existing literature largely lacks.

The analysis framework distinguishes three levels of adoption: company (policy and governance), team (shared practices and coordination), and individual (personal workflows and preferences). This multilevel structure provides analytical clarity and allows the findings to speak to different audiences — organizational leaders, team leads, and individual practitioners.

## Methods
Qualitative interview study with 24 UX practitioners recruited from eight different countries, with varying seniority levels (junior to senior/management) and roles (UX designer, UX researcher, product designer, UX manager). All participants were employed by different companies ranging from startups to large corporations. Interviews were conducted online and analyzed using qualitative content analysis, which produced 10 themes grouped under three major dimensions corresponding to the three research questions. Participant sampling was purposive to maximize diversity of perspectives.

## Main Arguments
- **Companies lack formal GenAI policies**: The majority of participants reported their companies had no official guidance on GenAI use, with informal messages advising caution (especially around data privacy) being the most common form of governance — leaving practitioners without clear guardrails.
- **GenAI use is primarily individual and writing-centered**: Practitioners report using GenAI most frequently for research-related writing tasks — synthesizing notes, drafting interview scripts, summarizing insights, creating personas from existing data — but rarely for visual design activities.
- **GenAI has significant limitations for core design activities**: Wireframing, prototyping, and graphic design are consistently cited as areas where current GenAI tools underperform relative to practitioners' needs, with outputs requiring substantial rework or being unsuitable for professional use.
- **There is a significant lack of team-level coordination**: UX teams have not developed shared practices, quality standards, or accountability mechanisms for GenAI use — making it difficult to ensure consistency, maintain quality, or appropriately credit AI contributions.
- **Prompt engineering and output evaluation are the critical skill gaps**: Practitioners feel under-equipped to craft effective prompts and to judge whether AI outputs are accurate, appropriate, or biased — skills they want formal training to develop.
- **Senior practitioners are more confident but junior practitioners are more dependent**: Experienced UX designers report using GenAI selectively and critically; concerns about over-reliance and skill atrophy are primarily directed at junior practitioners who may lack the baseline expertise to evaluate AI output quality.

## Limitations & Critiques
The 24-participant sample, while diverse, is small relative to the global UX practitioner population. The interview data captures self-reported perceptions and practices rather than observed behavior, introducing social desirability bias (practitioners may underreport AI-dependent behavior). The study was conducted at a specific moment in the GenAI adoption curve (2024), and the rapidly changing tool landscape means specific findings about limitations may already be outdated.

The paper does not distinguish between different types of GenAI tools in sufficient detail — practitioners' experiences with ChatGPT for text synthesis may differ substantially from their experiences with image generation or specialized UX tools. The absence of a longitudinal component means the paper cannot speak to how individual or team practices evolve over time.
