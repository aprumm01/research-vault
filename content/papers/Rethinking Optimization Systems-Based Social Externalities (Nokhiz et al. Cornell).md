# Rethinking Optimization: Systems-Based Social Externalities (Nokhiz et al. Cornell)

## Summary
Nokhiz, Haim, and colleagues at Cornell present a critical analysis of optimization-based AI systems, arguing that the "optimization" paradigm systematically externalizes social harms that do not appear in the objective function. The paper proposes a systems-based framework for identifying and accounting for social externalities in AI system design, drawing on environmental economics' externality concept to argue that harmful AI outcomes are not bugs but predictable consequences of optimization without social cost accounting. The work is foundational for responsible AI design methodology.

## Key Concepts
- Optimization and social externalities in AI
- Objective function design and excluded values
- Systems-based AI harm analysis
- Algorithmic externalities (social costs not in the objective)
- Responsible AI design methodology

## Theoretical Framework
Environmental economics (externality theory) applied to AI systems; critical data studies; system dynamics; the paper is positioned at the intersection of AI ethics, economics, and sociotechnical systems theory.

## Methods
Conceptual framework development; case analysis of AI systems where optimization produced social harms; formal characterization of externality types in ML system contexts.

## Main Arguments
- Optimization-based AI reliably produces social harms when socially relevant values are not represented in the objective function
- The externality framework from economics provides a productive lens: harms imposed on third parties not party to the optimization contract
- Current AI ethics approaches focus too much on individual system properties (fairness, bias) and too little on systemic externality patterns
- Responsible AI design requires proactive externality analysis before deployment, not post-hoc harm mitigation

## Limitations & Critiques
The economics metaphor may oversimplify complex social dynamics; operationalizing externality identification in practice requires extensive domain expertise; the framework does not specify who is responsible for internalization of externalities.
