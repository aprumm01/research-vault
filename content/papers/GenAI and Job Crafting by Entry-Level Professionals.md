## Abstract

**DOI:** [10.1007/s12599-025-00959-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12599-025-00959-x)

Abstract The rise of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) in the workplace is transforming knowledge work in organizations with important implications for professionals and organizations alike. This study focuses on entry-level professionals in knowledge work (ELPs) and highlights how ELPs reshape their work in response to the adoption and integration of GenAI in the workplace. Drawing on the theoretical lens of job crafting and building on insights from a qualitative study in consultancy, this research shows how ELPs use GenAI to proactively (re)craft their work tasks and relationships. Moreover, the study identifies a third behavioral dimension of job crafting that is becoming particularly salient in light of GenAI’s novel capabilities: signal crafting, which reflects ELPs’ use of GenAI to shape others’ perceptions of the distinctiveness and value of ELPs’ work. Further, the paper sheds light on the consequences arising from ELPs’ job crafting behaviors. This study contributes to the literature on (Gen)AI and knowledge work by providing a nuanced understanding of GenAI and its implications for ELPs, their work and career prospects.

## Summary
This paper examines how entry-level professionals in creative and knowledge work fields are using Job Crafting strategies to navigate the pressures and opportunities introduced by generative AI tools. Through interviews with recent graduates and early-career workers, the study documents how these workers proactively reshape their task boundaries, relationships, and professional identity narratives in response to AI. Key findings show that entry-level workers are particularly vulnerable to AI displacement anxiety but also more adaptive in developing new AI-complementary skills when they have clear professional identity frameworks to anchor their crafting.

## Key Concepts
- Job Crafting for entry-level professionals
- Generative AI and early career development
- Professional identity formation under AI pressure
- AI-complementary skill development
- Task boundary redefinition in AI-adjacent roles

## Theoretical Framework
Job Crafting theory (Wrzesniewski & Dutton); professional socialization; organizational behavior; draws on career development theory and GenAI adoption literature.

## Methods
Semi-structured interviews with entry-level professionals (1-3 years experience) in creative, UX, and knowledge work fields; thematic analysis of AI response strategies and identity narratives.

## Main Arguments
- Entry-level workers face unique AI vulnerability because their roles often involve the routine, well-defined tasks most amenable to AI automation
- Job Crafting is a productive frame for understanding how early-career workers actively respond rather than passively experience AI disruption
- Workers with strong professional identity frameworks craft more coherent responses; those with weak identity are more likely to experience anxiety without adaptive action
- Organizations and educators can support AI-adaptive Job Crafting by helping early-career workers articulate distinctive value propositions

## Limitations & Critiques
Entry-level sample may reflect a moment of particular anxiety about AI that will change as norms develop; the study cannot distinguish genuine crafting from post-hoc rationalization; sector-specific findings may not generalize across creative and knowledge work.
