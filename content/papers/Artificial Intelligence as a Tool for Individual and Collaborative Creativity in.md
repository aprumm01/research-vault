# Artificial Intelligence as a Tool for Individual and Collaborative Creativity in Design Education

## Summary
Integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in the design process is a growing area of research interest Three years after its public launch in 2022, AI has already established itself as the most disruptive tool revolutionizing how designers conceptualize, iterate and innovate As AI technologies continue to evolve, it is pertinent that design students are acquainted with the potential of the technology and how it can be integrated in their professional practice.

## Key Concepts
- AI literacy - competencies enabling individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies, communicate effectively with AI, and use AI as a tool in professional contexts
- COIL (Collaborative Online International Learning) - globally networked learning approach pairing intercultural student teams for remote collaborative design projects
- Human-AI collaboration - partnership where AI generates rapid iterations while humans define objectives, set parameters, and make critical decisions at beginning and end of creative process
- Prompt engineering - skill of breaking down design briefs into concise, unambiguous components that can be communicated to AI to generate relevant outputs
- AI biases - tendencies in AI-generated outputs reflecting training data limitations and requiring human oversight through reference images and diverse tool usage
- Visual diaries - personal narrative records documenting all design variations, prompts used, AI tools employed, and decision-making rationale throughout design process
- Conversational AI models - text-based AI (ChatGPT, Gemini, FabriAI) used for research, data analysis, mind mapping, and opportunity discovery
- Image AI models - visual generation AI (DALL-E 2, PlaygroundAI, VizcomAI) used for rapid conceptual rendering and exploring design variations
- Critical evaluation of AI outputs - designer competency to analyze whether AI-generated images align with design briefs, project goals, and design vision
- Intercultural design teams - cross-border partnerships exposing students to diverse cultural perspectives, enhancing global citizenship and multicultural collaboration skills

## Theoretical Framework
- Constructivist grounded theory (Charmaz 2006, 2008) - develops theories grounded in data from participant experience and interactions, suitable for studying dynamic phenomena like AI integration
- AI literacy framework (Long & Magerko 2020) - conceptualizes capabilities needed to interact with AI across cognitive, humanistic, and social orientations
- Cognitive perspective on AI - views AI as teammate affecting individual cognition, metacognition, behavior, with self-regulated learning and creativity as key capabilities
- Humanistic perspective on AI - centers on human values and capabilities to use and shape AI for personal and collective well-being
- Social perspective on AI - focuses on shared meaning-making and collective practices in human-AI collaboration

## Methods
- Constructivist grounded theory approach with iterative data collection and analysis
- Multi-faceted data collection: faculty observations, student visual diaries, surveys, reflection writings
- Case study of 4-week COIL project with 14 UDEM (Mexico) and 15 Wayne State (USA) interior design students
- Design task: 500 sq m children's space (ages 3-10) in commercial interior context
- Systematic documentation of design iterations, AI tools used, prompts, and decision rationale
- Survey data on student perceptions and experiences at various collaboration stages
- Reflective writing collected at project end on learning outcomes and AI integration understanding

## Main Arguments
- AI significantly modernizes design workflow by enabling rapid iteration, freeing students to focus on creative aspects rather than labor-intensive rendering tasks, while democratizing access for students with less developed manual sketching skills
- AI tools cannot replace foundational design education—students still need manual sketching and 3D modeling skills, as AI fails at technical drawings (plans, sections, axonometrics), code compliance, ergonomics, and spatial optimization
- Prompt engineering emerges as critical new designer competency, requiring ability to break down design briefs into concise, specific components and iterate prompts to align AI outputs with design vision
- Human critical judgment remains irreplaceable for evaluating AI outputs against design briefs, assessing emotional impact, ensuring sustainability, and making decisions requiring expertise and cultural sensitivity
- AI enhances rather than threatens creativity by overcoming brainstorming fatigue, generating unexpected concepts, and producing unique visual interpretations that students can evaluate and refine
- COIL format paired with AI creates rich learning environment where intercultural collaboration and technology experimentation reinforce each other, building both cultural competency and AI literacy simultaneously
- Students' initial unfamiliarity with AI for design demonstrates gap between general AI awareness and practical integration—formal training in design curricula is urgently needed to prepare competitive graduates
- Cultural biases in AI training data require intentional strategies: mixing diverse student teams, using multiple AI engines to compare outputs, and employing reference images to guide visual styles
- Effective AI use depends on self-regulated learning through experimentation, as students discover importance of written communication clarity and develop confidence troubleshooting unexpected AI results
- Real-world adoption data (41-46% of architectural practices using AI, primarily for early-stage conceptualization) validates urgency of preparing students with AI literacy for professional competitiveness

## Limitations & Critiques
- Evaluation challenge: study used traditional assessment criteria (research depth, creativity, functionality) but authors acknowledge AI literacy evaluation remains unresolved—no reliable method yet for assessing prompt formulation quality, critical evaluation of outputs, or ethical AI use
- Small sample size (29 students total) from only two institutions limits generalizability of findings across diverse educational contexts and student populations
- Four-week project duration may be insufficient to observe long-term AI literacy development or sustained changes in design thinking and collaborative practices
- Study excludes ethical considerations of AI use in design despite acknowledging their importance—authors defer this critical dimension to future research without examining student ethical reasoning
- Focus on early-stage conceptualization ignores AI limitations in later design phases requiring technical precision, code compliance documentation, and construction detailing
- VizcomAI's "very low learning curve" celebrated as advantage but may mask deeper learning deficits if students don't understand underlying design principles being automated
- Authors claim AI "fosters creativity" but provide limited evidence distinguishing between visual novelty and conceptual innovation—students may be impressed by rendering quality rather than idea quality
- Cultural bias mitigation strategies (mixing teams, using multiple engines) described but not evaluated—no data on whether these interventions actually produced more culturally inclusive design outcomes
- Reflection data subject to social desirability bias—students may express enthusiasm knowing faculty designed and are evaluating the AI-focused project
- Missing comparison group: no control section using traditional methods to isolate AI's specific contributions vs. COIL format's general benefits
