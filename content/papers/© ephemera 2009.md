## Summary
Case and Piñeiro's ephemera article examines the identity conflicts that arise in project-managed software environments, arguing that project management's temporal and goal-bounded structure creates distinctive identity stresses for workers whose professional identity is tied to craft and ongoing relationships rather than discrete deliverable production. Through ethnographic data from software development teams, the paper documents how workers navigate the tension between project identity (what the project needs now) and professional identity (who they are as practitioners over time). The paper connects organizational sociology to workplace experience in knowledge work.

## Key Concepts
- Identity conflict in project-managed work
- Professional identity vs. project identity
- Knowledge work and temporal structure
- Software development organizational culture
- Stop Whining, Start Doing as organizational discourse

## Theoretical Framework
Organizational identity theory; practice theory; critical management studies; draws on Goffman's identity presentation theory and Sennett's work on craft and commitment.

## Methods
Ethnographic study of software development teams; qualitative data from observation and interviews; discourse analysis of organizational communication about project management.

## Main Arguments
- Project management structures create identity instability for knowledge workers who define themselves through long-term craft development and relationships
- "Stop whining, start doing" discourse in project environments delegitimizes legitimate professional concerns about quality, learning, and craft
- Workers who resist project identity in favor of professional identity face organizational sanctions; compliance comes at the cost of professional development
- Identity conflict in project work is a systemic feature of project management as an organizational form, not individual personality conflict

## Limitations & Critiques
Single organizational context limits generalizability; the critical management perspective may be seen as advocacy rather than analysis; the findings may be more specific to Agile and sprint-based development cultures than to project management broadly.
