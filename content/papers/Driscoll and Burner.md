# Driscoll and Burner

## Summary
6 Psychological Foundations of Instructional Design Marcy P Burner This chapter provides an overview of the major psychological concepts and principles of learning that are foundational to the eld of instructional design (D) Most people have an intuitive notion of what it means to learn - they can do something now they could not do before, or they know something now they did not know before.

## Key Concepts
- **Behavioral Learning Theory**: Learning understood through observable behavior and environmental consequences; reinforcement shapes repeated behaviors
- **Cognitive Information Processing**: Multi-stage memory model (sensory, working, long-term) where attention, encoding, and retrieval transform information
- **Schema Theory**: Knowledge organized as interconnected packets in long-term memory; expertise develops through schema refinement and automation
- **Gagné's Five Domains of Learning**: Verbal information, intellectual skills, cognitive strategies, attitudes, and motor skills each require different instructional conditions
- **Constructivism**: Learning as active meaning-making from inside-out; learners construct knowledge through authentic activities and collaboration
- **Cognitive Load Theory**: Working memory limitations necessitate instructional strategies like worked examples and scaffolded task complexity
- **Connectivism**: Learning as network formation where knowledge resides in distributed connections across human and non-human nodes
- **Communities of Practice**: Computer-supported collaborative learning where groups build shared knowledge spaces through joint problem-solving
- **Nine Events of Instruction**: Systematic framework (attention, objectives, recall, stimulus, guidance, practice, feedback, assessment, transfer) supporting cognitive processes
- **Enterprise Schema**: Meaningful learning contexts that integrate multiple instructional goals into coherent real-world applications

## Theoretical Framework
Integrative framework synthesizing behavioral, cognitive, and constructivist learning theories for instructional design. Traces evolution from Skinner's radical behaviorism through information processing and schema theory to constructivism and connectivism. Emphasizes alignment between learning theory, instructional conditions, and performance outcomes across different learning domains.

## Methods
Theoretical synthesis chapter reviewing major psychological learning theories and their applications to instructional design practice. Draws on historical research programs from 1960s-present across behaviorism, cognitive psychology, constructivism, and digital learning theory.

## Main Arguments
- **Learning must be observable and permanent**: Distinguished from temporary performance changes or natural maturation; requires demonstration for instructional effectiveness
- **Different learning outcomes require different conditions**: Behavioral objectives, motor skills, intellectual skills, attitudes, and cognitive strategies each need tailored instructional approaches
- **Prior knowledge is foundational**: Schemas enable experts to process information automatically, freeing cognitive capacity for higher-order thinking
- **Instruction must manage cognitive load**: Complex learning benefits from worked examples, partial completion strategies, and graduated task complexity (4C/ID model)
- **Authentic contexts enhance transfer**: Constructivist environments using real-world problems, collaboration, and learner autonomy promote deeper understanding
- **Technology enables distributed knowledge**: Connectivism and CSCL expand learning beyond individuals to networks that continuously update shared knowledge
- **Theory guides but doesn't dictate practice**: Instructional designers must select appropriate learning theories based on specific contexts, learners, and performance goals
- **Implementation requires skilled facilitation**: Even well-designed instruction fails without proper training for facilitators and appropriate technological support

## Limitations & Critiques
- **Insufficient empirical evidence for connectivism**: Theory remains underconceptualized with scant research validation despite widespread adoption
- **Individual vs. social learning tension**: Psychological theories traditionally focus on individual cognition while sociocultural perspectives emphasize community processes
- **Technology implementation challenges**: Constructivist and CSCL approaches require specialized tools and facilitator training that create barriers to adoption
- **Linear information processing questioned**: Recent evidence challenges sequential stage models; working memory appears more central than originally theorized
- **Theory selection complexity**: Practitioners face difficulty choosing among competing theories with overlapping domains and conflicting assumptions
- **Assessment limitations**: Demonstrating learning through performance tests may not capture tacit knowledge or delayed application
- **Cultural learning vs. instructed learning**: Distinction between natural cultural acquisition and formal instruction not fully resolved
- **Generalizability concerns**: Principles derived from controlled research may not transfer to complex real-world instructional contexts
- **Overemphasis on cognitive processes**: Affective and motivational dimensions of learning receive less attention in traditional psychological frameworks
- **Rapid technological change**: Digital tools evolve faster than research can validate their educational effectiveness
