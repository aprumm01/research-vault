# Me, My Colleagues, AI: Using Generative Tools as Support for Users Data Collection, Analysis, and Organization in HCI Classes

## Summary
This experience report from IHC 2025 (the Brazilian Symposium on Human Factors in Computing Systems) describes an experiment integrating generative AI tools into a User Experience undergraduate course at a Brazilian university. Two teachers and 99 students across three classes used AI tools during the first project delivery phase — user data collection, analysis, and organization — and feedback from 51 students and both teachers revealed promising preliminary results, including enhanced performance and learning, alongside concerns about critical engagement and over-reliance.

## Key Concepts
- **AI-supported user research**: The use of generative AI tools to assist students with conducting, analyzing, and organizing user data (interviews, observations, personas) rather than coding tasks
- **UX course project structure**: A project-based learning format where students develop interactive system prototypes across three deliveries, with the first focused on user research and problem space organization
- **SDG 13 as design theme**: The use of the UN Sustainable Development Goal 13 (climate action) as the project theme — providing authentic, socially meaningful design challenges
- **Multilingual AI adoption**: The context of Brazilian Portuguese-speaking students using primarily English-language AI tools, raising questions about language access and output quality
- **Teacher experience asymmetry**: The contrast between an experienced HCI/UX educator (Teacher 1, 20+ years) and a first-time UX course teacher (Teacher 2), providing comparative perspectives on AI integration challenges
- **Bloom's taxonomy alignment**: The implicit framework for evaluating whether AI-assisted work reflects surface-level response generation or deeper critical thinking and conceptual engagement

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on HCI education literature and emerging research on AI in higher education as its contextual backdrop. It references key studies including Kharrufa and Johnson's work on AI-as-persona for HCI concept exploration, Sandhaus et al.'s findings on student-initiated AI use in design courses (increased creativity but shallow learning concerns), and Lu et al.'s review of AI-assisted UX tools (gaps between AI capabilities and designer needs). The study aligns with the Brazilian HCI grand challenge on AI implications, situating itself within a broader national research agenda on AI's role in design practice and education.

The experience report genre does not require formal theoretical framing, but the study implicitly uses constructivist learning principles (students build knowledge through active project work) and the design thinking model (empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test) as organizing structures for where AI integration can meaningfully occur.

## Methods
Experience report conducted during the first semester of 2025 at a Brazilian university. Three classes of a mandatory fifth-semester UX course participated (Class 1: 34 students with Teacher 1; Class 2: 32 students with Teacher 1; Class 3: 33 students with Teacher 2). Students were allowed — but not required — to use AI tools for user data collection, analysis, and organization activities. Feedback was collected from approximately half the students (51 of 99) via post-activity reflection instruments and from both teachers through documented reflections. The study design included ethical approval for data collection.

## Main Arguments
- **Generative AI tools enhanced student performance on user research tasks**: Preliminary findings from the feedback indicate that students who used AI tools were able to organize and synthesize user data more efficiently, generating interview summaries, persona drafts, and problem space maps more quickly.
- **Students valued AI for reducing cognitive load in data organization**: The most positively received use was AI assistance with structuring and categorizing qualitative data from user interviews — a task that students found time-consuming and unfamiliar.
- **Critical engagement with AI output varied significantly**: Some students reflected carefully on AI suggestions, using them as starting points for their own analysis. Others accepted outputs with minimal critique, raising concerns about whether they were developing research competencies or outsourcing them.
- **Teacher experience shaped integration quality**: Teacher 1's depth of HCI/UX knowledge enabled richer integration, with more scaffolded prompts and discussion of AI limitations. Teacher 2 reported uncertainty about when AI assistance was appropriate versus counterproductive.
- **Ethical and equity dimensions require explicit curriculum attention**: The combination of a sustainability-themed design project with AI tools raised questions about AI-generated content perpetuating biases in user personas and about differential access to premium AI tools across the student cohort.
- **The study contributes to a growing evidence base for AI in HCI education**: The positive preliminary findings align with broader literature on AI supporting ideation and synthesis while reinforcing the need for structured pedagogical guidance and critical AI literacy instruction.

## Limitations & Critiques
As an experience report, the study lacks a control condition and rigorous quantitative measures of learning outcomes. The feedback from approximately half the students (51/99) may not be representative, particularly if students who chose to respond differed systematically in their AI attitudes or usage. The study covers only the first of three project deliveries, capturing initial impressions rather than sustained effects across the full course.

The Brazilian context — Portuguese-language students using primarily English-language AI tools — may create specific challenges not addressed in the paper. The study does not report on the quality of final project deliverables, making it impossible to assess whether AI-assisted user research led to better-designed products. The experience report format also means findings are preliminary and would benefit from more systematic follow-up research.
