# Program Evaluation: Alternative Approaches and Practical Guidelines (Chapter 1)

## Summary

This chapter introduces the foundational concepts of program evaluation, defining it as the identification, clarification, and application of defensible criteria to determine an evaluand's value in relation to those criteria. The authors trace evaluation's intellectual origins back to ancient practices of assessment and judgment while positioning modern program evaluation as a distinct professional field that emerged in the mid-20th century.

The chapter establishes crucial conceptual distinctions that organize the field. It differentiates evaluation from research, noting that while both use similar methods, evaluation focuses on judging merit/worth for specific contexts while research aims to develop generalizable knowledge. The authors introduce the formative-summative distinction (evaluation for improvement vs. evaluation for decision-making about continuation/adoption) and discuss internal vs. external evaluator roles with their respective advantages and disadvantages.

The text also addresses evaluation's relationship to other forms of disciplined inquiry, clarifying how evaluation differs from but can incorporate research methodologies. The authors emphasize that evaluation is fundamentally about value judgments—determining worth and merit—rather than simply describing or measuring phenomena. This value-laden nature distinguishes evaluation from more "objective" research traditions.

## Key Concepts

- **Evaluand**: The object of evaluation (program, policy, product, person, etc.)
- **Merit**: Intrinsic value or quality of the evaluand independent of context
- **Worth**: Value of the evaluand for a particular context or set of stakeholders
- **Formative Evaluation**: Evaluation conducted during program development to provide feedback for improvement
- **Summative Evaluation**: Evaluation conducted after implementation to judge overall effectiveness and inform adoption/continuation decisions
- **Internal Evaluator**: Evaluator employed by the organization running the program
- **External Evaluator**: Evaluator from outside the organization, typically contracted for independence

## Theoretical Framework

The chapter draws on multiple theoretical traditions including the scientific method, social science research methodology, and philosophical traditions of value theory. It positions evaluation as applied social science with a normative dimension, acknowledging both empirical and value-based components of evaluation practice.

## Methods

As a textbook chapter, this presents no original empirical methodology. Instead, it synthesizes and organizes the conceptual and methodological frameworks developed by evaluation theorists and practitioners over several decades. The authors draw on the evaluation literature to construct their definitional and conceptual framework.

## Main Arguments

1. Evaluation is distinct from research in its focus on determining value for specific decision-making contexts
2. Both merit (intrinsic quality) and worth (contextual value) are legitimate objects of evaluation
3. The formative-summative distinction reflects different evaluation purposes, not different methods
4. Internal and external evaluators each have advantages: internal evaluators know the context better; external evaluators offer independence and fresh perspective
5. Evaluation involves explicit value judgments and should not pretend to be value-neutral
6. Professional evaluation emerged as a field due to increased demand for accountability and evidence-based decision-making

## Limitations & Critiques

- The chapter represents a largely Western, positivist-influenced view of evaluation
- Limited attention to participatory and empowerment evaluation approaches that would be developed more fully later
- The merit/worth distinction, while useful, may oversimplify how value is socially constructed
- Does not fully address power dynamics in evaluation relationships
- The formative/summative distinction has been critiqued as overly binary by later scholars
- Cultural and contextual factors in defining "value" receive limited attention
