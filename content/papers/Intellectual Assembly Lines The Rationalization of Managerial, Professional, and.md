# Intellectual Assembly Lines: The Rationalization of Managerial, Professional, and Technical Work (Perrolle, 1984)

**Year:** 1984

## Summary
Applies a Marxist-influenced labor process theory to analyze how computer systems rationalize the work of managers, professionals, and technical workers by separating conception from execution—extending Braverman's deskilling thesis to white-collar work. Argues that information systems encode and commodify the cognitive labor of skilled workers, creating new forms of intellectual alienation.

## Key Concepts
- Labor process theory
- Deskilling of professional and technical work
- Separation of conception from execution
- Intellectual alienation
- Rationalization of knowledge work

## Theoretical Framework
Braverman's Labor and Monopoly Capital (1974) extended to professional and managerial contexts; Marxist labor process theory applied to information systems; knowledge as a commodity that can be extracted, encoded, and controlled.

## Methods
Theoretical analysis and conceptual argument; draws on case studies of expert systems and decision-support systems in professional contexts; no original empirical data collection.

## Main Arguments
- Computer systems allow organizations to extract and encode the tacit knowledge of skilled professionals, making that knowledge a corporate asset independent of individual workers
- This extends Braverman's deskilling logic from factory workers to professional and managerial work
- Intellectual assembly lines fragment complex professional judgment into routinized sub-tasks programmable by expert systems
- The result is intellectual alienation—professionals lose ownership and control over their own expertise

## Limitations & Critiques
Written in 1984, before the internet and modern AI; the empirical base is limited and the argument is largely theoretical; subsequent research has challenged the uniformity of deskilling effects on professional work; does not address how workers resist or reappropriate information systems.
