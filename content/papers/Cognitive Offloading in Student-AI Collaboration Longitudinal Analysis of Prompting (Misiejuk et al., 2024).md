# Cognitive Offloading in Student–AI Collaboration: A Longitudinal Analysis of Prompting Strategies

## Summary
This study examines how students distribute cognitive effort when collaborating with large language models (LLMs) by analysing 281 prompts from 122 submissions across four assignments in a semester-long course. Using qualitative coding and Co-Occurrence Network Analysis (CNA), the study shows that high-quality submissions reflect cohesive, contextually rich prompting patterns, while low-quality submissions are characterised by disagreement and minimal contextualisation. The research reveals how prompting serves as a behavioural trace of cognitive engagement and how strategies develop — or stagnate — across assignments.

## Key Concepts
- **Cognitive Offloading in AI Collaboration**: The tendency to delegate cognitive effort to LLMs; the study distinguishes productive offloading (contextual framing, guidance) from passive offloading (bare commands, delegation without engagement)
- **Co-Occurrence Network Analysis (CNA)**: A method mapping which prompt codes appear together, revealing structural patterns of collaboration rather than just frequency counts
- **Prompting as Cognitive Trace**: Prompt construction reflects underlying cognitive strategies — the level of contextualisation and instruction quality serves as a proxy for cognitive engagement
- **Evaluative Judgment**: The capacity to assess AI outputs against task-relevant quality criteria, positioned as a prerequisite for effective student–AI collaboration
- **AI Hallucinations**: The tendency of LLMs to produce confident but factually incorrect or contextually inappropriate outputs; a source of cognitive load when students lack domain knowledge to identify errors
- **High- vs. Low-Quality Submission Profiles**: High-quality submitters showed integrated, cohesive prompting networks; low-quality submitters showed fragmented, disagreement-heavy patterns
- **Longitudinal Development of Prompting**: Prompting strategies were tracked across four sequential assignments, revealing whether students refined or consolidated their AI collaboration approaches

## Theoretical Framework
The paper roots its analysis in Cognitive Load Theory (CLT, Sweller 1994), which distinguishes intrinsic load (task complexity), extraneous load (poor presentation), and germane load (schema construction). Effective learning requires managing the first two to leave room for the third. AI tools introduce a new vector: they can reduce intrinsic load by generating outputs, but this reduction may come at the cost of germane processing if students do not critically engage with what the AI produces.

The study also draws on the concept of hybrid intelligence — the idea that humans and AI should complement each other's cognitive strengths rather than substituting for one another. In this framework, prompting is not merely a technical skill but a metacognitive act: how a student frames a prompt reflects their understanding of the task, the AI's capabilities, and their own epistemic limitations.

## Methods
Qualitative-quantitative mixed methods. 281 prompts from 122 submissions in four assignments within a graduate-level social network analysis course were qualitatively coded using an established taxonomy of prompt types. Co-Occurrence Network Analysis (CNA) was then applied to map associations between prompt code pairs, identifying which prompt strategies appeared together and how central each type was. Centrality measures (degree, betweenness) distinguished structurally important prompt types. Results were compared across high- and low-quality submission groups and across the four assignment waves.

## Main Arguments
- High-quality submissions show cohesive prompting networks where contextual details, polite language, and task instructions co-occur, indicating integrative cognitive engagement rather than simple task delegation
- Low-quality submissions are characterised by disagreement prompts and direct imperatives with minimal contextualisation, suggesting students offload cognitive framing to the AI rather than investing in it themselves
- Prompting strategies do not automatically mature across assignments — some students consolidate effective patterns while others remain stuck in low-engagement cycles, underscoring the need for deliberate instruction in AI collaboration
- AI hallucinations impose a hidden cognitive tax on students who lack domain knowledge: without the ability to recognise incorrect outputs, students face added extraneous load that can undermine learning
- Evaluative judgment — the ability to assess AI output quality — emerges as a prerequisite skill for productive collaboration; it cannot be assumed and must be scaffolded by instructors
- The study challenges a purely tool-use view of AI in education, positioning student–AI collaboration as a process requiring active metacognitive management

## Limitations & Critiques
The study examines a single course in one disciplinary context (social network analysis), limiting generalisability. The prompts were collected from a graduate course using a specific LLM task (dataset generation), which may not reflect prompting behaviour in other domains or task types. The qualitative coding relies on surface-level prompt features and may not capture deeper reasoning invisible in the text. Additionally, the study cannot fully disentangle whether high-quality prompting caused better submissions or whether more capable students naturally wrote better prompts.
