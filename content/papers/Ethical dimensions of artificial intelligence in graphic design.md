# Ethical dimensions of artificial intelligence in graphic design

## Summary

This systematic literature review comprehensively analyzes the ethical challenges and opportunities of using Artificial Intelligence in graphic design. The authors examine how AI is transforming graphic design work by introducing innovative tools that enhance creativity, efficiency, and production capabilities while simultaneously generating significant ethical concerns around authenticity, originality, and authorship. The paper addresses multiple ethical dimensions including algorithmic bias, user privacy, transparency, intellectual property rights, environmental impact, and the risk of design homogenization. Through systematic literature review methodology, the authors explore how AI enables faster realization of creative concepts by automating repetitive tasks and generating complex visuals, while raising critical questions about job displacement and the redefinition of the designer's role. The research contributes to academic discourse on responsible AI use in creative industries by examining future trends including intelligent design assistants, personalized adaptive design, AR/VR integration, and the democratization of design tools, ultimately arguing for a collaborative approach where human intuition intersects with AI's computational capabilities.

## Overview of the Document

The authors of this paper are Sandra Dedijer, Nemanja Kašiković, Magdolna Pál, Ivana Jurič, Živko Pavlović, Saša Petrović, and Gala Golubović, all affiliated with the University of Novi Sad in Serbia. Their institutional background positions them within an academic setting focused on publishing, printing, and multimedia technologies. This work represents a systematic literature review published in the "Innovations in Publishing, Printing and Multimedia Technologies" series (DOI 10.59476/ilpmt2025.5-14), situating it within the intersection of technology studies, design research, and applied ethics. The document type is an academic review article that synthesizes existing scholarship rather than presenting original empirical data.

The paper sits primarily within the fields of design ethics, human-computer interaction, and technology studies, with significant connections to intellectual property law, environmental sustainability, and creative industries research. Its significance lies in providing a structured ethical framework for understanding AI's impact on graphic design practice at a moment when these technologies are rapidly transforming creative workflows. The authors state their goal explicitly: "This paper aims to comprehensively analyse the challenges, and key ethical considerations of using AI in graphic design through a systematic literature review" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 5). The work matters because it addresses the urgent need for ethical guidelines in an industry experiencing technological disruption, where questions of authorship, bias, privacy, and sustainability have immediate practical implications for working designers, educators, policymakers, and technology developers.

The document's scope encompasses both theoretical ethical frameworks and practical implications for design practice, making it relevant for multiple audiences including practicing designers, AI developers, design educators, legal scholars, and policymakers concerned with regulating creative AI applications. The authors acknowledge support from the Serbian Ministry of Science, Technological Development and Innovation, indicating this work fits within a broader national research agenda on technology innovation.

## Research Overview

The central research question guiding this work asks: What are the key ethical challenges and opportunities presented by AI integration in graphic design practice, and how can these be addressed to ensure responsible and sustainable use? The authors explicitly frame their investigation around understanding "the challenges, and key ethical considerations of using AI in graphic design" with "special emphasis on ethical challenges related to authorship, creativity, bias, transparency, and broader societal impact" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 5).

The study was conducted by the seven-member research team from the University of Novi Sad's Faculty of Technical Sciences, working collaboratively on this systematic literature review. Their methodology involves synthesizing existing scholarship on AI ethics, creative industries, design practice, and technology studies to construct a comprehensive ethical framework. The authors state: "This paper aims to comprehensively analyse the challenges, and key ethical considerations of using AI in graphic design through a systematic literature review" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 5).

The data sources consist of academic literature, industry reports, and policy documents addressing AI ethics, design practice, intellectual property law, and creative technology. The methods employed include systematic review techniques to identify, analyze, and synthesize relevant scholarship across multiple disciplines. While the authors do not provide detailed information about their search strategy, database selection, or inclusion/exclusion criteria, they draw on sources spanning technology ethics (Green, 2020; Cath, 2018), design practice (Crawford, 2024; Eckert, 2023), intellectual property (Huang et al., 2023), and AI development (Wang, 2023).

Key research concepts explained include algorithmic bias—the tendency of AI systems to perpetuate existing social biases when trained on non-diverse datasets; intellectual property rights in AI-generated content—questions of ownership and attribution when AI contributes to creative output; design homogenization—the risk that widespread AI tool adoption may produce increasingly similar design outputs; and environmental impact—the carbon footprint associated with training and running large AI models. The authors explain: "AI systems, particularly those used for image recognition and content generation, are trained on extensive datasets. If these datasets are not sufficiently diverse, the algorithms may inadvertently perpetuate existing biases" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 7).

## Theories of Knowledge

The paper draws on several theoretical frameworks from ethics, technology studies, and design theory, though these are often applied implicitly rather than explicitly theorized. The primary theoretical lens is **Applied Ethics in Technology**, particularly as articulated in technology ethics scholarship. The authors cite Green (2020) and Cath (2018) to establish ethical frameworks for AI development that emphasize "accountability, fairness, and transparency principles" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 6). Applied ethics in this context refers to the systematic application of moral reasoning to practical problems arising from technological innovation, asking not just what can be built but what should be built and how it should be deployed.

A second theoretical framework is **Sociotechnical Systems Theory**, which understands technology and society as mutually constitutive rather than separable. The authors implicitly draw on this perspective when they argue that "AI presents notable risks and ethical challenges alongside its many benefits" and that "concerns regarding privacy breaches, algorithmic discrimination, job displacement, and security vulnerabilities underscore the complex impact of AI on the existing social order" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 6). This framework, rooted in science and technology studies, emphasizes that technological artifacts are not neutral tools but are shaped by and shape social relations, power structures, and cultural values.

**Critical Algorithm Studies** provides a third theoretical foundation, particularly regarding bias and fairness. Drawing on Crawford (2024) and others, the authors engage with scholarship examining how "algorithms may inadvertently perpetuate existing biases, leading to visual representations that lack inclusivity and misrepresent or marginalize certain social and cultural groups" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 7). This theoretical perspective, developed by scholars like Safiya Noble and Cathy O'Neil, argues that algorithms encode and amplify social inequalities through seemingly technical decisions about training data, optimization targets, and system design.

**Intellectual Property Theory** undergirds the discussion of authorship and ownership. The authors engage with legal and philosophical frameworks for understanding creativity, originality, and attribution, noting that "traditional understandings of authorship emphasize the artist's unique vision and direct involvement. However, with AI-generated art, the boundaries of authorship have become less defined" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 10). This draws on centuries of legal philosophy about what constitutes creative work and who deserves credit and compensation for it.

**Environmental Ethics** frameworks inform the discussion of AI's carbon footprint. The authors note that "training and running large AI models require a great deal of computing power and energy" resulting in "a high carbon footprint" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 9). This engages with environmental ethics frameworks that consider the moral obligations humans have toward the natural world and future generations, asking whether the benefits of AI innovation justify its environmental costs.

## Central Arguments

The central argument of this paper is that while AI offers transformative opportunities for graphic design practice through enhanced efficiency and creative possibilities, its integration requires careful ethical oversight across multiple dimensions to ensure responsible and sustainable use. The authors state: "The future of graphic design lies not in choosing between human and Artificial Intelligence but in harnessing the strengths of both. A collaborative approach, where human intuition, empathy, and storytelling intersect with AI's speed, scalability, and data analysis, will define the next era of visual communication" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 13).

This central thesis is supported by several interconnected sub-arguments. First, the authors argue that algorithmic bias represents a critical ethical challenge requiring active intervention. They contend that "if these datasets are not sufficiently diverse, the algorithms may inadvertently perpetuate existing biases, leading to visual representations that lack inclusivity and misrepresent or marginalize certain social and cultural groups" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 7). This sub-claim builds toward the central argument by establishing that AI's benefits cannot be realized without addressing its potential harms, particularly to marginalized communities.

Second, the paper argues that intellectual property frameworks must adapt to accommodate AI-generated content while protecting human creators. The authors assert: "The ability of AI to generate designs based on preexisting data introduces additional concerns related to intellectual property rights. If an AI tool produces a logo or artwork that closely resembles an existing piece, it may result in allegations of plagiarism or copyright infringement" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 8). This argument emphasizes that unclear ownership creates legal and ethical risks that could undermine trust in both AI systems and design practice more broadly.

Third, the authors contend that environmental sustainability must be factored into AI adoption decisions. They argue: "Training and running large AI models require a great deal of computing power and energy, advanced AI systems rely on powerful data processing, typically provided by large data centers that run around the clock" resulting in "a high carbon footprint" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 9). This environmental argument connects to the central thesis by suggesting that irresponsible AI deployment could create long-term costs that outweigh short-term creative benefits.

Fourth, the paper argues against design homogenization, asserting that "since many AI systems rely on the same datasets and algorithms, which can limit variation and originality in their results, and the more designers adopt AI tools to generate ideas and automate parts of their workflow, there is the concern that design outputs may begin to look increasingly similar" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 9). This sub-argument supports the central claim by highlighting that uncritical AI adoption could undermine the very creativity it promises to enhance.

Fifth, the authors argue for maintaining human oversight and creative control, stating that "designers must ensure that AI complements rather than replaces the human element, preserving creative work's emotional depth, cultural nuance, and originality" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 12). This positions human judgment as essential rather than optional in AI-assisted design workflows.

## Evidence

The authors support their arguments through synthesis of existing literature rather than original empirical data collection. For the algorithmic bias argument, they cite multiple sources documenting how "AI-powered image recognition tools may struggle to accurately identify individuals with darker skin tones due to inadequate representation in training datasets" and how "AI models trained predominantly on Western visual styles may neglect non-Western artistic traditions" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 7, citing Eckert, 2023; Passas, 2023; Le-Nguyen, 2024; Sashidharan, 2024). This evidence demonstrates documented cases of discriminatory outcomes from AI systems deployed without adequate diversity considerations.

Regarding intellectual property concerns, the authors reference the ongoing legal ambiguity: "If an AI tool generates content that closely mirrors a copyrighted work, it is unclear who bears responsibility, AI developers, the designers using the tool, or both" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 10, citing Huang et al., 2023; Crawford, 2024; Ok, 2025). This evidence reveals a gap in current legal frameworks that creates risk for all stakeholders.

For environmental impact, the authors cite research showing that "training and running large AI models require a great deal of computing power and energy" with "the environmental impact depend[ing] on factors such as the size and complexity of the model, the volume of training data, the efficiency of the hardware, and the energy source used" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 9, citing Ok, 2025; Le-Nguyen, 2024). This quantitative evidence establishes the material environmental costs of AI deployment.

The evidence on design homogenization comes from industry observations rather than formal studies, as the authors note concerns about "design outputs may begin to look increasingly similar" when "many AI systems rely on the same datasets and algorithms" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 9, citing Clevertize, 2024). This represents a weaker evidentiary base, relying more on expert opinion than systematic measurement.

Regarding future opportunities, the authors cite examples of emerging applications: "AI-powered design assistants are expected to become increasingly sophisticated, capable of interpreting project briefs, user preferences, and current design trends to provide tailored, data-driven suggestions" (Dedijer et al., 2025, p. 11, citing Sashidharan, 2024; Crawford, 2024). This evidence comes primarily from technology forecasting and industry analysis rather than completed implementations.

**Research Method Limitations:** The systematic literature review methodology has inherent limitations. First, the authors do not provide transparent information about their search strategy, databases used, or inclusion/exclusion criteria, making the review difficult to replicate. Second, as a review paper, it synthesizes secondary sources rather than generating primary evidence, meaning its conclusions depend entirely on the quality and scope of the literature reviewed. Third, the rapidly evolving nature of AI technology means that published scholarship may lag behind current capabilities and practices, potentially making some findings outdated. Fourth, the heavy reliance on recent sources (2023-2024) suggests limited engagement with longer-term trends or historical precedents in technology ethics. Fifth, the geographic focus on English-language and Western sources may miss important perspectives from other regions where AI and design practices differ. Finally, the paper focuses primarily on ethical concerns with less attention to empirical measurement of actual harms or benefits in deployed systems.

## Conclusion

This systematic literature review makes a significant contribution to understanding the ethical landscape of AI in graphic design by synthesizing diverse scholarship into a comprehensive framework addressing bias, intellectual property, environmental impact, privacy, transparency, and creative autonomy. For readers who need to recall this work clearly six months from now, the essential takeaway is that responsible AI integration in design requires active ethical oversight across multiple dimensions simultaneously, not just reactive problem-solving after harms emerge.

The paper's core contribution lies in mapping the ethical terrain systematically rather than proposing specific policy solutions. By identifying key challenge areas—algorithmic bias, intellectual property ambiguity, environmental costs, privacy risks, design homogenization, and accountability gaps—the authors provide a checklist for designers, developers, and policymakers to consider when deploying or regulating AI design tools. The work emphasizes that ethical AI use requires intentional design choices, transparent practices, diverse datasets, and ongoing human oversight rather than assuming technological neutrality.

What remains open after this review are several critical questions. First, how can intellectual property frameworks adapt quickly enough to keep pace with AI capabilities while protecting both human creators and fostering innovation? The authors identify the problem but offer limited guidance on practical legal solutions. Second, how should environmental costs be weighed against creative benefits, and who should bear responsibility for AI's carbon footprint—individual designers, platform companies, or society as a whole? Third, what specific mechanisms can effectively prevent design homogenization while still allowing designers to benefit from AI efficiency? Fourth, how can bias be systematically measured and addressed in design-specific AI applications where subjective aesthetic judgment is central?

The paper also leaves unresolved tensions between competing values. The call for both innovation and caution, efficiency and originality, automation and human control reflects genuine dilemmas without easy resolution. The authors' proposed "collaborative approach" between human and AI creativity sounds appealing but lacks concrete guidance on how to operationalize this balance in practice. Additionally, the paper's focus on professional designers may underemphasize how AI democratization affects non-expert creators and whether this expansion raises different ethical concerns.

Looking forward, this work lays groundwork for future empirical research examining how these ethical challenges manifest in actual design practice, how designers navigate competing pressures, and which interventions effectively promote responsible AI use without stifling beneficial innovation. The paper succeeds in showing that AI in design cannot be understood purely through technical or business lenses but requires sustained ethical reflection and multi-stakeholder governance.

## Discussion Questions

1. If AI systems trained on Western design aesthetics perpetuate cultural bias, should design education focus more on teaching designers to critically audit AI training data, or should regulatory bodies mandate dataset diversity requirements before AI design tools can be commercialized? What are the trade-offs of each approach?

2. The authors argue that "the future of graphic design lies not in choosing between human and Artificial Intelligence but in harnessing the strengths of both." However, in practice, how should designers determine which creative decisions to delegate to AI versus retain under human control? What criteria distinguish tasks where AI assistance enhances creativity from situations where it undermines creative autonomy?

3. Given that training large AI models has significant environmental costs, at what point do the creative benefits of AI design tools no longer justify their carbon footprint? Should individual designers, platform companies, or governments bear primary responsibility for making AI design practice environmentally sustainable?

4. The paper identifies intellectual property ambiguity as a major concern when AI generates content similar to existing copyrighted works. Should copyright law extend protections to AI-generated designs, treat them as uncopyrightable (and thus freely usable), or create a new intermediate category? How would each approach affect human designers' livelihoods and creative incentives?
