# "All Roads Lead to ChatGPT": How Generative AI is Eroding Social Interactions and Student Learning Communities

## Summary
This study investigates how generative AI is reshaping peer interactions and social dynamics within computing education through 17 semi-structured interviews with undergraduate students across seven R1 universities in North America. Findings reveal that genAI tools are deeply embedded in social help-seeking processes, with students redirecting peer questions to AI rather than engaging collaboratively. The study documents emerging feelings of isolation, demotivation, and shame as traditional peer support networks erode.

## Key Concepts
- **Help-seeking mediation**: GenAI acts as an intermediary in peer assistance, with students sharing AI outputs instead of their own knowledge when asked for help
- **Peer learning erosion**: Traditional collaborative problem-solving among students is being displaced by individual AI consultation
- **AI shame**: Students experience shame about their AI use in the presence of peers, leading to concealed usage patterns
- **Social isolation**: Students report feeling increasingly isolated as the sense of community that develops through collaborative struggle diminishes
- **Belonging and retention**: The social support systems that foster academic belonging—especially for underrepresented groups—are being undermined
- **Help-seeking barriers**: GenAI lowers the socio-emotional barriers to help-seeking (fear of appearing incompetent, burdening peers) but replaces human connection with AI interaction
- **Sense of community**: Classrooms as social knowledge-construction spaces are shifting toward individualized AI-mediated learning

## Theoretical Framework
The paper is grounded in sociocultural theories of learning that position classrooms as social communities where knowledge is collaboratively constructed. Drawing on prior work on help-seeking behaviors in educational contexts, the authors argue that the socio-emotional dimensions of peer interaction—mentorship, belonging, shared struggle—are not merely incidental but foundational to learning. The concern is that genAI, by efficiently addressing the functional need for help, eliminates the social process through which that help was historically negotiated and delivered.

The study also draws on research connecting sense of belonging to academic success and retention, particularly for underrepresented groups in computing who often rely most heavily on peer support networks. The intervention of AI into these relationships therefore carries differential equity implications.

## Methods
Qualitative study using 17 semi-structured interviews (30–45 minutes each) with computing undergraduates (8 women, 9 men) from seven R1 universities in North America, conducted via Zoom between June–October 2024. Participants were recruited through CS faculty announcements, computing student organizations, and university social platforms. The study was IRB-approved, and participants were compensated with $10 gift cards. Analysis focused on participants' comparisons of help-seeking from peers, instructors, and the internet versus genAI tools.

## Main Arguments
- GenAI tools now function as mediators in peer help-seeking: when students ask classmates for help, those classmates increasingly redirect them to AI rather than providing assistance themselves, fundamentally changing the nature of peer interaction
- The accessibility and low-barrier nature of genAI (always available, no social cost) undermines the motivation to engage in peer learning, even when such engagement would be more educationally valuable
- Students report feeling isolated and missing the experience of solving problems collaboratively with friends—a loss that affects both users and non-users of AI tools
- Shame around AI use creates social friction: students feel embarrassed using AI in front of peers, yet increasingly feel compelled to do so, producing a culture of concealed reliance
- The degradation of peer support networks has asymmetric effects, with underrepresented groups in computing who benefit most from peer mentorship and community potentially experiencing greater negative impacts
- Educators must balance AI integration with deliberate cultivation of social learning structures rather than allowing AI convenience to passively displace community

## Limitations & Critiques
The study relies on self-reported interview data from a relatively small sample (17 participants), which limits generalizability. Participants were all native English speakers from North American R1 universities, which may not reflect the diverse global landscape of computing education. The cross-sectional design captures a moment in time rather than tracing longitudinal changes in community dynamics. The study does not quantify the degree of social change or establish causal relationships between AI adoption and community erosion.

Additionally, the framing somewhat assumes that pre-AI peer interactions were uniformly positive and that AI is uniquely responsible for current isolation—other factors (remote learning legacies, increased class sizes, competitive academic cultures) may be contributing to similar dynamics independently of AI adoption.
