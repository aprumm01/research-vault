# Stop whining, start doing! Identity conflict in project managed software production

## Summary
This article explores the relationship between software developers (programmers, coders, system architects) and IT project managers through narrative exchanges on the Slashdot online discussion forum. The authors analyze how conflict between IT professionals and their project managers manifests through identity work, drawing attention to strategies of resistance and dissent expressed in programmers' narratives contrasted with the performative expectations of project managers. The study contributes a grassroots critique of project management based on the lived experience of those involved in its co-construction.

The research reveals that programmers and project managers construct their professional identities in opposition to each other. Programmers emphasize "code aesthetics" and "technical knowledge" as central to their identity, viewing their work as a craft or art form that deserves respect. They often characterize project managers as technically ignorant and focused solely on meeting deadlines at the expense of quality. Conversely, project managers construct their identities around "performance concerns" and "PM knowledge," viewing programmers as complainers who fail to appreciate business constraints and the complexity of managing client relationships, schedules, and budgets.

The authors employ the concept of "performativity" in two senses: (1) discursive performances drawing on Austin's speech act theory, where programmers enact identity and membership through their forum postings; and (2) performativity as the optimization of input to output, drawing on Lyotard's critique of knowledge in post-industrial societies. This dual framework reveals how both groups struggle to impose their characteristic knowledge as the truly performative one, with aesthetic versus performance concerns and technical versus managerial knowledge forming key axes of conflict.

## Key Concepts
- **Performativity (Austinian)**: Speech acts through which programmers and managers do things in their narrative exchanges, bringing about social effects through displays of technical bravado, aesthetic preferences, and expressions of dissent or resistance
- **Performativity (Lyotardian)**: The principle of optimal performance in post-industrial societies where knowledge is assessed by its exchange-value rather than truth-value; the search for efficiency replaces the search for truth
- **Code Aesthetics**: The notion that code is worthy of attention for its own sake, celebrating personal styles and aesthetic convictions that offer programmers satisfaction; managers are accused of failing to understand or respect this aesthetic dimension
- **Technical Knowledge**: Claims about superior technical understanding that programmers use to bolster their identity and deride the alleged ignorance of project managers
- **Hacker Ethic**: Drawing on Himanen's work, characteristics including passion for work, intrinsic motivation, commitment to open access, and ambivalence toward commercial exploitation
- **Identity Work**: The continuous performative process through which programmers and project managers construct, maintain, and express their professional identities in relation to a designated "other"
- **Projectification**: The increasingly ubiquitous framing of organizational work in terms of projects, with implications for how people constitute and interpret the world

## Theoretical Framework
The study draws on post-structural organizational theory, combining insights from Labour Process theory with a more fluid post-structural interpretation of power dynamics. The authors integrate:

1. **Austin's Speech Act Theory** (performativity as discursive action) to understand how forum participants enact identity and membership through their narrative exchanges
2. **Lyotard's Postmodern Condition** to analyze how performativity as optimization of input-to-output shapes knowledge production and workplace relations in post-industrial IT settings
3. **Goffman's Self-Presentation** theory to understand the self-presentational aspects of online exchanges
4. **Knowledge Worker Identity Literature** (Alvesson, Karreman, Willmott) to situate the co-construction of identity conflict between technical professionals and managers

The theoretical contribution lies in revealing the pragmatic dynamics of identity conflict in hi-tech, project-managed settings where close technical proximity exists between managers and those they manage.

## Methods
The study employs qualitative analysis of naturalistic data from the Slashdot (www.slashdot.org) online discussion forum. The researchers visited and studied the forum periodically over five years, becoming familiar with both form and content of bulletin board exchanges. Data was drawn from discussion threads on "the aesthetics of coding" and "project management for programmers."

Key methodological characteristics:
- The forum initiates approximately twelve discussions daily, with exchanges lasting days to weeks
- Participants are predominantly programmers or IT professionals, with approximately 80% US citizens/residents
- Analysis focused on completed discussions (no researcher intervention)
- Anonymous posting allowed through "Anonymous Coward" designation
- Slashdot's design discourages purely social chat, focusing on substantive technical discussion
- The moderation system provides a sobering effect on "flamebaits" and "trolls"
- Original spelling and grammatical errors preserved in quoted material for authenticity

## Main Arguments
- Opposition to project management is a fundamental aspect of programmer identity construction; programmers represent themselves as more knowledgeable (technically and in general) and more initiated regarding what makes for high-quality software
- Project managers construct identities as more knowledgeable about business aspects of software production and as action-focused, contrasted with programmers who "whine" about difficulties
- Despite hierarchical inequality (managers positioned higher), there is apparent parity between groups due to programmers' advanced technical knowledge that project managers often lack
- Both programmers and managers are similarly indispensable to production, creating mutual interdependence that intensifies rather than diminishes antagonism
- The conflict is primarily discursive, expressed through discussions within groups rather than direct disputes between groups
- Both parties often espouse the same ultimate organizational goals but differ on how to achieve them (elegant code vs. better management discipline)
- Identity performances play a constitutive role in the production, expression, and maintenance of workplace conflicts within IT environments

## Limitations & Critiques
- The authors acknowledge it is difficult to be precise about the demographics of the Slashdot community given the anonymity of bulletin board forums
- The data represents programmers' perspectives more heavily than managers', as Slashdot's techie culture means programmers dominate discussions
- The study does not claim to speak directly to the ontological reality of worker/management conflict or make claims about underlying structural relations
- The analysis draws from a specific cultural context (primarily US-based English-speaking IT professionals) which may limit generalizability
- The forum context may encourage more extreme expressions of conflict than would occur in face-to-face workplace interactions
- The study does not fully address how the observed identity conflicts translate into actual workplace behaviors or organizational outcomes
