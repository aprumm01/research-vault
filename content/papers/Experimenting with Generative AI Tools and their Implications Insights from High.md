# Experimenting with Generative AI Tools and their Implications Insights from High School UX Educators

## Summary
As generative AI (GAI) tools become more advanced and adopted in professional user experience (UX) design fields, high school educators teaching UX and design-related courses have begun tinkering with GAI tools to use in their classrooms Through interviews with high school UX educators, we examined their experiences incorporating GAI tools when teaching interdisciplinary UX courses Key observations we identified include: 1) interactive student-tostudent engagement and student-to-GAI collaborative learning; 2) modifications in brainstorming and visualization workflows with GAI; 3) concerns and barriers with the ongoing integration of GAI tools into high school curricula.

## Key Concepts
- Computer Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL) - pedagogical framework emphasizing collaboration and digital workspaces, extended by GAI to include human-GAI interaction
- Studio pedagogy - approach treating students as designers working on real-world projects with self-learning through "learning by doing"
- Student-to-student collaboration - GAI fostering traditional peer-to-peer learning interactions
- Student-to-GAI collaboration - students working with LLM agents as collaborative partners
- Visualization and ideation in design process - using GAI tools for visual mockups, brainstorming, and early-stage concept development
- AI literacy - knowledge and skills needed to effectively use and critically evaluate GAI tools
- Novelty effect - enhanced engagement possibly linked to GAI's newness and unpredictable outputs

## Theoretical Framework
**Computer Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL)**: Framework illustrating collaborative learning when using computers as main aid in classroom activities. Extended by authors to include GAI agents as collaborators beyond traditional human-human interaction.

**Studio Pedagogy**: Design education approach where educators treat students as designers working through real-world projects with self-learning emphasis, featuring both Learning Studios (theoretical) and Experience Studios (hands-on).

**Design Process Models**: Stanford's model (Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test, Assess) and Double Diamond approach (convergence/divergence) as frameworks for understanding where GAI tools fit into design workflows.

## Methods
**Methodology**: Semi-structured qualitative interviews conducted virtually via Zoom

**Participants**: 8 high school UX educators from design-focused public and private schools in three northeastern US states, teaching grades 9-12, with average 10 years teaching experience and formal education in both design and education

**Data Collection**: 30 minute to 1 hour recorded interviews (with explicit permission) covering GAI tool usage, AI literacy, curriculum integration, and challenges

**Analysis**: Transcription and thematic qualitative coding with codes including "Student Engagement," "Streamlining," "Pitfalls," and "Skill Development" to identify recurring themes across interviews

**Research Questions**:
1. How do high school UX educators tinker with GAI in their UX classes?
2. How might UX educators using GAI during lesson delivery influence or change student learning and in-class interaction?

## Main Arguments
- GAI tools enable two distinct types of collaborative learning: (1) student-to-student collaboration where GAI output sparks deeper peer discussion due to unique iterations, and (2) student-to-GAI collaboration where LLMs function as troubleshooting partners
- GAI visualization tools modify brainstorming and design workflows by merging ideation and visualization phases, though this may impact development of foundational design skills like sketching and ideation
- Integration of GAI into high school curricula faces significant barriers including negative sentiment from students and educators, fear of job replacement, and lack of strategic organizational approaches
- The positive learning outcomes observed may be linked to GAI's novelty factor, raising questions about sustainability as tools become commonplace
- Critical and thoughtful GAI adoption requires balancing efficiency gains with preservation of foundational design skill development, particularly at the high school level

## Limitations & Critiques
**Study Limitations**:
- Small sample size (8 educators) from only three northeastern US states limits generalizability
- Focus only on educators already using GAI tools (self-selection bias toward favorable perspectives)
- Qualitative interview methodology without quantitative validation of learning outcomes
- No direct observation of classroom interactions or student perspectives captured
- Short timeframe unable to assess long-term impacts beyond novelty effects

**Critical Concerns Raised**:
- **Accuracy verification**: Lesson example using ChatGPT for protein synthesis lacked explicit checks for scientific accuracy of AI-generated content
- **Skill development threat**: GAI visualization may hinder students' development of traditional foundational skills (sketching, manual ideation, brainstorming) essential for design practice
- **Cognitive offloading**: Students offloading ideation to GAI tools may stunt creativity development and critical thinking in early design phases
- **Novelty dependency**: Positive engagement outcomes may be temporary, linked to GAI's unpredictability and newness rather than sustainable pedagogical value
- **Uneven implementation**: "Scattershot approach" to integration lacks strategic coordination, standards, or AI literacy training
- **Negative sentiment**: Significant resistance from both students and educators around authorship concerns, job security fears, and ethical questions about GAI use
