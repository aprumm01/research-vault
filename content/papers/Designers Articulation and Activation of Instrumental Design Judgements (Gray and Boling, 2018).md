# Designers' Articulation and Activation of Instrumental Design Judgements in Cross-Cultural User Research

## Summary
This paper examines how a Scandinavian design team articulated and activated instrumental design judgements while conducting co-creation workshops with Chinese consumers. Using shared data from the Design Thinking Research Symposium (DTRS11), the study analyzes over 13 hours of video recordings spanning design, debrief, and interview sessions to document how the team's cultural understanding evolved through the design process.

The research focuses on instrumental judgement, which Nelson and Stolterman define as a designer's interaction with tools involving technique, instrument selection, proportion, and gauge. The authors trace how the team referenced workshop participants, shifted from totalizing cultural references to translator-mediated interactions, and evolved their understanding of the translation role from linguistic utility to cultural mediation.

Key findings reveal that instrumental judgements shifted over time: during the design phase, participant interactions were projected without deep cultural consideration; during the debrief phase, translators became central to nuancing and making familiar the cultural meanings being explored. The team attempted to engage with cultural concerns through assuming equivalents, relating to familiar contexts, questioning translators, and trying out understandings, though limitations on cultural perspective remained.

## Key Concepts
- **Instrumental Judgement**: Designer's process of mediating interaction with tools, encompassing technique selection, proportion, gauge, and evaluation
- **Design Judgement**: Tacit knowledge and practical wisdom (phronesis) that forms the backbone of design expertise
- **Nuancing**: Translator practice of adding layers or gradation to cultural understanding during debrief
- **Making Familiar**: Team members seeking equivalents or analogues within their own cultural experience
- **Cross-Cultural Design**: Design activity traversing cultural boundaries between product, designer, and user

## Theoretical Framework
The study builds on Nelson and Stolterman's typology of design judgements, particularly instrumental judgement as distinct from framing, compositional, and other judgement types. It connects to literature on cross-cultural design methods, design cognition, and phronesis/practical wisdom in design practice.

## Methods
Qualitative analysis of shared DTRS11 data-set comprising 13+ hours of video and audio recordings, photographs, and transcripts. Analysis employed discourse analysis (tracking third-person plural pronouns referencing participants), thematic analysis (identifying cultural awareness themes), and synthesis to reveal shifts in instrumental judgement over time. The design team consisted of three UX designers working with external consultants and local translators.

## Main Arguments
- Instrumental judgements in cross-cultural contexts are frequently underspecified in design methods
- The design team's references to participants increased dramatically through the process, showing growing engagement with participant perspectives
- Translators shifted from linguistic utility to cultural mediators, demonstrating evolution in the team's understanding of their instrumental role
- Cultural considerations were often unconscious or tacit; when culture became foregrounded, it was often instantiated through stereotypes
- Team members attempted to understand culture through: assuming equivalents, relating to familiar contexts, asking translators, and trying out understandings
- Designers often operated analogously to fashion designers "buying objects in shops" - using workshop outputs as springboards without fully engaging their cultural context
- Cross-cultural design methods should foreground cultural assumptions and support explicit consideration of instrumental judgements

## Limitations & Critiques
- Single case study of one design team limits generalizability
- The team had limited prior experience with Chinese culture; findings may differ with more culturally experienced teams
- No Chinese team members were involved in the planning phases, limiting cultural perspective integration
- Secondary analysis of shared data-set; researchers were not present during original sessions
- Focus on one project type (consumer product co-creation) may not transfer to other design contexts
- The analysis identifies patterns but does not experimentally test interventions to improve cross-cultural practice
