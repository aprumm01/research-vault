# Future imaginaries in the making and governing of digital technology Multiple, contested, commodified

## Summary
Visions of the future are omnipresent in current debates about the digital transformation This introductory article and the full special issue are concerned with the function, power, and performativity of future visions and how they relate to the making and governing of digital technology Revisiting existing concepts, we particularly discuss and advance the concept of sociotechnical imaginaries.

## Key Concepts
- **Sociotechnical imaginaries**: Collectively held visions of desirable futures animated by shared understandings of forms of social life and social order attainable through advancement of science and technology, serving as both descriptive frameworks and performative forces shaping technological development
- **Future visions as performative**: Forward-looking narratives and imaginaries not merely predictive or aspirational but actively constituting reality by guiding investment decisions, research priorities, policy frameworks, and public discourse, thereby making imagined futures more likely to materialize
- **Multiple and contested imaginaries**: Recognition that diverse stakeholders (corporations, policymakers, civil society, technical communities) hold competing visions for digital technology futures, with power dynamics determining which imaginaries gain dominance and shape actual development trajectories
- **Commodification of futures**: Process by which future visions become marketable assets—venture capital narratives, corporate innovation stories, consulting future scenarios—transforming imaginaries from shared cultural resources into proprietary strategic tools serving particular economic interests
- **Governance through anticipation**: Policy and regulatory approaches leveraging future imaginaries to guide technology development proactively rather than reactively, raising questions about whose imaginaries embedded in governance frameworks and what futures thereby enabled or constrained

## Theoretical Framework
**Sociotechnical Imaginaries Theory (Jasanoff & Kim)**: Theoretical framework examining how collectively held visions of desirable futures co-produced through science, technology, and social order shape both technological development and societal transformation, emphasizing imaginaries as simultaneously descriptive (how people envision futures) and prescriptive (how visions guide action).

**Science and Technology Studies (STS) Perspectives**: Drawing on STS traditions examining how technologies not determined by internal logic but shaped by social forces, political negotiations, cultural values, and power relations, positioning future imaginaries as sites where these forces converge to contest and construct technological trajectories.

## Methods
**Special issue synthesis and theoretical development**: Introduction to special issue reviewing and advancing conceptual frameworks for understanding future imaginaries through synthesis of existing literature, critical examination of sociotechnical imaginaries concept, and presentation of refined theoretical apparatus for analyzing how visions function in technology governance.

**Cross-case conceptual analysis**: Drawing on empirical studies throughout special issue examining diverse digital technology domains (AI, platform governance, smart cities, digital health) to identify patterns in how imaginaries operate, compete, become commodified, and influence actual technology development and regulation.

## Main Arguments
- **Future imaginaries possess constitutive power shaping present technology development**: Visions of digital futures not merely speculative or aspirational but materially consequential—directing research funding, shaping regulatory frameworks, mobilizing political support, attracting investment, organizing development priorities. Describing AI as "transformative" or "existential risk" fundamentally affects what AI systems built, how regulated, and who controls development. Understanding technology governance requires analyzing imaginary construction, circulation, and institutionalization as political processes determining whose visions of desirable futures encoded in emerging technologies.

- **Digital technology imaginaries multiply proliferate and increasingly contested**: Unlike earlier technological eras with relatively coherent dominant visions (e.g., nuclear age progress narrative), digital transformation characterized by competing imaginaries from diverse stakeholders—Silicon Valley disruption narratives versus European digital sovereignty visions versus civil society data justice frameworks versus authoritarian surveillance state models. This multiplicity creates both uncertainty (lack of clear direction) and opportunity (space for democratic negotiation), but power asymmetries mean not all imaginaries equally influential in shaping actual development.

- **Commodification transforms imaginaries from public goods to strategic assets**: Corporations increasingly productize and proprietary future visions through innovation narratives, future scenarios as consulting products, venture capital pitch decks selling particular technology futures. This commodification shifts imaginaries from shared cultural resources enabling collective deliberation toward competitive advantages serving particular economic interests, potentially impoverishing public discourse as diverse visions enclosed within intellectual property or treated as market differentiators rather than collective goods requiring democratic governance.

- **Anticipatory governance embeds particular imaginaries into policy with democratic deficits**: Proactive technology governance—regulatory sandboxes, foresight exercises, responsible innovation frameworks—ostensibly enables shaping technology development toward desirable outcomes. However, these governance modes embed particular future visions (often industry-friendly innovation narratives emphasizing opportunity over risk) while marginalizing alternative imaginaries (precautionary, justice-oriented, or technology-skeptical visions). Democratic legitimacy requires making embedded imaginaries explicit and contestable rather than treating them as neutral technical foresight.

- **Critical imaginary analysis reveals power relations and opens space for alternatives**: Analyzing which future visions gain traction, whose imaginaries marginalized, what assumptions embedded, and what futures thereby enabled/precluded exposes power dynamics in technology governance. Making imaginaries visible as constructed choices rather than inevitable trajectories creates space for democratic contestation—insisting on alternative visions, questioning dominant narratives, and demanding whose interests served by particular technology futures. STS scholarship serves political function by denaturalizing seemingly inevitable digital futures.

## Limitations & Critiques
**Limited empirical grounding**: As special issue introduction synthesizing conceptual framework, lacks detailed empirical analysis of specific cases demonstrating how imaginaries actually operate in particular technology contexts, relying on subsequent issue articles for empirical substance—introduction remains abstract without robust examples.

**Insufficient attention to materiality and constraints**: While emphasizing imaginaries' performative power, risks overemphasizing discursive construction while underplaying material constraints—technological affordances, path dependencies, economic structures, physical infrastructures—that limit which imaginaries can materialize regardless of narrative power.

**Power analysis underspecified**: Identifies that imaginaries contested and power-laden but provides limited analytical framework for assessing relative power of different actors, mechanisms through which dominant imaginaries maintained, or specific tactics for challenging hegemonic visions—leaves power as general observation rather than actionable analytical category.

**Democratic alternatives remain aspirational**: Critiques commodification and elite domination of future visions but offers limited concrete guidance on how to democratize imaginary construction and governance—participatory technology assessment, citizen assemblies, or other specific mechanisms underexplored.

**Western-centric perspective**: Analysis primarily reflects Western (European/North American) debates about digital technology governance with insufficient attention to how imaginaries circulate, adapt, or clash across global contexts with different political systems, cultural values, and technology development models.

**Temporal dynamics underexamined**: Limited attention to how imaginaries evolve, stabilize, or collapse over time—what causes once-dominant visions to lose traction, how long imaginaries require to become institutionalized, or how speed of technological change affects imaginary stability and governance effectiveness.
