# Labels DO Matter! A Critique of AECT's Redefinition of the Field

## Summary
This paper critiques the Association for Educational Communications and Technology's (AECT) 2008 decision to revert to using the term "educational technology" instead of "instructional technology." Lowenthal and Wilson argue that this change is problematic primarily because of the weak rationale offered for the change and its potential to confuse practitioners and external audiences about the nature of the field.

The authors examine how professionals in the field struggle with the "cocktail party question" - explaining what they do to people outside their profession. They present evidence from job postings, academic program titles, listserv discussions, and publications to demonstrate that labels matter significantly for professional identity, marketability, and field recognition. Their analysis of 327 job postings found that "instructional technology" appeared three times more often than "educational technology," and "instructional design" was the most commonly used term.

The paper draws on theories of language and identity from Vygotsky, Gee, and Wertsch to argue that labels shape how we think about ourselves and our field. The authors conclude that while no perfect label exists, the method of decision-making and rationale for changes should be more transparent and involve broader input from practitioners and outsiders.

## Key Concepts
- **Educational Technology**: A label emphasizing K-12 settings and formal education contexts; AECT's 2008 preferred term
- **Instructional Technology**: A label suggesting broader application across school and work settings; used 1994-2008 by AECT
- **Instructional Design**: The most commonly used label in job postings; focuses on the design aspect of the field
- **Cocktail Party Question**: The challenge professionals face when explaining their work to those unfamiliar with the field
- **Professional Identity**: How language and labels shape individual and collective identity within a community of practice

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on sociolinguistic and sociocultural theory to argue that labels matter:
- Vygotsky's work on language, thought, and learning
- Gee's theories of language, identity, and discourse communities
- Wertsch's arguments about language's role in cultural inclusion
- Bourdieu's concept that language is never value-neutral
- Bruner's work on how language creates and shapes identity

## Methods
The authors conducted a mixed-methods analysis:
1. Content analysis of 327 job postings from four employment websites (Chronicle of Higher Education, HigherEdJobs.com, AECT Job Board, Indiana University I.T. Job Board)
2. Analysis of 134 academic program titles from AECT's Curricula Data of Degree Programs
3. Review of ITFORUM and DEOS listserv discussions
4. Informal analysis of book titles in the field

## Main Arguments
- Labels have far-reaching consequences for members within the organization and those outside of it
- The change from "instructional technology" to "educational technology" was not adequately justified in AECT's 2008 publication
- Language shapes thought, identity, and how others perceive our profession
- Market and branding considerations matter - companies carefully consider label changes
- Job postings and academic programs predominantly use "instructional technology" and "instructional design" rather than "educational technology"
- Design should have equal standing with technology in defining the field
- The process of making definitional changes should be more transparent and involve broader stakeholder input

## Limitations & Critiques
- The authors acknowledge their analysis is primarily U.S.-focused, despite AECT's international membership
- They recognize that any label will "unquestionably highlight some things while ignoring others"
- The listserv analysis was informal and selective
- The job posting data represented only a six-week snapshot
- The Curricula Data of Degree Programs may contain outdated information
- The authors note they cannot find the "right" or "perfect" label for the field
