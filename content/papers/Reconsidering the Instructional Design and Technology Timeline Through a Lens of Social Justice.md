# Reconsidering the Instructional Design and Technology Timeline Through a Lens of Social Justice

## Summary
This article presents a critical examination of how the instructional design and technology (IDT) field constructs its history through timeline projects. Bradshaw argues that typical IDT timelines, which string together prominent figures, theories, and design models in chronological order, inadvertently perpetuate ignorance about systemic injustice by presenting key events in isolation from their broader social, historical, and cultural contexts. By juxtaposing the IDT development timeline with concurrent social justice events in the United States, the author reveals troubling blind spots in how the field understands its own history and development.

The article traces IDT history from the early 1900s through the 1960s, noting that prominent contributors during these periods were predominantly white males working within contexts of legally enforced segregation, racial violence, and restricted opportunities for marginalized groups. For example, while Robert Gagne was developing pioneering work on instructional systems during World War II, the U.S. military was racially segregated and Japanese Americans were being interned. While Bloom and Maslow published influential theories in 1954-1956, Emmett Till was murdered and Rosa Parks sparked the Montgomery bus boycott. The article argues that learning these events separately, without making explicit connections, is miseducative.

Bradshaw calls for the IDT field to actively engage with social justice issues both historically and in contemporary practice, questioning how instructional designs might reinforce or disrupt systems of oppression.

## Key Concepts
- **Combined Timeline Approach**: Juxtaposing IDT developments with concurrent social justice events to reveal hidden connections and blind spots in field history
- **Epistemologies of Ignorance**: The concept that ignorance about systemic injustice is not accidental but produced and maintained through specific knowledge practices (drawing on Alcoff, Code, Harding, Mills)
- **Mechanistic Thinking**: The component-based, process-oriented approach embedded in ADDIE and other ID models that can be reinforced through how models are visually presented
- **Mystification**: The process by which limiting or excluding contextual information produces wrong attributions for outcomes
- **Praxis**: Freire's conception of reflection and action upon the world to transform it, offered as a guide for engaging social justice in IDT

## Theoretical Framework
The article draws on critical theory frameworks including Bruner's social construction, Freire's education as freedom, and epistemologies of ignorance (Alcoff, Code, Harding, Mills). Bradshaw uses Dewey's concept of miseducation—learning in unhealthy directions that inhibit growth—to critique how IDT history is typically taught. The theoretical lens positions the field's historical self-understanding as politically consequential, shaping what questions practitioners can formulate about their work's social impact.

## Methods
This is a conceptual/critical article that uses historical analysis and juxtaposition rather than empirical research methods. Bradshaw constructs a combined timeline drawing from social justice benchmark lists (Adams et al., Brunner & Haney) and standard IDT development timelines. The comparative analysis identifies temporal proximities and conceptual connections typically obscured in traditional field histories.

## Main Arguments
- IDT timelines construct field identity and communicate implicit messages about who can contribute and what counts as important
- Presenting IDT developments in isolation from social context perpetuates a "culture of ignorance" about systemic injustice
- The field's lack of attention to social justice is not accidental but reinforced through how history is taught and how the field conceptualizes itself
- Key IDT figures worked within contexts of legally enforced discrimination that may have influenced their theories in ways rarely examined
- Being silent about social justice issues is not being neutral—"You can't be neutral on a moving train" (Zinn)
- The field must move beyond adding "ethical" to definitions toward actively questioning how designs reinforce or disrupt oppression
- Dialectical engagement rather than debate, cultural synthesis rather than invasion, should guide how the field addresses these issues

## Limitations & Critiques
The article acknowledges that in providing only brief mentions of historical social events, it risks reducing them to "objectified knowledge bits" that could be dealt with mechanistically—the very problem it critiques. The analysis focuses specifically on U.S. contexts and may not address how IDT developed differently in other cultural settings. The article represents early work in a larger critical qualitative study the author has been conducting. While calling for engagement with social justice, the article provides more critique than specific guidance for how practitioners might redesign their work to address identified concerns.
