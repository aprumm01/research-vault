# AI Failure Loops

## Summary
Kawakami et al. introduce the concept of "AI Failure Loops" to explain why workplace AI repeatedly fails in feminized labor contexts such as K-12 teaching, home healthcare, and social work. They argue that the systemic devaluation of workers' expertise, particularly in professions dominated by women and people of color, creates a self-reinforcing cycle: reductive assumptions about what AI can automate lead to deployments that do not reflect the complexity of frontline labor, which further erodes the visibility of workers' skills and deepens occupational devaluation. The paper calls for a pro-worker reorientation of AI design and governance.

## Key Concepts
- AI Failure Loops
- Feminized labor and occupational devaluation
- Cognitive offloading and worker expertise invisibility
- Sociotechnical failure modes
- Pro-worker AI futures

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on science and technology studies (STS) and human-computer interaction (HCI) scholarship on workplace AI, labor dynamics, and power. It centers occupational devaluation, a dimension previous AI failure literature has largely overlooked, as the structural condition that makes these loops self-sustaining.

## Methods
Focused literature review of academic and grey literature on AI deployments across three feminized labor domains in the United States: K-12 education, home healthcare, and social work. Synthesizes cross-sector patterns into a unifying conceptual framework.

## Main Arguments
- AI systems in feminized labor contexts consistently overestimate what AI can automate and underestimate the complexity of workers' expertise
- Failed AI deployments do not merely fail to help workers; they actively obscure and erode the recognition of workers' skills
- These failure patterns are recursive and structural, not incidental design errors
- Addressing AI Failure Loops requires centering worker expertise in AI design, evaluation, and governance practices

## Limitations & Critiques
The framework is derived from a focused literature review rather than primary empirical data, which limits its ability to capture variation across specific deployments. Its focus on the U.S. context may not fully generalize to feminized labor dynamics in other national or institutional settings.
