# Points of Contact LS and ET

## Summary

## Key Concepts
- Historical divergence between educational technology and learning sciences as distinct research communities
- Robert Gagné's instructional systems approach versus Robert Glaser's learning sciences orientation
- Design-based research methodology as potential bridge between communities but lacking design expertise
- Participatory design involving end-users as active participants in design processes
- Industry of education as potential convergence point for instructional design and learning sciences
- Insular nature of instructional design practice limiting cross-community collaboration
- Design principles as useful outcomes from learning sciences partnerships
- Need for genuine design expertise in design-based research beyond methodological label

## Theoretical Framework
The text articulates the epistemological and methodological tensions between educational technology/instructional design and learning sciences fields. Educational technology historically emphasizes systematic design processes, instructional strategies, and implementation concerns following Gagné's tradition. Learning sciences prioritizes understanding learning mechanisms, cognitive processes, and theory building following Glaser's scientific approach. The framework identifies three potential integration pathways: (1) industry convergence through practical education development needs, (2) design as common practice ground where both communities engage in development, and (3) participatory design methods engaging stakeholders across boundaries. The analysis reveals that despite apparent overlap, fundamental differences in goals, methods, and values perpetuate separation.

participatory design

## Main Arguments
- Educational technology and learning sciences have operated as separate communities despite addressing related problems
- Design-based research adopted by learning scientists often excludes genuine design expertise and produces limited actionable outcomes
- Partnerships between communities can generate useful design principles when authentic collaboration occurs
- Participatory design offers methodology for involving end-users and bridging professional boundaries effectively
- Instructional designers operate too insularly, limiting exposure to learning sciences insights and methods
- Emerging education industry may create practical convergence pressures transcending academic divisions
- Design practice provides more natural integration point than research methodology alignment
- Benefits of closer community ties outweigh risks when proper participatory structures are established

## Limitations & Critiques
- Limited empirical evidence provided for claims about community separation and its consequences
- Idealized view of participatory design benefits without addressing implementation challenges in academic contexts
- Unclear mechanisms for overcoming institutional structures reinforcing community boundaries (journals, conferences, departments)
- May underestimate genuine epistemological differences making integration difficult beyond superficial collaboration
- Focuses on academic communities while practitioners may already integrate approaches pragmatically
- Does not address power dynamics in participatory design when academic researchers engage practitioners
- Assumes design practice is neutral ground rather than contested space with own professional boundaries
- Limited attention to resource requirements for sustained cross-community collaboration efforts
