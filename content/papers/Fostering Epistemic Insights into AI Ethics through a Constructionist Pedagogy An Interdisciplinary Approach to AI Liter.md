## Summary
This paper presents a constructionist pedagogical approach to teaching AI ethics, arguing that students develop deeper ethical understanding when they construct AI ethics knowledge through hands-on design activities rather than through abstract principle instruction. Drawing on Papert's constructionism, the paper documents a course in which students design and build AI artifacts that embody ethical considerations, developing epistemic insights — deeper understanding of what AI ethics means in practice — through the construction process. The paper contributes both a theoretical framework and practical course design for AI ethics education.

## Key Concepts
- Constructionist pedagogy for AI ethics
- Epistemic insights through construction
- AI artifact design with ethical considerations
- Hands-on AI ethics education
- Papert's constructionism applied to AI

## Theoretical Framework
Constructionism (Papert); constructivist learning theory; AI ethics education; draws on maker education and design-based learning to frame ethical knowledge construction through building.

## Methods
Action research in an AI ethics course; qualitative analysis of student constructions, reflections, and epistemic development; documentation of pedagogical approach and learning outcomes.

## Main Arguments
- Students who construct AI artifacts embodying ethical considerations develop more actionable and durable ethical understanding than those who study ethics abstractly
- Constructionist AI ethics education surfaces specific, concrete ethical tensions that abstract principle-based instruction obscures
- The construction process forces students to operationalize ethical values — to decide what "fairness" means in an actual algorithm — producing deeper epistemic insight
- AI ethics education needs constructionist components alongside analytical components to develop practitioners who can act ethically, not just reason about ethics

## Limitations & Critiques
Single course context; epistemic development is difficult to measure rigorously; the constructionist approach requires significant technical infrastructure and may not scale to non-technical educational contexts.
