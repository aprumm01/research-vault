# the-deskilling-controversy - attewell-1987

## Summary
The thesis that capitalism continues to degrade and deskill work in the twentieth century, creating an ever more unskilled proletariat, has been forcefully argued by Harry Braverman and his colleagues In this article I present a series oftheoretical, empirical, and methodological criticisms ofthe deskilling position, drawing upon a diverse literature, and upon original research Particular attention is given to the application of the deskilling thesis to contemporary trends in the computerization of clerical work, as a way of highlighting methodological weaknesses in the deskilling approach.

## Key Concepts
- Deskilling thesis (Braverman) - capitalism systematically degrades work into unskilled detail labor
- Counter-tendencies to deskilling (task redistribution, automation replacing unskilled work, coordination costs)
- Babbage Principle (cost savings through division of labor)
- Craft labor versus detail labor distinction
- Intervening factors between entrepreneurial decisions and economy-wide skill levels

## Theoretical Framework
Marxist labor process theory (Braverman 1974), critiqued through empirical occupational analysis and case study methodology

## Methods
Mixed-methods critique combining theoretical analysis, occupational statistics from U.S. Census (1900-1979), DOT (Dictionary of Occupational Titles) skill measurements, and original ethnographic research in insurance industry

## Main Arguments
- Braverman's deskilling thesis is theoretically and empirically flawed; capital logic produces both deskilling and countertendencies
- Four major countertendencies: (1) task redistribution without deskilling, (2) automation replacing already-unskilled work, (3) coordination costs limiting further division of labor, (4) high-skill/high-wage strategies sometimes more profitable
- Sectoral growth patterns intervene between entrepreneurial decisions and economy-wide skill levels; high-skill sectors (health, education, professional/technical) grew disproportionately
- Occupational data contradict deskilling thesis: craft workers maintained constant proportion (29-38%) of non-farm manual labor 1900-1979; professional/technical workers grew from 4.2% to 15% of workforce
- Economic expansion allows work reorganization without worker deskilling (skilled workers retain complex tasks, new workers fill lower-level positions)
- Braverman's craft/detail dichotomy is oversimplified; romanticizes craft labor and underestimates knowledge required by "unskilled" workers
- Qualitative case studies can support deskilling narratives while quantitative industry-wide data show upgrading

## Limitations & Critiques
- DOT (Dictionary of Occupational Titles) data have methodological disputes about validity
- Analysis limited to U.S. context and may not generalize to other capitalist economies
- Does not fully address temporal variations in deskilling patterns across different historical periods
- Insurance industry case study may not be representative of all sectors
