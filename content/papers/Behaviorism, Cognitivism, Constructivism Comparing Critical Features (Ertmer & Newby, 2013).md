# Behaviorism, Cognitivism, Constructivism: Comparing Critical Features (Ertmer & Newby, 2013)

## Summary
Ertmer and Newby present a comparative analysis of three major learning theories (behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism) specifically from an instructional design perspective. The paper argues that instructional designers need theoretical literacy to select and apply instructional strategies intelligently, and that no single theory is adequate for all learning contexts. Each perspective is examined in terms of its underlying assumptions about knowledge, the nature of learning, and the resulting implications for instructional design practice.

## Key Concepts
- Behaviorism: stimulus-response, reinforcement, observable behavior change
- Cognitivism: information processing, mental schemas, memory structures
- Constructivism: knowledge construction, situated learning, learner activity
- Instructional strategy selection based on learning task demands
- Theoretical literacy as a core competency for instructional designers

## Theoretical Framework
Drawing on performance improvement and instructional design scholarship, the paper uses a comparative analytical framework to examine the epistemological assumptions, learning conditions, and instructional implications of each theory. It draws on Reigeluth's instructional design prescriptions and the longstanding argument that theory must bridge research and practice.

## Methods
Conceptual synthesis and comparative analysis. Originally published in Performance Improvement Quarterly in 1993 and reprinted in 2013 as a foundational reference text; the 2013 version reflects updates situating each theory within contemporary ID practice.

## Main Arguments
- Learning theories provide verified instructional strategies and a principled foundation for strategy selection, making theoretical fluency essential for ID practitioners
- Behaviorism is best suited to tasks requiring automated responses; cognitivism to concept learning and recall; constructivism to complex, ill-structured problem solving
- Instructional designers should not commit exclusively to one theoretical position but draw selectively based on the task demands and learner characteristics
- The degree of cognitive processing required is the critical variable in matching theory to instructional context

## Limitations & Critiques
The three-part framework simplifies a diverse theoretical landscape; many contemporary learning theories (sociocultural, embodied cognition) are not addressed. As a conceptual synthesis rather than an empirical study, the claims are not validated against specific design outcomes. The 1993 original predates significant developments in constructivist and situated learning theory.
