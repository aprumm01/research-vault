# Profound Learning for Human Flourishing

## Summary
This book chapter introduces Profound Learning (PL) as a transformative framework for instructional design that centers human flourishing as its ultimate goal. The authors argue that when learning and its design become abstracted from core human experiences and purposes, they feel empty and shallow—like "feeding raw material into a machine." PL offers an alternative: a formative process emphasizing the learner's commitment to practices that transform, deform, and reform their identity over time.

The chapter identifies ten prominent characteristics of Profound Learning: deep reflection and examination; gaining insights resistant to superficial answers; meaningful learning with rich impact; perspective changing; contextual awareness of self and others; depth of knowledge that is durable; experiencing understanding beyond prior assumptions; reevaluation of previous ideas and values; paradigm changing; and consequential knowledge that produces real effects over time. Unlike surface-level learning focused on checking boxes, PL emphasizes complex thinking, deepening understanding, and seeing from multiple perspectives throughout a lifetime.

The authors connect PL to human flourishing—drawing on philosophical traditions from Aristotle and Aquinas to Martha Nussbaum and Alasdair MacIntyre. They then demonstrate how PL can be operationalized in instructional design through integration with Universal Design for Learning (UDL) and the ADDIE model. Detailed tables provide guiding questions that align ADDIE phases (Analyze, Design, Develop, Implement, Evaluate) with UDL principles and PL processes (Identity Formation, Transformation, Deformation, Reformation, Intentionality).

## Key Concepts
- **Profound Learning (PL)**: A formative process emphasizing commitment to practices across time that transform, deform, and reform the learner through intentionality
- **Human Flourishing**: The actualization of human purposes—an activity rather than a static end state—achieved through disciplines and practices tied to one's purposes as a human being
- **Transformation**: Durable, meaningful change in a learner's understanding or identity
- **Deformation**: Less durable, disruptive, or distorting change that may lead to reformation
- **Reformation**: Change that reconfigures the learner in a different and improved way
- **Intentionality**: The interface between subjective mental states and the objective world; the origin of learning and foundation of human experience

## Theoretical Framework
The chapter draws on ancient philosophical traditions (Aristotle, Thomas Aquinas) and contemporary virtue ethics (Martha Nussbaum, Alasdair MacIntyre) to ground human flourishing as the telos of education. It integrates this with Universal Design for Learning (CAST, 2018), which emphasizes developing purposeful, motivated, resourceful, knowledgeable, strategic, and goal-directed learners. The ADDIE instructional design model provides the practical structure, while PL adds the deeper purpose-driven dimension.

## Methods
This is a conceptual/theoretical chapter that synthesizes philosophical frameworks with instructional design models. The authors present two detailed tables: Table 10.1 maps PL practices to UDL guidelines with practical applications; Table 10.2 provides guiding questions aligning ADDIE phases with UDL and PL processes. A narrative case study of "Chris," an experienced instructional designer, illustrates how the framework might be applied in practice.

## Main Arguments
- Teaching and learning have sometimes become technical endeavors divorced from human purposes—"feeding raw material into a machine"
- Profound Learning reframes instructional design in the context of human flourishing, an ancient concept with continuing relevance
- Human flourishing is not a static end state but an ongoing activity of engaging disciplines and practices tied to one's purposes
- PL is characterized by deep reflection, resistance to superficial answers, meaningful impact, and consequential knowledge
- Instructional designers should create architectures for learning that build depth over time, not just one-off instruction that checks boxes
- UDL provides useful guidelines that can facilitate PL when applied with a broader lens than just special education populations
- The integration of ADDIE, UDL, and PL offers novice designers a model for progressively deepening their practice
- Designing for PL has three-fold benefits: more effective organizations, more successful individuals, and personal transformation for designers themselves

## Limitations & Critiques
The chapter presents a theoretical framework without empirical validation of its effectiveness. The integration of ADDIE, UDL, and PL creates a complex model that may be challenging for novice designers to implement. The philosophical grounding in virtue ethics may not resonate with all practitioners or align with all organizational contexts. The focus on human flourishing as an ultimate goal may conflict with performance-focused organizational priorities. The framework does not address how to measure or assess profound learning outcomes.
