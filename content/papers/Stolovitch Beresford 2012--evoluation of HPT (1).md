# Stolovitch Beresford 2012--evoluation of HPT (1)

## Summary
Stolovitch and Beresford (2012) trace the historical development of Human Performance Technology (HPT) from its roots in behavioral psychology and systems engineering through its maturation as a comprehensive performance improvement methodology. The chapter examines how HPT evolved from a narrow focus on training to a broader systems approach encompassing analysis, intervention selection, implementation, and evaluation across organizational, individual, and environmental performance factors. Key figures (Gilbert, Mager, Rummler) and pivotal frameworks (Gilbert's behavior engineering model, Rummler-Brache process improvement) are situated in the field's evolution.

## Key Concepts
- **Human Performance Technology (HPT)**: A systematic approach to improving human performance through analysis, intervention design, and evaluation
- **Performance analysis**: Diagnosing the gap between actual and desired performance and identifying its causes
- **Behavior Engineering Model (BEM)**: Gilbert's framework attributing performance gaps to environmental (information, resources, incentives) and individual (knowledge, capacity, motivation) factors
- **Non-training interventions**: HPT's core insight that most performance gaps are not caused by skill deficits and require non-training solutions
- **Process improvement**: Rummler-Brache contribution situating individual performance within organizational process and system contexts

## Theoretical Framework
Behavioral psychology (Skinner, Gilbert) combined with systems theory (Deming, Rummler). HPT frames human performance as a product of the interaction between individual capabilities and the environmental system in which work occurs.

## Methods
Historical narrative review of HPT's intellectual development; analysis of foundational texts, key figures, and influential frameworks from the 1960s through the 2000s.

## Main Arguments
1. HPT evolved from a training-centric to a systemic performance improvement orientation as practitioners recognized that most performance gaps are not training problems
2. Gilbert's Behavior Engineering Model remains the field's most influential framework for performance diagnosis
3. HPT's systems orientation requires attending to organizational processes, management practices, and environmental factors—not just individual skill and knowledge
4. The field's evolution reflects a continuous movement toward greater rigor, broader scope, and stronger evidence orientation

## Limitations
Historical account reflects the perspectives of HPT's dominant figures; alternative genealogies emphasizing other influences are possible. The field's boundaries with adjacent fields (OD, learning and development, management consulting) remain contested.
