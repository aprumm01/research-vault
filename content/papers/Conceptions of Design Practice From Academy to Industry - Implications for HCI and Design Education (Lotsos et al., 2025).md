# Conceptions of Design Practice From Academy to Industry: Implications for HCI and Design Education

## Summary
This paper investigates how designers at different stages of development—from students to educators to industry professionals—conceive of professional design practice. Through 21 semi-structured interviews with participants across these three populations, the researchers identify three distinct conceptions of design practice: Artifact-Centered, Human-Centered, and Cross-Functional. These conceptions represent increasingly refined understandings of the designer's role, goals, values, and competency in practice.

In Artifact-Centered Design, design is defined by its output—the artifact—and associated qualities like aesthetics and technical complexity become the key values. Competency is synonymous with how skilled the designer is at their craft. Human-Centered Design shifts focus to creating usable artifacts that address identified user needs, with values centered on usability, empathy, impact, and integrity. Competency expands to encompass proficiency in identifying user needs and skillfully designing artifacts that meet those needs. Cross-Functional Design represents a further development where the designer operates within organizational contexts alongside other professionals, considering feasibility, business impact, and stakeholder values while maintaining a user-centered design process.

The study reveals that designers' conceptions are shaped by their education and professional experiences, with narratives of transition between conceptions representing key learning and developmental moments. The authors argue these conceptions can inform HCI and design education curricula by highlighting key issues and tensions students navigate as they develop their professional identities.

## Key Concepts
- **Artifact-Centered Design**: A conception where design is defined by its output, valuing aesthetics and technical complexity; competency equals craft skill
- **Human-Centered Design**: A conception centering usability and user needs, with competency encompassing user research and empathetic design skills
- **Cross-Functional Design**: A conception integrating organizational context, stakeholder values, and business constraints while maintaining user-centered processes
- **Conceptions of Practice**: How participants understand what a designer does (Role), design goals (Goal), key values (Values), and what makes a "good" designer (Competency)
- **Shift Narratives**: Accounts of transition between conceptions representing key learning and developmental moments in a designer's career

## Theoretical Framework
The study contributes to practice-led research in HCI, which aims to develop deeper understanding of professional design practice to better support practitioners and inform education. The researchers draw on literature about the "research-practice gap," competency development, and the relationship between designers and organizations. The theoretical approach uses grounded theory methods to build a model of conceptions from participant data.

## Methods
- **Participants**: 21 individuals—9 students (undergraduate/graduate in HCI and design programs), 4 educators (full-time faculty), and 8 professionals (2-10 years experience)
- **Data Collection**: Semi-structured interviews via Zoom (45-90 minutes, average 70 minutes)
- **Recruitment**: Email outreach to HCI/design programs, professional associations, LinkedIn, and snowball sampling
- **Analysis**: Four-phase grounded theory approach—(1) familiarization and transcript correction, (2) open coding and analytic memoing on subset, (3) concept mapping to build theory, (4) deductive coding of remaining interviews
- **Theory Building**: Created concept maps for each interview, then grouped codes into narrative categories yielding three conceptions

## Main Arguments
- Designers develop increasingly refined conceptions of practice as they gain experience, with each conception building on previous ones
- The three conceptions (Artifact-Centered, Human-Centered, Cross-Functional) specify different understandings of role, goals, values, and competency
- Student conceptions are shaped by education and can negatively impact their experience (e.g., feeling "not good enough" for design)
- The transition from Human-Centered to Cross-Functional often involves navigating tensions between user advocacy and organizational constraints
- Studying conceptions helps identify key learning moments that can inform curriculum design and pedagogy
- Understanding student perspectives in HCI education is as important as developing novel pedagogical approaches
- The conceptions provide analytical tools for researchers and educators to investigate design practice and education

## Limitations & Critiques
The study focuses on participants from U.S. higher education institutions, limiting generalizability to other educational and cultural contexts. The sample size, while appropriate for qualitative research, may not capture the full diversity of design conceptions. The retrospective nature of interviews relies on participants' ability to accurately recall and articulate their developmental experiences. The study does not establish causal relationships between educational experiences and conception development. The three conceptions represent a simplified model that may not capture all variations in how designers understand their practice.
