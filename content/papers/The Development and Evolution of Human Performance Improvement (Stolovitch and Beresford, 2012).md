# The Development and Evolution of Human Performance Improvement

## Summary
This book chapter traces the historical development and evolution of Human Performance Improvement (HPI) from its origins to its current state as a recognized professional field. The authors examine how HPI emerged from instructional technology and behavioral psychology to become a systematic approach to improving workplace performance through people-focused interventions.

The chapter defines HPI through three perspectives: vision (achieving valued accomplishments through people), concept (systematic mission tied to achieving successful accomplishments valued by all stakeholders), and desired end (worthy performance as valued accomplishment derived from costly behavior). HPI emerged from the recognition that training alone often fails to improve performance, leading to broader examination of environmental and organizational factors.

Key historical milestones include Thomas Gilbert's development of mathetics (science of learning) and the Behavior Engineering Model (1962-2007), Joe Harless's Performance Improvement Process and front-end analysis methodology (1970), and Rummler and Brache's comprehensive performance improvement model (1995). The chapter traces the evolution of professional organizations, from NSPI (National Society for Programmed Instruction, founded 1962) to ISPI (International Society for Performance Improvement, 1995).

## Key Concepts
- **Human Performance Improvement (HPI)**: Achieving increasingly successful accomplishments through people, directly tied to organizational goals valued by all stakeholders
- **Worthy Performance**: Gilbert's concept defining performance as valued accomplishment derived from costly behavior (Pw = Av/Bc)
- **Behavior Engineering Model**: Gilbert's framework identifying six factors affecting performance across environment (information, resources, incentives) and individual (knowledge, capacity, motives)
- **Front-End Analysis**: Harless's methodology for identifying performance gaps and root causes before selecting interventions
- **Performance Consultant (PC)**: Professional role identifying gaps between desired and actual performance, analyzing systemic factors, and recommending integrated interventions

## Theoretical Framework
HPI draws from multiple knowledge sources: cybernetics, behavioral psychology, communications theory, information theory, systems theory, management science, cognitive sciences, and neuroscience. It is empirical, neither committed to any particular delivery system nor confined to any specific context. The field operates on key assumptions including that human performance follows specific laws, knowledge of behavior is limited but growing, and HPT must rely on both practical experience and scientific research.

## Methods
Historical analysis tracing the field's development through examination of foundational works, professional society evolution, and key contributors. The chapter presents the HPT Model developed by Van Tiem, Moseley, and Dessinger (2004) as the field's most globally recognized framework.

## Main Arguments
- HPI is a natural evolution toward systemic alignment of human capital management with organizational requirements
- The field emerged from recognition that training interventions alone often fail to produce desired performance improvements
- Human capital has been empirically shown to yield higher rates of return than physical capital, making HPI increasingly valuable
- Professional societies (ISPI, ASTD) have been critical in transforming HPI from learning-focused to performance-focused practice
- Simple solutions for closing performance gaps are almost always ineffective; systemic analysis is required
- HPI bears similarities to and should collaborate with related fields including organizational development, HRD, and organizational effectiveness

## Limitations & Critiques
- HPI has not yet generated a firm theoretical foundation of its own, instead drawing from multiple applied fields
- The field's guiding principles are molded by empirical data and documented, systematic practice rather than unified theory
- Calculating the ratio of valued accomplishment to costly behavior (worthy performance) is challenging in practice
- HPI professionals must work in partnership with professionals from related fields, requiring broad collaboration skills
- The need to demonstrate return-on-investment remains an ongoing challenge for HPI practitioners
