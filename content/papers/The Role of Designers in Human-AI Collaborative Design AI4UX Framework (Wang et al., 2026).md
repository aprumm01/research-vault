# The Role of Designers in Human–AI Collaborative Design: Capturing the New Patterns in AI4UX Framework

## Summary
This article introduces the AI4UX framework, a conceptual tool for analyzing how GenAI is reshaping UX design practice. The framework identifies four domains arising from the dual positioning of GenAI as both a design material and a design tool, crossed with the distinct practices of UX designers and UX researchers. The paper argues that designers must navigate increased competency requirements — not just using AI tools but understanding AI as a material being designed — while ensuring human-centered design principles are preserved.

## Key Concepts
- **AI4UX framework**: A four-domain conceptual framework structured by two axes: GenAI as material vs. GenAI as tool, and UX designers vs. UX researchers — providing a structured lens for analyzing different human-AI collaboration scenarios
- **GenAI as design material**: AI as the subject of design rather than just a design aid — encompassing AI-native products (Rabbit R1, AI Pin), AI-enabled hardware, and AIGC (AI-generated content) that designers must understand and shape
- **GenAI as design tool**: AI as an instrument within the design process — chatbots, image generators, research platforms, and specialized UI generation tools that augment designer capabilities
- **UX designer vs. UX researcher distinction**: The often-collapsed distinction between designers (focused on creative solution generation) and researchers (focused on understanding users and evaluating designs) — treated here as requiring different AI integration strategies
- **Human creativity protection**: The argument that while AI can boost short-term creative performance, it risks diminishing long-term creative capacity and collective design diversity
- **Meaningful transparency in AI research**: The need for UX researchers using AI to clearly disclose AI's role in data collection, synthesis, and insight generation to maintain research integrity

## Theoretical Framework
The paper adopts a constructivist perspective (following Creswell, 2009) to frame knowledge about AI-mediated UX practice as constructed through interactions among designers, AI systems, and design contexts — rather than as fixed properties of any one element. This underpins the AI4UX framework's design as a relational structure rather than a simple capability taxonomy.

The framework draws on activity theory traditions by treating design as mediated activity, with AI serving as the mediating artifact whose characteristics shape what kinds of work are possible. It also engages with design epistemology (Fallman, Dorst) to maintain that design is more than creative artifact production — it encompasses designerly ways of knowing, critical evaluation, and ethical judgment that AI cannot replicate.

## Methods
This is a conceptual/review article published in Design for Augmented Humanity (2026, Vol. 1). The AI4UX framework is developed through a synthesis of existing literature on human-AI collaboration in design, industry developments (Figma Make, ChatGPT-FigJam integration), empirical studies cited, and the authors' own theoretical analysis. The paper is not based on primary empirical data collection but draws on a wide range of published work to construct and illustrate the framework.

## Main Arguments
- **The dual positioning of GenAI as both material and tool creates a unique challenge**: Unlike previous design eras (wood/metal in industrial era; pixels/flows in digital era), designers now simultaneously design with AI and design for/about AI — requiring competencies across both dimensions simultaneously.
- **AI-native products represent a new design frontier requiring material-level understanding**: Products like the Rabbit R1 and AI Pin cannot be designed using conventional UX methods because users' mental models of AI behavior are unstable; designers must understand AI capabilities, limitations, and failure modes as material properties.
- **GenAI boosts short-term creativity but may harm long-term creative development**: Drawing on Doshi and Hauser (2024) and Kumar et al. (2025), the paper argues that AI assistance creates a structural tradeoff — enhanced immediate creative output vs. diminished individual creativity and collective design diversity over time.
- **UX researchers need meaningful transparency protocols when using AI**: The paper distinguishes AI-supported synthesis (legitimate) from opaque AI-generated insights presented as researcher findings (problematic), arguing that meaningful transparency requires explicit disclosure of AI's role in each stage of the research process.
- **GenAI as an intelligent evaluation agent raises both opportunities and risks**: AI can supplement real users in some evaluation scenarios (cognitive walkthroughs, accessibility checks, heuristic evaluations), but cannot replace empathic user research because it lacks lived experience and genuine emotional response.
- **Future designers require elevated competencies across four domains**: The AI4UX framework implies that design education and professional development must address AI-as-material knowledge, AI-tool fluency, researcher AI literacy, and critical evaluation of AI-generated designs.

## Limitations & Critiques
As a conceptual framework paper, the AI4UX framework is not empirically validated — it is proposed as a descriptive and organizing tool rather than a tested predictive model. The paper's coverage is broad, touching on AI-native product design, GenAI tools, UX research, and design education, but the breadth comes at the cost of depth in any single area. The framework's four-domain structure, while analytically clean, may oversimplify the heterogeneity of UX practice across different industries, scales, and cultural contexts.

The paper's recommendations for "protecting human creativity" are philosophically appealing but practically underspecified — it is unclear what specific pedagogical or organizational interventions would prevent the long-term creative atrophy the authors warn about. The study draws primarily on Western design practice and industry examples, which may limit applicability in non-Western design contexts.
