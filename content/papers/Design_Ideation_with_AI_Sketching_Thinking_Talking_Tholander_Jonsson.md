# Design Ideation with AI: Sketching, Thinking and Talking with Generative Machine Learning Models

## Summary
This paper investigates how generative machine learning and large language models (specifically GPT-3) can be integrated into co-creative design ideation processes. Through a workshop with design practitioners and researchers, the authors identify opportunities and challenges, finding that the form of user interaction shapes expectations and that broader AI discourse both limits and enables co-creative processes. The paper proposes post-human frameworks for understanding human-AI co-creativity in design.

## Key Concepts
- **Design Ideation**: The early-stage creative process of generating design concepts, here supported by AI through text-based prompting
- **Co-creativity**: A framing where both human and AI are treated as active participants in generating design ideas, moving beyond tool-use metaphors
- **Prompt Engineering**: The skill of crafting natural language inputs to elicit useful outputs from large language models, described as a form of programming in natural language
- **Post-human Design**: A theoretical orientation that de-centers the human as the sole locus of creative agency, treating AI and technology as co-participants
- **Large Language Models (LLMs)**: General-purpose models like GPT-3 that differ from specialized design tools by responding to any topic through open-ended dialogue
- **Computer-Supported Creativity**: The broader field of tools designed to support creative work, from CAD systems to generative design, situating this study historically
- **Fixation and Framing**: How discourse around AI—whether as mimicry, replacement, or collaborator—shapes what designers can imagine doing with these tools

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on post-anthropocentric and post-human design theory (Wakkary, Giaccardi, Suchman) to reconceptualize creative agency as distributed across humans and technologies. Rather than asking whether AI can replicate human creativity, the authors ask how human-AI entanglements produce new forms of creative practice. This is complemented by distributed cognition and co-performance frameworks, which treat artifacts and tools as active participants in sociotechnical assemblages.

The study also engages with computer-supported creativity literature (Bernal et al.'s taxonomy of computer-aided, computer-based, and computer-augmented design) to position LLMs as qualitatively different from specialized design support tools—more open-ended, less domain-specific, and more reliant on interaction design to unlock utility.

## Methods
A workshop was conducted in which professional interaction designers and design researchers used GPT-3 to develop design concepts for an emergency waiting room experience. Participants engaged in practical design exercises interleaved with structured reflection on their AI interactions. Data was analyzed qualitatively, yielding three thematic findings related to practical usefulness, interaction expectations, and broader AI discourse effects.

## Main Arguments
- GPT-3 demonstrated practical usefulness for rapid idea generation but showed significant limitations when designers expected expert-level domain knowledge or consistent creative judgment—the open-ended black-box nature of LLMs creates a "capability gap" between user expectations and system performance
- The natural language interaction modality imported expectations from human conversation, leading participants to anthropomorphize the system and be surprised by its inconsistencies; prompt engineering emerged as a distinct skill requiring deliberate cultivation
- Broader cultural and media discourse around AI (hype, fear, replacement narratives) actively shaped how participants framed their co-creative interactions, sometimes constraining exploration of more collaborative or post-human framings
- Framing AI as a co-creator rather than a tool opens new design possibilities but also raises unresolved questions about authorship, quality evaluation, and creative responsibility
- The paper argues for new interface metaphors and interaction designs that better communicate LLM capabilities and support iterative, exploratory ideation workflows

## Limitations & Critiques
The study is limited by its small, expert sample (design practitioners and researchers) who may have more facility with ambiguous tools and reflective practice than typical design students. GPT-3 is now superseded by significantly more capable models, which may change the dynamics observed. The workshop format creates artificial conditions that may not reflect sustained integration of AI into real design workflows.

The paper does not empirically measure creative output quality, relying instead on participant reflections—which may be subject to novelty effects and optimism bias. The post-human framing, while theoretically rich, may be difficult to translate into practical pedagogical or design tool recommendations.
