# Understanding the Past, Present, and Future of Design Fictions (Muller & Fiesler, CHI 2020)


## Summary
Muller, Fiesler, Bardzell, Cheon, Su, Baumer, Light, and Blythe organized and documented the CHI 2020 workshop "Understanding the Past, Present, and Future of Design Fictions," published as a CHI 2020 Extended Abstracts (ACM, doi: 10.1145/3334480.3375168). The workshop paper establishes the growing importance and diversity of design fiction within HCI, identifies unresolved conceptual questions (origins, definitions, methods, evaluation), and proposes both a taxonomy of design fiction genres and preliminary evaluation guidelines for reviewers. The paper serves as a call for participation and agenda for collective reflection within the CHI community on design fiction's current state and future directions.

## Key Concepts
- Design fiction as a diverse and growing practice within HCI and CHI
- Taxonomy of design fiction genres: stories, pastiche scenarios, imaginary abstracts, futuristic autobiographies, artifacts, probes, dramas
- Unresolved questions: origins and history, definitions, methods, evaluation criteria
- Participatory and feminist politics of "who gets to be an author" of design fictions
- Design fiction as both method and artifact with distinct contribution types
- Relationship to predecessor methods: scenarios, personas, probes, speculative/critical design

## Theoretical Framework
HCI design research; draws on speculative design (Dunne and Raby), participatory design history, Future Studies, critical design, feminist HCI, and postcolonial approaches; situates design fiction within a lineage including Bleeker (2009), Sterling, and CHI community work from the 2000s-2019; the paper functions as a structured research agenda-setting document rather than an empirical study; CHI 2020, Honolulu, HI.

## Methods
Workshop organization and summary paper; Call for Participation structured around submission genres (development/use of DFs, materials, the fiction itself, reflective accounts, historical essays, categorizations); workshop agenda included plenary panels, breakout sessions on the nature of DFs and their audiences, and review guideline development; outcomes included a workshop report and potential review guidelines for CHI submissions of design fiction contributions.

## Main Arguments
- Design fiction is increasingly prevalent and important within HCI, but the term is used in conflicting ways even within the CHI community, and the field has reached a critical point for reflection and agenda-setting
- The history of design fiction is contested: while many trace origins to Dunne/Raby and Bleeker, participatory design, future studies, and speculative fiction traditions provide deep prior roots that are often unacknowledged
- No single definition of design fiction is desirable or achievable, but conceptual organization is needed -- including a principled taxonomy of genres (stories, artifacts, probes, dramas, imaginary abstracts, etc.)
- Design fictions raise important political and epistemic questions about who gets to author futures and whose values are embedded in speculative visions, calling for more inclusive authorship practices
- The CHI community needs shared evaluation criteria for design fiction as an archival contribution, since standard empirical review criteria are ill-suited to evaluating these genre-diverse works

## Limitations & Critiques
As a workshop summary paper, this document is a research agenda rather than completed research; its arguments are promissory rather than evidenced. The taxonomy of design fiction genres presented is explicitly tentative and requires empirical grounding through the workshop itself. The workshop focus on CHI-community perspectives may underrepresent design fiction practices from outside academia or from non-Western contexts. The emphasis on genre classification may inadvertently constrain rather than enable creative design fiction practices.
