# The Human Performance Technology Model

## Summary
This chapter from "Fundamentals of Performance Technology" introduces the Human Performance Technology (HPT) Model, a systematic approach to improving workplace productivity through people-focused interventions. The authors define performance technology as "a systematic, comprehensive approach to improving job performance" that can be applied to individuals, small groups, and large organizations.

The HPT Model consists of four main stages: Performance Analysis (identifying gaps between desired and actual performance), Cause Analysis (determining organizational and individual factors affecting performance), Intervention Selection and Design (choosing comprehensive solutions), and Intervention Implementation and Change (managing the change process). The chapter traces the field's scientific foundations through key contributors including Thomas Gilbert (Behavior Engineering Model), Geary Rummler (components of performance systems), Robert Mager (behavioral objectives), Joe Harless (Front-end Analysis), and Dale Brethower (performance-based instruction).

The authors emphasize that PT is "people-oriented," reflecting core beliefs that people are important, solutions should be beneficial for people and future-oriented, and approaches must be multidimensional and team-oriented. The chapter includes case studies from Visteon Automotive Systems and Health Alliance Plan demonstrating practical application of the HPT Model.

## Key Concepts
- **Performance Technology (PT)**: Systematic, comprehensive approach to improving job performance through methods, procedures, and strategies
- **HPT Model**: Framework with four stages: performance analysis, cause analysis, intervention selection/design, and implementation/change
- **Behavior Engineering Model**: Gilbert's framework identifying six factors affecting performance (data, instruments, incentives, knowledge, capacity, motives)
- **Front-end Analysis (FEA)**: Harless's approach to identifying performance problems and prioritizing solutions
- **Worthy Performance**: Gilbert's concept defining performance as behavior valued for its accomplishment, measured by value divided by cost

## Theoretical Framework
The HPT Model integrates behavioral and systems theories, drawing from Gilbert's behavior engineering, Rummler's organizational performance systems, Mager's objectives-based design, and Harless's analytical approaches. It emphasizes systematic problem-solving and evidence-based intervention selection.

## Methods
The chapter presents the HPT Model conceptually, supported by historical analysis of the field's development and two case studies demonstrating practical application (Visteon Automotive Systems' Performance Improvement Tool Kit and Health Alliance Plan's Human Resource Effectiveness Report).

## Main Arguments
- Greater productivity will be achieved through people, not just machines or cost-cutting
- PT practitioners must begin with performance analysis before jumping to interventions
- Cause analysis should examine both environmental support (data, resources, incentives) and individual repertoire (skills, capacity, motivation)
- Interventions should be selected based on costs and benefits to both organization and employees
- PT is not a "passing bandwagon" because people are organizations' most critical resource in the knowledge era
- Results orientation and measurable outcomes are essential for demonstrating PT's value

## Limitations & Critiques
- PT initiatives are sometimes viewed skeptically as "passing bandwagons" by the business sector
- Training has traditionally been overused as a solution when other interventions may be more appropriate
- Data gathering and analysis can be difficult because workplace performance outcomes are hard to define and measure
- The field requires practitioners to work across disciplines and manage complex stakeholder relationships
- Demonstrating ROI remains a challenge for PT practitioners seeking organizational buy-in
