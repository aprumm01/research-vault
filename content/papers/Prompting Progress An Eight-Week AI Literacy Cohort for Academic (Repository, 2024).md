## Key Concepts
- Community of practice model for professional AI literacy development among academic advisors
- Academic advisors as frontline staff requiring AI partnership capabilities rather than competition with technology
- GPT-4 application to core advising functions including degree planning, policy queries, and email drafting
- AI literacy encompassing familiarity, technical skills, ethical guardrails, and workflow integration
- Responsible AI adoption balancing efficiency gains with high-touch student support maintenance
- Pre/post evaluation demonstrating measurable improvements in familiarity, literacy, and confidence
- Workflow efficiency gains documented through recruitment email production time reduction
- Ethical competency framed as core skill rather than peripheral concern in AI adoption

## Theoretical Framework
The program employs community of practice framework (Wenger) to support collective learning and professional identity evolution around AI tools. The theoretical model positions AI literacy as multidimensional construct including: (1) technical familiarity with tool capabilities, (2) practical competency in task-specific applications, (3) ethical reasoning about appropriate use contexts, and (4) critical evaluation of outputs for accuracy and bias. The eight-week cohort structure provides scaffolded progression from foundational understanding through guided practice to independent application, with peer learning and shared documentation supporting knowledge construction. The framework emphasizes partnership rather than replacement logic, preserving advisors' professional judgment and relationship-building roles while automating routine information processing tasks.

## Methods
survey, literature review

## Main Arguments
- Academic advisors can rapidly develop AI literacy through structured community of practice interventions
- Measured improvements from pre to post show familiarity rising 2.50 to 3.50, overall literacy 2.71 to 3.90, and confidence 3.07 to 4.00 on 5-point scales
- Workflow logs demonstrate efficiency gains in routine tasks like recruitment email generation freeing time for student-centered activities
- Ethical guardrails emerge as new professional competency requiring explicit development rather than assumed understanding
- Community of practice model enables collective problem-solving and shared resource development accelerating adoption
- Advisor-centric programs support responsible AI integration aligned with student support missions
- Successful adoption requires framing AI as partnership tool enhancing rather than threatening professional roles

## Limitations & Critiques
- Small pilot program (15 participants) at single institution limits generalizability across diverse advising contexts
- Eight-week timeframe captures initial adoption but not long-term sustainability or evolving practice patterns
- Self-report measures of literacy and confidence may not align with objective competency assessments
- Limited documentation of actual student outcome impacts beyond advisor efficiency gains
- Does not address unequal access to AI tools across institutions based on licensing costs
- Workflow efficiency gains documented primarily for routine tasks with less evidence for complex advising scenarios
- Potential bias toward participants already interested in AI adoption (volunteer selection effect)
- Lacks comparison group to isolate effects of structured program versus organic adoption patterns
