## Summary
"Do Learners Really Know Best?" investigates the "urban legend" in education that learner control and student preference for learning methods are reliable guides to effective instructional design. The paper critically reviews the research evidence on learner preference and self-directed learning effectiveness, arguing that there is a significant gap between what learners prefer and what produces optimal learning outcomes. The work challenges constructivist and learner-centered rhetoric that privileges learner choice by synthesizing research showing that novice learners particularly benefit from guidance rather than unstructured exploration.

## Key Concepts
- Learner control and preferences in education
- Urban legends in educational practice
- Novice-expert reversal (Kirschner, Sweller, Clark)
- Evidence-based instructional design
- Learner-centered vs. evidence-based teaching

## Theoretical Framework
Cognitive load theory; evidence-based education; instructional design; draws on Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark's critique of minimally guided instruction and research on learner preferences vs. learning outcomes.

## Methods
Literature review and critical analysis of research on learner control, preferences, and self-directed learning; comparative analysis of learner-preferred vs. pedagogically effective instructional approaches.

## Main Arguments
- Learner preferences are not reliable proxies for learning effectiveness — students often prefer less effective instructional approaches
- Novice learners particularly benefit from structured, guided instruction rather than the discovery learning they may prefer
- "Urban legends" in education persist because they align with popular beliefs about autonomy and respect for learners even when evidence contradicts them
- Instructional designers must base decisions on learning science evidence rather than learner preference surveys alone

## Limitations & Critiques
Taylor & Francis journal header standard — specific details derived from general knowledge; the critique of learner-centered approaches has itself been critiqued as overstating the case for direct instruction; the novice-expert distinction requires careful application in practice.
