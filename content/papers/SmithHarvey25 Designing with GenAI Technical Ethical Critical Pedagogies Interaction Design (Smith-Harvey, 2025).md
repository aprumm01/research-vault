# Designing with Generative AI: Towards Technical, Ethical, and Critical Pedagogies in Interaction Design

## Summary
This reflective paper describes pedagogical approaches for introducing generative AI in interaction design (IxD) education at Auckland University of Technology (AUT), drawing on curriculum development from first-year workshops in the Bachelor of Interaction Design. The author examines AI through three lenses: as a UX design paradigm, as a tool, and as an ethical subject. Case studies from first-year workshops—including an introduction to GenAI and "vibe coding" sessions—document how students develop foundational AI fluency while critically examining authorship, bias, and ethical positioning. Published at SIGGRAPH Asia 2025 Educator's Forum.

## Key Concepts
- **Technical pedagogy for AI**: Teaching students how AI tools work and how to use them effectively in design workflows, including LLMs, agentic systems, and image/text generators
- **Ethical pedagogy for AI**: Framing AI as an ethical subject requiring critical examination of bias, authorship, data use, indigenous knowledge sensitivity, and environmental impact
- **Critical pedagogy for AI**: Moving students beyond passive automation toward principled, process-oriented design practice that interrogates AI's socio-technical implications
- **Vibe coding**: An emerging practice where designers use natural language prompts to direct AI code generation without precise technical specifications, representing a new human-computer interaction paradigm
- **Experiential and reflective learning**: Kolb's (2014) and Boyd and Fales' (1983) frameworks applied to AI integration—students learn through doing and critically analyzing their AI interactions
- **AI as UX paradigm**: Nielsen's (2023) framing of "intent-based outcome specification" where natural language prompts guide AI systems, reshaping what interaction design means
- **Digital workbook documentation**: AUT's institutional requirement that students document their critical thinking, methods, and iterative processes as a pedagogical device for developing reflective practice

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on Kolb's (2014) experiential learning cycle and Boyd and Fales' (1983) reflective learning theory, positioning AI integration within AUT's established studio-based, experiential pedagogy. Students learn through doing—engaging directly with AI tools—and critically analyzing those experiences in documented reflections. This framework treats AI not as a subject to be learned about but as a medium to learn through, with critical reflection on the learning experience itself as the pedagogical mechanism.

The paper also draws on Wiberg and Stolterman Bergqvist's (2023) analysis of how AI reshapes the interaction designer's role and identity, and on work examining AI's socio-ecological implications (labor devaluation, cognitive atrophy, environmental impact). These concerns position GenAI as an ethical subject requiring critical IxD attention rather than merely a productivity tool to be mastered.

## Methods
Reflective practitioner research based on curriculum development and workshop delivery in two 30-point, 13-week first-year studio courses in the Bachelor of Interaction Design at AUT. The case studies include two workshops: (1) Introduction to GenAI as foundational knowledge, framing AI as a tool, medium, and ethical subject; and (2) a vibe coding workshop introducing generative AI as a new UX/UI design paradigm. Evidence includes course design documentation, student workbook reflections, and the author's own pedagogical experience. The paper uses a case study methodology grounded in the author's reflective practice, building on prior work on adaptive pedagogy during COVID-19 disruptions and early AI experiments in IxD education.

## Main Arguments
- Effective AI integration in interaction design education requires three simultaneous pedagogical registers—technical, ethical, and critical—rather than treating AI as simply a new tool to add to existing workflows
- Vibe coding represents a genuinely new design paradigm in which natural language prompt articulation becomes a core design skill, requiring interaction designers to develop competencies that bridge design intent and AI interpretation
- Framing AI as an ethical subject in design education—through discussions of bias, Māori and indigenous knowledge sensitivities, environmental impact, and authorship questions—prepares students to be critical practitioners rather than uncritical adopters
- The experiential and reflective learning approach is particularly well-suited to AI pedagogy because it structures critical examination of AI interactions as a learning practice, preventing the uncritical passive consumption that unreflective tool use encourages
- Documentation requirements (digital workbooks, APA-style citation of AI use including prompts) serve as pedagogical devices that develop transparency habits and metacognitive awareness of AI's role in student work
- There is currently a dearth of studies on how to meaningfully integrate GenAI into IxD pedagogy specifically; this paper contributes practical insights from curriculum development that can inform other interaction design programs navigating similar challenges

## Limitations & Critiques
The reflective practitioner methodology draws entirely on the author's single-institution experience at AUT, limiting generalizability to other design programs, educational systems, or cultural contexts. The New Zealand/AUT context—with its specific institutional AI strategy, Māori cultural considerations, and design education culture—may not map directly onto other programs. The paper is primarily descriptive rather than evaluative, reporting pedagogical approaches without systematic assessment of student learning outcomes.

As an early-stage curriculum reflection rather than a mature program evaluation, the paper cannot offer evidence about the long-term effectiveness of the described approaches or whether students develop the technical, ethical, and critical AI competencies the curriculum targets. The "vibe coding" concept, while newly relevant, may represent a rapidly shifting landscape where specific practices evolve faster than curriculum can adapt.
