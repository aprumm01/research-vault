# Artificial intelligence and the future of work – Lessons from the sociology of expectations

## Overview of the Document

This paper was authored by Lilla Vicsek, a researcher whose institutional affiliation reflects work funded by the Hungarian Scientific Research Fund through the project "Visions on Artificial Intelligence and Society" (K131733). The document was published in 2021 in the International Journal of Sociology and Social Policy, Volume 41, Numbers 7/8, pages 842-861. This is a theoretical and critical analysis paper situated at the intersection of sociology of expectations (SE), science and technology studies (STS), and future-of-work discourse. Vicsek works within scholarly traditions examining how technological visions shape social futures, building particularly on economic sociologist Jens Beckert's work on fictional expectations and Bruno Brown's sociology of expectations framework.

The paper matters because dominant expert discourse about AI and employment has significant consequences for policy development, research funding, investment priorities, and public understanding. Major international organizations like the OECD and ILO have published studies on this topic, governments have developed strategies to address potential automation impacts, and the issue receives substantial media coverage. If these visions contain problematic assumptions—as Vicsek argues—then they risk foreclosing alternative pathways and producing blind spots about what actually matters. The document represents a critical intervention arguing that how we talk about AI futures may be more consequential than the technical capabilities themselves, because expectations performatively shape what gets built, funded, and legitimized.

The work engages two dominant ideal-type positions in Western expert discourse: the "Positive Effects" position (new jobs will be created, productivity gains will benefit society, analogous to previous industrial revolutions) and the "Negative Effects" position (massive permanent unemployment, dystopian inequality, this time is different because cognitive tasks are automatable). Vicsek argues both positions share problematic characteristics: they give agency to technology rather than humans, involve hype rhetoric that obscures uncertainty, present polarized visions that foreclose nuanced alternatives, and focus on effects rather than how AI is developed and governed. The paper proposes that sociology of expectations can reveal these hidden assumptions and suggest better ways forward, particularly through scenario building and backcasting methodologies that take uncertainty and co-shaping seriously.

## Research Overview

The central research question asks: How can the sociology of expectations framework enhance understanding of dominant expert positions regarding AI and the future of work, and what are the implications of current framings of this debate? More specifically, Vicsek investigates what assumptions undergird popular visions of AI's employment effects, what consequences these particular formulations might have, and how alternative framings might advance more sophisticated understanding. The research was conducted by Lilla Vicsek in Hungary during 2019-2021 as part of a broader research project on AI and society.

The study employs a theoretical analysis methodology rather than empirical data collection. As Vicsek explains, "After providing a literature review regarding SE, an approach based on the latter is applied to interpret the dominant ideal-type expert positions in the future of work debate to illustrate the value of this perspective" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 12). The method involves interpreting what is implied by ideal-type expectations, identifying what gets left out, and discussing potential consequences of such formulations and omissions. The author constructs two ideal types building on Weber's concept, acknowledging that "not all of the characteristics I identify as components of the ideal types are present in individual expert projections in exactly the same way" (p. 112), but arguing these abstractions help interpret foremost divisions in the debate.

Key research concepts include fictional expectations (future projections that create their own reality by making assertions beyond empirical facts, acknowledging fundamental uncertainty), constitutive role of visions (how future projections legitimize, show direction, and coordinate actions of diverse actors in the present), technological determinism (attributing agency primarily to technology rather than social choices), hype and disappointment cycles (temporal patterning of inflated positive expectations followed by setbacks), and co-shaping (dynamic mutual influence between technology and society rather than unidirectional causation).

The paper draws on extensive literature across sociology, STS, and future-of-work scholarship. Vicsek cites economists like David Autor and James Bessen (Positive Effects position), futurists like Martin Ford and Yuval Noah Harari (Negative Effects position), and sociologists of expectations like Nik Brown, Harro van Lente, and Jens Beckert. The definitional approach to AI adopts Anthony Elliott's definition: "any computational system which can sense its environment, think, learn and react in response (and cope with surprises) to such data-sensing. AI-related technologies may include both robots and purely digital systems that employ learning methods" (Elliott, 2019, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 75).

As Vicsek notes, sociological theory has "fundamentally concentrated on the past and present" with less theoretical apparatus for engaging futures (Mische, 2009, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 58), which motivates the turn to sociology of expectations as a framework specifically designed to analyze how future visions do work in the present. The author states that "studying visions in their own right can substantially advance our understanding of the social implications of imagining futures in certain ways" (Brown et al., 2017, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 70).

## Theories of Knowledge

The paper centers on **sociology of expectations (SE)** as its primary theoretical framework, an area within sociology and STS dealing with the constitutive role of future visions in innovation and technology. SE scholars argue that "it is important to study how the future is viewed by actors, as such perspectives can play a relevant part in what happens in the present and future" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 154). The framework assumes fundamental uncertainty and openness toward the future on one hand, and mechanisms that foreclose certain routes on the other. As Vicsek explains, "expectations can on the one hand involve novelties and increase indeterminacy about the future, but on the other, when powerful, can result in the shaping of the future by influencing actions and values" (p. 176).

**Beckert's theory of fictional expectations** extends SE by emphasizing that "under conditions of uncertainty, assessments of how the future will look share important characteristics with literary fiction; most importantly, they create a reality of their own by making assertions that go beyond the reporting of empirical facts" (Beckert, 2016, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 171). This framework treats future projections not as predictions to be evaluated for accuracy, but as performative statements that legitimate, coordinate, and direct action in the present. Beckert and Bronk (2019) argue that "uncertainty and indeterminacy are indeed key characteristics of modern economies because 'relentless innovation' and novelty characterize the latter, while modern society is comprised of a complexity of systems that can change radically and tip" (cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 163).

**Technological and social determinism** appear as analytical categories for understanding agency attribution. Mackenzie and Wajcman's (1999) work on social shaping of technology provides the foundation for critiquing technological determinism, which Vicsek defines as occurring when "technological elements and their 'capacity to bend humans to their character' are seen as stronger than the social element in future developments" (Urry, 2016, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 225). Technologically deterministic narratives present further development as "inevitable, taken-for-granted, the logical next step," obscuring "the role of many influential actors and contingencies: it is blind to power, to culture, to how values can change over time, to path-dependence related to earlier technologies, and to the role of visions that shape the future" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 232).

**Co-evolution and co-shaping frameworks** from STS provide alternative conceptualizations. Geels and Smit (2017) argue for "a perspective that takes into account the dynamic co-evolution of technology and society" rather than unidirectional causation (cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 237). This theoretical lens suggests that "the complex ways in which people interact with new technologies fundamentally reshapes the further development of those very technologies" (Elliott, 2019, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 343).

**Weber's ideal type methodology** provides the analytical tool for constructing abstract models where "certain characteristics are accentuated to help in the interpretation of social reality" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 111). This allows Vicsek to identify patterns across diverse expert writings without claiming every individual text exhibits all characteristics identically.

**Kuhnian normal science** appears implicitly when Vicsek discusses how fictional expectations contribute to "closure and a lack of consideration of alternatives" when "collective imaginaries can take the status of taken-for-granted futures that no longer have to be justified" (Konrad, 2006, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 190). This parallels Kuhn's concept of paradigmatic thinking that makes certain questions unaskable.

**Mische's projectivity theory** provides concepts for analyzing future orientations, particularly the distinction between viewing ourselves "walking forwards into a future in which choices are laid out before us" versus "walking backwards into an unknowable future, in which possibilities flow out behind us from our actions" (Facer, 2013, building on Mische, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 376). This metaphorical distinction captures different relationships between present action and future possibility.

**Hype cycle theory** from Van Lente et al. (2013) analyzes "temporal patterning, with interchanging cycles of hype and disappointment which can influence innovation activity" (cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 250). Hype is understood not as faulty prediction but as "very strong positive expectations that gain a high level of exposure and attention that may have the performative role of attracting resources, alliances, and coordinating and legitimizing action" (p. 253).

External frameworks enriching interpretation include **scenario building and backcasting methodologies** from futures studies. Scenario building "has a commitment to multiple future possibilities" and helps "participants to rethink how they perceive reality, to challenge their assumptions and prevailing mind-sets" (Bradfield et al., 2005, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 602). Backcasting "creates a normative vision of an ideal future, and works its way back to the present" and "assumes that actors deliberately attempt to engender a desired future" (Robinson, 2003, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 610).

## Central Arguments

Vicsek advances a central argument that dominant expert positions on AI and the future of work contain problematic assumptions that foreclose important conversations and pathways, and that applying a sociology of expectations lens can reveal these limitations while suggesting more productive framings. The author states: "Dominant future scripts can be characterized by a focus on the effects of AI technology that give agency to technology and to the future, involve the hype of expectations with polarized frames, and obscure uncertainty" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 16).

The first major sub-argument concerns agency attribution and technological determinism. Vicsek argues that "as both ideal-type expert positions concentrate on the effects of technology, agency is awarded to AI technology from both a Positive Effects and Negative Effects perspective, whilst reduced agency is often only attributed to humans, governments, or other organizations (in relation to getting ready for and responding to changes caused by technology)" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 319). This framing has consequences: "a technologically deterministic discourse that primarily awards agency to technology" hides alternative questions like "How could technology be developed in a way so as to contribute to the creation of a desirable situation with respect to work?" or "Who should have a say in how AI is developed, how it is implemented, and in what ways?" (p. 346-349). The author emphasizes that "these alternative questions are concealed in the two technologically deterministic perspectives that concentrate on effects and lead to the grounding assumptions of the debate that make some conversations possible (such as what should governments do to help with reskilling, or how can individuals prepare for the future), while hindering others" (p. 353).

The second argument addresses hype rhetoric and its consequences. Both ideal types involve "high expectations about what technology will be capable of in the near future, as well as with regard to its dissemination" using expressions like "fourth industrial revolution" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 419). The author contends that "a consequence of this hype rhetoric may be that technological bottlenecks are obscured and potential issues with the spread of a technology are not taken into account" (p. 424). Crucially, Vicsek notes that "speculation about what will happen with the development of AI and robotics is not a new phenomenon. Several periods of hype and disappointment with AI have already taken place" (p. 448), citing examples where predictions did not materialize at expected speeds.

The third argument concerns the obscuring of uncertainty through polarization. Vicsek argues that "applying the category of 'breadth' – which involves analyzing how many possible trajectories are seen by actors who make prognoses about the future – we often see that only one outcome is claimed to be likely to happen in the future" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 502). The use of precise-sounding statistics and numbers "can all lead to the downplaying of the uncertainty involved in predicting the future" (p. 509). While the two positions oppose each other, creating "some degree of uncertainty... in societal discourse," their polarization into utopian and apocalyptic genres results in "such work becoming too one-dimensional, one-sided, and lacking in consideration of the multiple directions the future might take" (p. 522).

The fourth argument asserts that focusing on future effects obscures present problems. Vicsek contends that "both Positive Effects and Negative Effects fictional expectations focus on the future, they both leave issues of the present in the background. For example, the social and business arrangements and practices that contribute to the production of AI are typically neglected" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 382). This includes "huge, low-paid, insecure workforce working on training and tuning algorithms," concentration of "wealth and power... in a small number of huge companies – over which, according to some authors, there is not enough democratic oversight," and how "social characteristics (such as the culture of the designers of AI) influence technological development, which results in solutions which are gender and race biased" (p. 386-396).

The fifth argument proposes that expectations themselves shape futures through performativity. Discussing Moore's law, Vicsek argues that "when major actors believe in this law, this steers their actions and acts as a self-fulfilling prophecy. Aims are formulated in accordance with Moore's law, and research and development funds are allocated according to these expectations" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 411). This reveals expectations as "just examples of 'forceful fiction' related to the topic of AI" rather than natural laws (p. 416).

The final argument advocates for alternative methodological and theoretical approaches. Vicsek argues that "more sophisticated theorizing is needed which goes beyond these positions and which takes uncertainty and the mutual shaping of technology and society into account – including the role expectations play" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 20). The author proposes that "scenario building and backcasting are two tools that could help move forward thinking about the future of work – especially if this is done in a way so as to build strongly on SE" (p. 26). These methods should incorporate insights from SE that typical scenario work overlooks, "such as the factors that some argue can change technology-society configurations, such as values, culture changes over time, lock-ins due to current solutions, resistance and conflict in relation to technologies, as well as placing more focus on issues of power, inequality, and marginalization" (p. 699).

## Evidence

The evidence supporting these arguments comes primarily from analysis of published expert discourse rather than empirical data collection. Vicsek cites specific examples from both ideal-type positions. For the Negative Effects position, the author references Frey and Osborne's (2013) influential study that "look at how technology will be capable of doing what is required for specific jobs and equate this capability with the rate of unemployment that may be expected as a consequence" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 433), exemplifying functionalist assumptions about technology diffusion. Ford (2015), Harari (2018), and Tegmark (2017) represent this position with claims about massive permanent unemployment. Harari's argument that "people will not be able to reeducate themselves at the speed that technological development will require, and new kinds of work for individuals will simply not exist this time, as cognitive tasks are at risk of automation" (cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 143) exemplifies the "this time is different" rhetoric.

For the Positive Effects position, Vicsek cites Autor (2015), Bessen (2016, 2019), Miller and Atkinson (2013), and Peters (2017) who argue that "new kinds of jobs will be created, thus unemployment will not be so massive, and may not even be a problem at all (although there might be a period of transition), or that AI will assist humans in their work in many cases but not take over whole jobs" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 133). The "this time it will be the same" rhetoric draws "analogies... recurrently... with the industrial revolution" (p. 139).

Evidence for hype rhetoric includes references to Moore's law being "recurrently brought up to support claims that development is occurring more rapidly" with the assumption that "AI is assumed to develop exponentially as well" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 124). The author notes expressions like "fourth industrial revolution" (Schwab, 2017) characterizing current discourse. Historical evidence shows that "it was anticipated in the last decades of the twentieth century that, by the beginning of the 2010s, domestic robots that were capable of doing a range of housework would be widespread. This did not happen" (Urry, 2016, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 451), demonstrating past failures of hyped predictions.

Media analysis provides supporting evidence: "Brennen et al. (2018) and Chuan et al. (2019) show that the two ideal types are not just present in scientific and pop-scientific work, but appear in media coverage as well" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 145), indicating widespread diffusion of these framings.

Evidence for agency attribution comes from linguistic analysis. The popular question "will robots take our jobs?" and websites like willrobotstakemyjob.com that "calculate the probability of specific jobs being taken over" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 326) exemplify how robots as actors receive agency in discourse. Vicsek argues this framing "shifts the focus from software to robots, often with a focus on how humans might be substituted by the latter. It leaves the issue of software, programs, and algorithms in the background" (p. 330).

The research method carries inherent limitations. As theoretical analysis of discourse, it does not empirically test whether these expectations actually produce the foreclosing effects Vicsek claims. The ideal type construction, while analytically useful, necessarily simplifies complex and varied expert positions. As Vicsek acknowledges, "not all of the characteristics I identify as components of the ideal types are present in individual expert projections in exactly the same way" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 112). Some nuanced expert work may already incorporate co-shaping perspectives or acknowledge uncertainty more than the ideal types suggest.

The paper's geographic and cultural scope focuses primarily on Western expert discourse, particularly English-language scholarship and media. Vicsek notes this explicitly: "the two major circulating visions of the future of work that dominate this debate by experts in the West" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 106). This leaves open questions about whether non-Western contexts exhibit different patterns. The author briefly mentions that "other ideal types could be constructed as well" (p. 107) and that "there may be national-level differences in outcomes" (p. 677), but does not systematically explore these variations.

The methodology privileges certain voices—published expert discourse—over others like workers, managers implementing automation, or AI developers themselves. While Vicsek argues that expert visions influence policy and investment, the paper provides limited evidence of actual causal pathways from discourse to outcomes. The claim that "it is argued that the related writings have influenced the understanding of a number of governments and think tanks regarding future policies" (Bailey and Barley, 2019, cited in Vicsek, 2021, p. 147) rests on others' assertions rather than demonstrated mechanisms.

The paper's own normative commitments—favoring democratic participation, reducing inequality, attending to marginalized groups—while explicitly stated, shape which consequences count as problematic. Vicsek's concern that dominant framings "make some conversations possible, while hindering others" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 18) reflects particular political values about what conversations should be possible, though many readers will share these commitments.

## Conclusion

This research contributes a critical framework for understanding how dominant expert discourse about AI and employment may be as consequential as the technology itself. By applying sociology of expectations to future-of-work debates, Vicsek demonstrates that both major positions—despite their apparent opposition—share underlying assumptions that give agency to technology, obscure uncertainty, involve hype rhetoric, and focus on effects rather than development processes. These shared characteristics have important implications: they foreclose conversations about democratic governance of AI, obscure present inequalities in the AI industry, downplay fundamental uncertainty about how technology-society configurations will actually unfold, and make certain policy responses (adaptation, reskilling) seem natural while rendering others (shaping development priorities, regulating deployment) invisible or impractical.

The paper's immediate contribution to UX and HCI communities lies in revealing how the "will robots take our jobs?" framing obscures questions about how intelligent systems should be designed, who should participate in design decisions, and what values should guide development. For practitioners, this suggests that debates about whether to build AI-augmented tools versus full automation represent not technical questions but political choices about whose interests get prioritized and what kind of work futures we collectively create.

The theoretical contribution extends sociology by demonstrating how SE can analyze not just specific technologies but entire socio-technical futures, showing how expectations operate at multiple levels—specific projects, whole technology fields, and macro-level framings linking technology to social problems. The methodological proposal to integrate SE with scenario building and backcasting offers concrete tools for practitioners and policymakers seeking to move beyond polarized visions.

What remains open are empirical questions about causality and mechanisms. While Vicsek argues convincingly that dominant framings foreclose alternatives, the paper does not demonstrate how this foreclosure actually occurs in specific policy processes, funding decisions, or development priorities. Future research could trace how particular expectations influenced specific outcomes, identifying the actors, institutions, and moments where alternatives got excluded.

Also unresolved is whether the co-shaping perspective Vicsek advocates can itself become a dominant imaginary with its own blind spots. If everyone agrees technology and society mutually shape each other, does this framing enable certain actions while hindering others? The paper's critique of determinism is itself a form of expectation-shaping that privileges human agency and democratic participation—values worth defending, but which nonetheless reflect particular political commitments rather than neutral analysis.

The paper opens questions about what makes some expectations powerful while others remain marginal. SE theory explains that "not all expectations have the same status," and "it is relevant who has the power to make their projections count" (Vicsek, 2021, p. 184), but the mechanisms producing this differential power deserve further investigation. Why did Frey and Osborne's 2013 study become so influential despite methodological critiques? What institutional positions, rhetorical strategies, or network connections make certain visions "count"?

Finally, the COVID-19 pandemic that occurred during this paper's publication raises questions about how major disruptions reshape future expectations. Vicsek's call for considering "potential disruptions and surprises" takes on new urgency post-pandemic, as massive shifts to remote work, accelerated automation in some sectors, and increased precarity have altered both material conditions and imaginaries about work futures. How resilient are the dominant framings Vicsek critiques when confronted with such systemic shocks?

## Discussion Questions

1. Vicsek argues that both positive and negative expert positions give agency to technology rather than humans, foreclosing questions about how AI should be developed and governed. But doesn't highlighting technology's agency serve a useful rhetorical purpose for mobilizing political response and funding? Might "robots taking our jobs" be a necessary simplification to make complex issues politically tractable, even if sociologically inadequate?

2. The paper advocates for scenario building and backcasting that incorporate SE insights about uncertainty, power, and co-shaping. But if multiple sophisticated scenarios replace polarized predictions, does this diffusion of visions actually reduce the performative force of any single imaginary? Could embracing radical uncertainty paradoxically make it harder to coordinate action and allocate resources, since strong (even if oversimplified) expectations legitimate investment?

3. Vicsek critiques how current framings obscure present problems in AI development—exploited workers training algorithms, concentrated corporate power, embedded biases—by focusing on future employment effects. Yet isn't anticipating future consequences precisely what responsible governance requires? How can we balance attention to present injustices with necessary speculation about future impacts, without one systematically crowding out the other?

4. The paper treats both utopian and dystopian visions as equally problematic because they share deterministic assumptions and hype rhetoric. But might these positions play different political roles—with dystopian warnings mobilizing regulation and worker protections, while utopian promises legitimate continued development? If so, should we evaluate them differently based on consequences rather than shared formal characteristics, even if this means strategically endorsing narratives we know are oversimplified?
