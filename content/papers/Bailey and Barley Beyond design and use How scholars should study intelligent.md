## Summary
This paper proposes a unified approach to studying intelligent technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) that extends current studies of design and use. Current discussion of the implication of AI and the future of work gloss four important issues: variation, power, ideology, and institutions. By a unified approach the authors mean a research agenda that coordinates studies of variation in use with research on power, ideology, design, and institutional change, all focused on a specific technology or set of technologies.

## Key Concepts
- Unified approach - research agenda coordinating studies of variation in use with research on power, ideology, design, and institutional change across a technology's trajectory
- Technology timeline - conceptual framework extending from power/ideology (left) through design, implementation, and use to institutional change (right)
- Intelligent technologies - AI and associated technologies including machine learning, big data, robotics, smart sensors, Internet of things, and analytics
- Variation in use - contextual differences in how organizations implement technologies and people integrate them into work practices, producing varied outcomes
- Design ideology - cultural perspectives of AI designers that privilege technical over social considerations, shaping whether technologies automate or augment work
- Artificial intelligence paradigm - design philosophy focused on removing humans from the loop and replacing them with intelligent technologies (McCarthy tradition)
- Augmented intelligence paradigm - alternative design philosophy emphasizing technologies that complement and assist workers (Engelbart tradition)
- Closure - point at which a technology's dominant design becomes cemented, after which opportunities for altering design and purpose narrow significantly
- Institutional change - broad societal transformations (family, transportation, marriage) arising from widespread technology adoption beyond organizational boundaries
- Action research - using research findings to shape design of new technologies prior to workplace adoption to avoid dire forecasts

## Theoretical Framework
- Social Construction of Technology (SCOT) - technologies' meanings and forms emerge through social processes involving multiple stakeholder groups with competing visions
- Structuration theory (Orlikowski 1992) - technology development and use shaped by interaction of designers, users, institutional properties, and technology itself
- Technology biography approach (Williams & Pollock 2012) - studying technology's development across space and time, from design through implementation to ongoing evolution
- Adaptive structuration theory (Poole & DeSanctis) - distinguishing faithful vs. ironic appropriation based on whether users employ technology consistent with designers' intentions
- Critical systems thinking - engaging stakeholders in scenario building to imagine ideal institutions and tracing paths to achieve them through technology design

## Methods
- Conceptual/theoretical paper proposing research agenda rather than presenting empirical study
- Literature review synthesizing SCOT, design studies, and technology-work-organization scholarship
- Case examples illustrating unified approach: self-driving commercial trucks, electronic on-board recorders in trucking, police predictive algorithms
- Historical analysis of technology studies: numerically controlled machine tools (Noble), bicycle evolution (Pinch & Bijker), expert systems (Forsythe)
- Comparative analysis of research traditions positioned along technology timeline from design to use

## Main Arguments
- Current AI discourse obscures four critical issues: variation in implementation/use across contexts, power dynamics shaping design agendas, design ideologies determining automation vs. augmentation, and institutional changes beyond workplace
- Scholars of technology, work, and organization must extend research both left (power/ideology in design) and right (institutional consequences) of the use-focused studies that have dominated the field since 1980s
- The idea that technology implications are solely situated, contextual, and emergent at point of use is insufficient—designers' visions and powerful stakeholders' agendas shape outcomes before implementation
- Timing is critical: once intelligent technologies achieve closure, opportunities to influence design and purposes narrow dramatically, making proactive engagement urgent
- Studies of variation in use remain essential but must be coordinated with investigation of who funds/authorizes design, what ideologies shape design, and what institutions are affected
- Power dynamics drive technology adoption: Thomas showed that regardless of real reasons for wanting new technology, engineers could only justify purchases by appealing to efficiency and lower labor costs
- Design ideologies matter: Forsythe demonstrated AI designers' cultural perspective privileged technical over social, while Markoff contrasted AI (replacing humans) vs. augmented intelligence (complementing humans) paradigms
- Institutional effects extend far beyond workplace: widespread unemployment from AI could transform family life (Hochschild's findings on work as refuge), transportation infrastructure (commute-centered systems), urban planning (hollowed-out city cores)
- Current methodological approaches are too narrow: SCOT focuses on design, technology-work-organization focuses on use, but neither adequately bridges to power/ideology (left) or institutions (right)
- Interdisciplinary teams are essential: no single scholar can cover power, ideology, design, use, and institutional change—requires collaboration across disciplines with coordinated parallel studies

## Limitations & Critiques
- Ambitious unified approach may prove impractical: assembling diverse interdisciplinary teams, coordinating epistemologically different research traditions, and maintaining coherence across parallel studies poses significant pragmatic challenges
- Authors assume technology firms and powerful stakeholders will engage with scholars, but evidence is thin—citations of ethics panels and consortia may overstate willingness to share power or alter profit-driven agendas
- Proposal lacks concrete methodological guidance: how to actually "coordinate" disconnected studies, establish shared research questions, or integrate findings from SCOT historians, ethnographers, policy analysts, and institutional sociologists remains vague
- Action research goal conflicts with descriptive research: influencing design while maintaining scholarly objectivity and distance creates tension not addressed in framework
- Timeline metaphor oversimplifies: treats technology trajectory as linear when machine learning systems blur design/use boundaries (footnote acknowledges but doesn't resolve this)
- Self-driving truck example is illustrative rather than demonstrative—authors don't actually conduct or show results from unified approach, only point to existing separate studies
- Authors' call to "step forward to lead teams" presumes scholars have agency and resources to redirect large research agendas, but structural barriers (funding, tenure pressures, disciplinary silos) not addressed
- Historical examples (Noble, bicycle) span decades—unclear whether real-time studies of power/ideology are feasible when intelligent technologies develop rapidly
- Missing attention to global power dynamics: framework assumes US/Western context, but AI development increasingly involves Chinese firms, governments, and different regulatory regimes
- Proposal doesn't address potential conflicts of interest when researchers receive funding from technology firms while claiming to study power dynamics that benefit those same firms
