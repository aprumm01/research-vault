## Summary
Süner-Pla-Cerda, Töre-Yargin, and Günay investigate how industrial design (ID) graduates construct and negotiate their professional identities as they transition into UX-affiliated roles, publishing in Design Issues (vol. 39, no. 2, Spring 2023, MIT Press). Semi-structured interviews with 14 ID graduates working in diverse UX positions in Turkey examine their motivations, perceived competence, and identity work. The study finds that industrial designers are drawn to UX by the satisfaction of autonomy, competence, relatedness, and beneficence -- particularly the more tangible user contact and interdisciplinary collaboration that UX affords compared to traditional manufacturing-focused ID work. Designers construct their professional legitimacy around three competence narratives: design methodology, design mindset, and a grounded approach to user-centered decisions.

## Key Concepts
- Professional identity and narrative identity work in design transitions
- Self-determination theory (SDT) applied to occupational motivation: autonomy, competence, relatedness, beneficence
- Three identity-legitimating competences: methodology, mindset, grounded approach
- UX as welcoming interdisciplinary newcomers; design background as differentiating asset
- Gaps between ID education and UX expectations: UI/visual design, digital prototyping, UX terminology
- Becoming vs. knowing: ontological approach to professionalization (Dall'Alba)

## Theoretical Framework
Qualitative occupational identity research; draws on Dall'Alba's ontological framework of professional ways of being (becoming rather than knowing); self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan) for occupational motivation; identity work and narrative identity work concepts (Barker Caza et al., Ibarra and Barbulescu); situated within design education and professionalization scholarship; Design Issues journal (MIT Press, 2023, doi: 10.1162/desi_a_00715); Turkish higher design education context.

## Methods
Semi-structured interviews with 14 ID graduates (10 women, 4 men) working in UX-affiliated positions in Turkey; diverse experience levels (0-1 year to 10+ years post-graduation); purposive sampling with snowball extension from an ID faculty network; interviews approximately one hour each, voice-recorded, transcribed, and imported to Atlas.ti; inductive open-coding for narrative themes using content analysis.

## Main Arguments
- Industrial designers are motivated to pursue UX roles by autonomy (flexible, experimental work culture), competence (immediate feedback and validation), relatedness (interdisciplinary team culture), and beneficence (tangible positive impact on users' daily lives) -- all of which are more accessible in UX than in traditional ID industry
- Designers construct professional legitimacy through three competence narratives: (1) design methodology (transferable process skills like user research, prototyping, iteration), (2) design mindset (holistic, problem-framing orientation), and (3) a grounded approach (user-centered decision-making that is evidence-based rather than aesthetically driven)
- Designers differentiate themselves from non-design UX entrants by emphasizing the rigor and transferability of their ID training, while also acknowledging gaps in UI/visual design and digital prototyping
- Lack of UX-specific technical skills (terminology, UI conventions, coding/digital prototyping) produces feelings of insufficiency and damages design confidence despite formal design training
- UX is experienced as an incomplete, open-ended professional identity -- one that is continuously refined through experience rather than fixed at graduation

## Limitations & Critiques
The study is limited to 14 participants in Turkey, where design education is highly specialized and the UX job market has specific structural characteristics; findings may not generalize to other national or educational contexts. The sample was sourced through the authors' existing professional network, introducing potential selection bias toward participants with more positive transitions. The narrative data reflects self-reported professional identity constructions, which may differ from observed practice. The study predates widespread GenAI adoption in design work, which may alter how designers construct their professional legitimacy.
