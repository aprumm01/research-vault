# Computer-Mediated Discourse Analysis: An Approach to Researching Online Behavior

## Summary
This chapter introduces Computer-Mediated Discourse Analysis (CMDA) as a methodological approach for researching language and language use in computer-mediated environments. Herring positions CMDA within the broader linguistic paradigm of discourse analysis while acknowledging its interdisciplinary applications across communication studies, education, information science, and social sciences. The approach applies methods adapted from language-focused disciplines such as linguistics, rhetoric, and conversation analysis to analyze the verbal features of computer-mediated communication (CMC).

The author organizes CMDA around four domains of language: structure (including typography, morphology, syntax), meaning (including word meanings, utterance meanings, macrosegment meanings), interaction (including turns, sequences, exchanges), and social behavior (including linguistic expressions of status, conflict, play, and community). Herring provides detailed guidance on formulating research questions, selecting data, operationalizing concepts, and applying both qualitative and quantitative analytical techniques. She illustrates these principles through a worked example analyzing whether a virtual community qualifies as a "community" based on participation criteria.

## Key Concepts
- **Computer-Mediated Discourse (CMD)**: The communication produced when humans interact with one another by transmitting messages via networked computers
- **Four Domains of Language**: Structure, Meaning, Interaction, and Social Behavior as hierarchical levels of analysis
- **Virtual Community**: A group of people who primarily interact through computer-mediated communication, characterized by active participation, shared history, and group-specific communication norms
- **Operationalization**: The process of identifying specific, observable linguistic behaviors that constitute evidence for abstract concepts

## Theoretical Framework
CMDA is grounded in discourse analysis traditions from linguistics and sociolinguistics, treating language as a window into human behavior and social phenomena. The approach assumes that observable linguistic behavior can be used to make inferences about communication processes, social identity, and group dynamics. Unlike approaches focused on user perceptions or technological features, CMDA privileges the analysis of naturally occurring discourse as primary evidence.

## Methods
CMDA employs both qualitative and quantitative techniques depending on research goals. Qualitative analysis examines message content, structure, and context through close reading. Quantitative analysis involves coding, counting, and statistical testing of linguistic features across samples. The author recommends random sampling of CMC logs, developing coding schemes based on operationalized concepts, and using statistical tests appropriate for categorical data (chi-square, log likelihood).

## Main Arguments
- CMC should be studied primarily through analysis of the discourse it produces, not solely through surveys or experimental manipulations
- Language-focused analysis can reveal social phenomena that users themselves may not be consciously aware of
- The four domains of language provide a systematic framework for addressing different types of research questions about online behavior
- Concepts like "virtual community" can be empirically investigated by operationalizing their defining features as observable discourse behaviors
- CMDA can be combined with other methods (surveys, experiments, ethnography) for richer understanding

## Limitations & Critiques
- CMDA requires language-focused training that researchers from other disciplines may lack
- The approach is limited to text-based CMC and may need adaptation for multimodal environments
- Large-scale corpus analysis requires substantial technical and methodological expertise
- The focus on observable language may miss important non-verbal or contextual factors in communication
- Operationalizing abstract social concepts can be reductive if not done carefully
