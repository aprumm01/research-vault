# Embedding Cooperative Learning into the Design of Integrated Learning Systems: Rationale and Guidelines

## Summary
This paper addresses the tension between Integrated Learning Systems (ILS) and cooperative learning principles. ILSs are advanced computer-based instructional systems consisting of computerized courseware covering multiple grade levels, classroom management and reporting features, and supplementary materials. While ILSs have become popular in K-12 schools (11-25% of US schools owned ILSs at the time of publication, accounting for 50% of educational software purchases), they introduce potential negative effects on students' academic and social growth due to their emphasis on individualized instruction.

The author identifies three key problems with traditional ILS design: (a) de-emphasis of affective outcomes and increased student isolation, (b) lack of teacher involvement in curriculum planning and delivery, and (c) disparate effects on student achievement based on academic level, with middle-achieving students benefiting least. The paper argues that integrating cooperative learning strategies into ILS instruction could address these concerns while maintaining the benefits of computer-based instruction.

Brush provides detailed guidelines for embedding cooperative learning components into ILS design, focusing on three key elements: positive interdependence (structuring activities so group success depends on individual contributions), individual accountability (ensuring each student masters the material), and collaborative skills training (teaching students how to work effectively in groups). The paper concludes with a proposed redesigned ILS architecture that integrates cooperative group assignments, collaborative training activities, and modified courseware sequences.

## Key Concepts
- **Integrated Learning System (ILS)**: An advanced computer-based instructional system consisting of computerized courseware covering several grade levels, complex classroom management and reporting features, and supplementary materials for student use.
- **Positive Interdependence**: The perception that group members are linked so that one cannot succeed unless others do; includes goal, reward, resource, role, identity, and environmental interdependence.
- **Individual Accountability**: The requirement that each member of a cooperative group must master the information for which the group is responsible.
- **Collaborative Skills**: Skills needed to work effectively with others, including communicating ideas, building trust, providing leadership, and managing group conflicts.
- **Free Rider Effect**: When less able group members allow others to complete the majority of group activities.
- **Sucker Effect**: When more able group members expend less effort to avoid doing all the work for the group.

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on Deutsch's (1962) definition of cooperative learning as situations where students can achieve instructional goals only if other group members achieve theirs as well. This contrasts with individualistic learning where achievement is independent of others' work. The theoretical foundation relies heavily on Johnson and Johnson's (1991) framework of cooperative learning components, which identifies positive interdependence, individual accountability, and collaborative skills as essential elements for effective cooperative learning.

## Methods
This is a conceptual/theoretical paper rather than an empirical study. The author synthesizes existing research on ILSs and cooperative learning to develop design guidelines. The paper reviews meta-analyses by Becker (1992, 1994) on ILS effectiveness, Slavin's (1983, 1987, 1995) analyses of over 100 cooperative learning studies, and specific studies examining cooperative learning with computer-based instruction (e.g., Mevarech 1994, Brush 1997).

## Main Arguments
- Traditional ILS design promotes individualized instruction that can lead to negative affective outcomes, student isolation, and lack of teacher involvement
- Long-term individualized ILS use leads to student anxiety, feelings of inadequacy, and decreased teacher interaction
- ILSs benefit low and high achievers but provide less support for middle-achieving students who are "less likely to need a different level or pace of instruction"
- Cooperative learning has been shown to improve academic achievement (75% of 100+ studies), increase motivation, improve attendance, and enhance self-esteem
- Simply placing students in groups does not constitute cooperative learning; structured positive interdependence and individual accountability are required
- ILS courseware and management systems can be redesigned to embed cooperative learning components through role assignments, group tracking/reporting, embedded quizzes, collaborative tutorials, and prompts reinforcing collaborative skills
- A redesigned "cooperative ILS" would include: group role assignments, collaborative training activities prior to instruction, modified courseware with interdependence cues, individual assessment points, and management system options for group-based reporting and rewards

## Limitations & Critiques
- The paper is theoretical rather than empirical; the proposed ILS redesign had not been implemented or tested at the time of publication
- Research on cooperative learning with ILSs specifically (rather than general computer-based instruction) was limited
- Studies examining cooperative learning with computers showed mixed results on whether positive effects transfer across different subject areas and student populations
- Tateyama-Sniezek (1990) reviewed 12 studies and found only 6 reported significant results favoring cooperative learning with computers, concluding it is "difficult for teachers to consistently integrate methods to ensure positive interdependence and individual accountability within the cooperative learning activities"
- Optimal group size and composition (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous) for computer-based cooperative activities remained debated
- The practical challenges of implementing cooperative learning in computer lab settings (limited space, need for dispersal during individual assessments) are acknowledged but not fully resolved
