## Summary
Holt examines the nature of judgment in engineering design, drawing on Geoffrey Vickers's concept of the "appreciative system" to argue that design judgment operates across three knowledge domains: formative, commercial, and instrumental. He addresses what judgment is, how its quality can be assessed, and whether it can be taught—concluding with a strategy for engineering education that develops judgment capacity alongside technical knowledge through situated practice and mentorship.

## Key Concepts
- Design judgment
- Engineering design
- Appreciative system (Vickers)
- Tacit knowledge
- Design education

## Theoretical Framework
Vickers's theory of the appreciative system and professional expertise theory inform the analysis of how designers develop judgment through experience, reflection, and mentorship.

## Methods
Theoretical analysis; application of Vickers's philosophical framework to engineering design practice and education.

## Main Arguments
- Judgment is ubiquitous in design work but resists precise definition; it involves tacit knowledge built through experience
- Design judgment operates in three domains: formative (what should be), commercial (what is viable), and instrumental (how to achieve it)
- Good judgment involves appropriate weighting of these domains given context and constraints
- Teaching design judgment requires creating opportunities for judgment practice, failure, and mentored reflection
