# Fostering Epistemic Insights into AI Ethics through a Constructionist Pedagogy

## Summary
This study presents three constructionist activities designed to promote ethical understanding and epistemic insights into AI among primary school students (ages 10-12). Grounded in constructionism, students built and interacted with rule-based AI, machine learning-based AI, and generative AI to understand how these systems acquire and process knowledge differently from humans. Through hands-on experimentation with guided reflection, students examined ethical principles such as fairness, bias, transparency, accountability, and non-maleficence while developing interdisciplinary understanding spanning computer science, mathematics, and humanities.

## Key Concepts
- Epistemic insight - understanding how knowledge is created, validated, and applied within AI systems, enabling critical engagement with ethical dimensions of AI
- AI literacy - competencies enabling individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies, communicate with AI, and use AI responsibly (UNESCO 2022)
- Constructionist pedagogy - learning approach engaging students in actively designing and building AI systems to make abstract ethical concepts tangible and accessible
- Epistemic complexity of AI - interdisciplinary challenges requiring students to understand how AI acquires, processes, and applies knowledge differently from human intelligence
- Rule-based AI transparency - clear, traceable decision-making processes based on explicit programmed rules visible to users
- ML-based AI opacity - probabilistic decision-making based on patterns learned from data, making predictions harder to trace than rule-based systems
- Generative AI complexity - systems creating new content through learned patterns, with even less transparent internal workings than supervised learning models
- Interdisciplinary AI ethics - integrating knowledge from computer science, mathematics, philosophy, and humanities to tackle real-world AI challenges
- Human values in AI development - recognition that AI systems reflect biases and values of their creators rather than being neutral or objective
- Critical evaluation of AI outputs - assessing whether AI-generated results align with design briefs, project goals, and ethical standards

## Theoretical Framework
- Constructionism (Harel & Papert 1991) - learners construct knowledge through designing and building tangible artifacts, making abstract concepts concrete
- Epistemic insight framework (Billingsley et al. 2018) - theoretical lens for addressing interdisciplinary AI ethics by helping students understand knowledge creation and validation in AI systems
- Dual-contrast pedagogy - comparing different AI approaches (rule-based vs ML-based vs generative) to highlight epistemological differences
- Cognitive orientation in AI capabilities (Markauskaite et al. 2022) - viewing AI as teammate affecting individual cognition, metacognition, and behavior
- Humanistic orientation in AI capabilities - centering on human values and capabilities to use and shape AI for collective well-being
- Social orientation in AI capabilities - focusing on shared meaning-making and collective practices in human-AI interaction

## Methods
- Eight-lesson AI curriculum conducted in August 2023 at Hong Kong public primary school
- 34 fifth-grade students (17 boys, 17 girls, average age 11) with no prior AI experience
- Activity 1 (1 hour): Rule-based AI using Scratch to create banana classifier with explicit if-else rules
- Activity 2 (1 hour): ML-based AI using Google Teachable Machine to train image classifier on 60 banana images
- Activity 3 (15 minutes): Generative AI using Poe language model for poetry creation
- Data collection: video recordings of classroom interactions, screen recordings, student worksheets, cognitive interviews
- Thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke 2006) to identify patterns in understanding of ethical principles and epistemic insight
- Triangulation with observational data to validate activity effectiveness

## Main Arguments
- Constructionist approach with guided reflection successfully fosters both technical understanding and ethical awareness in primary students, overcoming limitations of traditional case study methods that oversimplify complex issues
- Epistemic insight enables students to move beyond AI misconceptions (viewing AI as either threat or objective tool) toward nuanced understanding of how AI systems function and reflect human values
- Hands-on building experiences make visible the epistemological differences between AI types: rule-based AI offers transparency, ML-based AI introduces probabilistic uncertainty, generative AI adds creative complexity
- Students demonstrate interdisciplinary thinking when they connect mathematical reasoning (sample size impacts) to AI concepts (feature learning) to ethical implications (fairness and bias)
- Direct interaction with AI tools allows students to grasp fairness/bias issues concretely—they observe how imbalanced datasets cause misclassifications and superficial features lead to biased outcomes
- Students develop accountability awareness by recognizing their role as developers in defining features, setting rules, and curating data, understanding that AI mistakes stem from human decisions
- Transparency understanding differentiates by AI type: students appreciate rule-based AI's clarity, express frustration with ML opacity ("even when banana is perfectly yellow...machine could still recognize it as bad"), and recognize generative AI as least transparent
- Students exhibit critical attitude toward AI limitations, recognizing machines need extensive data while humans learn faster from fewer examples, and that AI can make "stupid" mistakes like confusing people with bananas
- Understanding that AI systems lack independent thought and simply follow human instructions helps students recognize that AI is "not entirely objective" but shaped by human values and biases
- Continuous ethical reflection throughout hands-on process—rather than separate ethics discussions—enables students to integrate technical learning with ethical considerations organically

## Limitations & Critiques
- Small sample size (34 students) from single Hong Kong school limits generalizability across diverse cultural, linguistic, and educational contexts
- Short intervention duration (8 lessons total, with only 2.25 hours on these three activities) may not allow observation of lasting impacts on ethical reasoning or AI literacy development
- Study excludes ethical considerations of AI use despite acknowledging their importance—authors defer this critical dimension to future research without examining student ethical decision-making
- Lack of control group makes it impossible to isolate constructionist pedagogy's specific contributions from general hands-on learning effects or teacher enthusiasm
- Self-reported data and cognitive interviews subject to social desirability bias—students may express enthusiasm knowing faculty designed and evaluate the curriculum
- Thematic analysis conducted by researchers who designed the curriculum introduces potential confirmation bias in identifying positive outcomes
- Activities focus on simplified AI scenarios (banana classification, poetry generation) that may not transfer to understanding complex real-world AI systems with multiple stakeholders
- Assessment of "AI literacy" remains undefined—no validated instrument to measure whether students actually developed competencies for responsible AI interaction beyond classroom context
- Missing longitudinal follow-up to determine whether epistemic insights persist over time or fade without reinforcement
- Study provides no comparison of effectiveness across different age groups—claims about "age-appropriate" design lack empirical validation beyond this single grade level
