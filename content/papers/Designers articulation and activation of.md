# Designers articulation and activation of

## Summary
ARTICLE HISTORY Cross-cultural design practices have begun to rise in prominence, but these practices have infrequently intersected with common user-centred design practices that value the participation and lived experience of users In this paper, we analyse a shared dataset that documented the efforts of a Scandinavian design team as they designed a co-creation workshop with Chinese consumers We identified how the design team referred to workshop participants,.

## Key Concepts
- **Instrumental design judgements**: Practical decisions designers make about participants, methods, and contexts when conducting cross-cultural user research
- **Cross-cultural user research**: Design research practices spanning different cultural contexts, requiring navigation of cultural differences and assumptions
- **Articulation of judgements**: How designers verbalize, explain, and justify their design decisions regarding research participants and methods
- **Activation of judgements**: How articulated understandings translate into actual research design and interaction choices
- **Cultural assumptions in UCD**: Implicit beliefs about culture embedded in user-centered design practices when applied across cultural boundaries
- **Co-creation workshop design**: Planning participatory design activities for users from different cultural backgrounds than design team
- **Scandinavian-Chinese design encounter**: Case of European design team conducting research with Chinese consumers, revealing cross-cultural design tensions

## Main Arguments
- **Cultural assumptions implicit in references**: How design team talked about Chinese participants revealed underlying cultural stereotypes, generalizations, or essentialist thinking even while pursuing user-centered approach
- **Instrumental judgements reflect designer positioning**: Practical decisions about workshop design were shaped by team's Scandinavian cultural background and limited understanding of Chinese contexts
- **Tension between UCD ideals and cultural distance**: User-centered design principles emphasizing participation and lived experience difficult to enact when cultural gaps create interpretation challenges
- **Articulation-activation gap**: How designers described their intentions for respecting participants sometimes diverged from actual design choices embedded in workshop structure
- **Cross-cultural competence requires reflexivity**: Effective cross-cultural design research demands awareness of own cultural positioning and how it shapes judgements about participants
- **UCD methods not culturally neutral**: Standard participatory design approaches carry cultural assumptions that may not transfer seamlessly across contexts

## Limitations & Critiques
- **Single case limitation**: One Scandinavian-Chinese design encounter may not represent full range of cross-cultural design challenges or dynamics
- **Designer perspective emphasis**: Focus on design team's articulations may miss participants' actual experiences or how cultural gaps manifested in workshop execution
- **Outcome measures absent**: Analysis of design process judgements but limited assessment of whether workshop succeeded in generating useful insights or building relationships
- **Cultural essentialism risk**: Framing as "Scandinavian" versus "Chinese" may inadvertently reinforce binary thinking rather than attending to within-culture diversity
- **Intervention potential unexplored**: Identifies problematic judgements but doesn't examine how designers might develop more culturally reflexive practices
- **Power dynamics underexplored**: Limited analysis of how economic, organizational, or geopolitical power relationships shaped cross-cultural research encounter
- **Missing participant voices**: Focus on design team references without parallel analysis of how Chinese participants understood or resisted team's cultural assumptions
- **Generalizability questions**: Findings specific to particular design team, national cultures, and project context; transferability to other cross-cultural scenarios unclear
