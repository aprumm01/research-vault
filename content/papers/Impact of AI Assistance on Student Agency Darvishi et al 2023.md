# Impact of AI Assistance on Student Agency

## Summary
This large-scale randomised controlled experiment (n=1,625 students across 10 courses) investigates whether students learn from AI assistance or merely rely on it, and whether self-regulation strategies can substitute or complement AI prompts in peer review tasks. Results show that students tend to rely on rather than learn from AI assistance — when AI is removed, performance drops — but that hybrid human-AI approaches combining AI with self-monitoring checklists are no more effective than AI alone. The study raises important questions about the trade-off between AI scaffolding and the development of enduring self-regulation skills.

## Key Concepts
- **Student Agency**: The capacity for students to actively shape their learning experiences, make responsible decisions, and control their educational journey; operationalised here as the ability to maintain peer feedback quality in the absence of AI prompts
- **AI-Assisted Peer Review**: The experimental context in which AI detects insufficiently detailed or generic peer feedback and prompts students to revise it; used to test whether this scaffold produces lasting behavioural change
- **Reliance vs. Learning from AI**: The central conceptual distinction — students who learn from AI improve independently; those who rely on it maintain performance only with AI present
- **Self-Regulation Strategies (SRL)**: Specifically, self-monitoring checklists implemented as an alternative to AI prompts; tested as a potential pathway to internalising feedback quality standards
- **Hybrid Human-AI Approach (SAI)**: The condition combining AI prompts with self-monitoring checklists; expected to be superior but found to be no more effective than AI alone
- **Personalised Nudging**: AI-generated prompts that alert students when their feedback is too short, too generic, or similar to prior comments, triggering revision before submission
- **Winne's SRL Model**: The theoretical framework for agency — defined as the capacity to exercise choice and self-regulation, particularly in making informed decisions about learning

## Theoretical Framework
The study grounds its analysis in Winne's (2006, 2013) model of self-regulated learning, where agency is defined as the ontological capacity to make choices and self-regulate action. This is contrasted with externally scaffolded AI assistance, which structures behaviour without necessarily developing the internal regulatory capacity needed to sustain that behaviour when the scaffold is removed.

The paper also draws on educational technology literature on AI-powered personalised nudging and adaptive feedback systems, positioning these tools within a broader tension between efficiency (AI delivers immediate, personalised support) and developmental goals (students internalise the standards and capabilities scaffolded by the AI). The concept of automation dependency is invoked: technologies designed to support independent performance may paradoxically create dependency by substituting for the very cognitive processes they were meant to support.

## Methods
Randomised controlled experiment conducted over eight weeks with 1,625 students across 10 courses at the University of Queensland. All students used AI-assisted peer review for four weeks (establishing a baseline). In weeks 5–8, students were randomly assigned to four conditions: (AI) continued AI prompts (control), (NR) no prompts, (SR) self-monitoring checklists only, and (SAI) both AI prompts and checklists. Peer feedback quality was measured via automated scoring of comment length, specificity, and semantic similarity to prior comments. Statistical comparison across conditions tested the impact of removing AI, substituting self-regulation, and combining both.

## Main Arguments
- Students in the no-prompt condition (NR) show significantly reduced peer feedback quality when AI is removed, indicating that performance during the AI phase was maintained by reliance rather than learning from the assistance
- Self-regulation strategies (SR group) can partially compensate for the loss of AI prompts but are not as effective as continued AI assistance, suggesting internalisation is incomplete even after four weeks of exposure
- The hybrid approach (SAI — combining AI prompts with self-monitoring checklists) does not outperform AI alone, challenging the assumption that complementing AI with metacognitive tools produces additive benefits
- The results suggest that AI assistance may inhibit the development of self-regulation by reducing the need for students to exercise independent metacognitive judgment — a form of agency-displacement
- The study highlights a fundamental tension in AI-EdTech design: tools optimised for performance scaffolding may undermine the long-term development of agency and self-regulation that sustainable learning requires
- In a world where AI is ubiquitous, the paper argues for redesigning AI tools to gradually fade support rather than maintain constant scaffolding — enabling a transition from AI reliance to self-regulation

## Limitations & Critiques
The study focuses narrowly on peer review feedback quality as the operationalisation of agency, which may not capture the full scope of student agency as a construct. The eight-week window may be insufficient to observe genuine skill transfer; longer longitudinal designs are needed. The automated scoring of peer feedback (length, specificity) is a proxy for quality and may not reflect the deeper educational value of feedback content. The results are specific to the peer review context and may not generalise to other AI-supported learning activities. The study does not examine individual differences (e.g., prior SRL capacity, motivation) that may moderate the reliance vs. learning distinction.
