# Beyond Automation: How UI/UX Designers Perceive AI as a Creative Partner in the Divergent Thinking Stages

## Summary
This CHI 2025 paper investigates how professional UI/UX designers use and perceive AI during divergent thinking phases of the design process — specifically research and ideation. Through interviews with 19 professional designers, the study identifies four key roles AI plays in supporting divergent thinking: aiding research, kick-starting creativity, generating design alternatives, and facilitating prototype exploration. The paper reveals that designers value AI tools that enhance efficiency and control without replacing their creative judgment.

## Key Concepts
- **Divergent thinking in UI/UX design**: The creative exploration phase of the design process (corresponding to the Discover and Develop phases of the Double Diamond model) where designers generate many possibilities before converging on solutions
- **Double Diamond model**: The UK Design Council's design process framework with four phases — Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver — used to structure the analysis of where AI fits in divergent vs. convergent phases
- **AI as kick-starter for creativity**: The role of AI in breaking initial creative blocks by generating diverse starting points that designers then react to and refine, lowering the barrier to beginning ideation
- **Control and agency in AI collaboration**: Designers' strong preference for AI tools that augment rather than direct their creative process — maintaining designer authority over conceptual choices while offloading routine execution
- **Low-fidelity vs. high-fidelity blurring**: The observed tendency for AI tools to enable rapid prototyping that blurs the traditional distinction between early low-fi sketches and more refined high-fi mockups
- **Visual interaction preferences**: Designers' desire for AI tools that support visual rather than purely text-based interaction, matching the visual-spatial nature of UI/UX work

## Theoretical Framework
The paper is grounded in divergent thinking theory and its application to design cognition. It draws on Goldschmidt's linkographic analysis showing that divergent and convergent thinking are more concurrent than sequential (challenging the linear Double Diamond model), and on Xie's work showing that expert designers use more structured cognitive strategies than novices during ideation.

The study also engages with AI-assisted creativity research, building on prior work (Zhou et al., Chandrasekera et al.) that shows AI can enhance both the depth and breadth of design solutions at specific integration points. The paper's contribution is to investigate this specifically for the divergent thinking phases, which have received less systematic attention than later design stages in AI-assisted design research.

## Methods
In-depth semi-structured interviews with 19 experienced professional UI/UX designers recruited through purposive sampling to ensure diversity in background, industry, and expertise. Interviews probed participants' current divergent thinking practices, their use of AI tools during these phases, and their perceived values and desires regarding AI in divergent design work. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis. No specific platforms, tools, or tasks were assigned — participants described their natural workflows, making this an ecological study of real practice.

## Main Arguments
- **Designers identify four distinct AI roles during divergent thinking**: (1) Aiding research — AI tools streamline data collection and synthesis from competitive analyses and user research; (2) Kick-starting creativity — AI provides initial ideas to react against, reducing blank-page paralysis; (3) Generating design alternatives — AI produces visual variations that expand the solution space; (4) Facilitating prototype exploration — AI enables rapid fidelity-jumping to gather feedback earlier in the process.
- **Designers view AI as an efficiency tool that liberates creativity rather than replacing it**: Participants consistently positioned AI as handling time-consuming, lower-level tasks (collecting competitive benchmarks, generating initial layouts) so that designers can focus on higher-order creative and strategic decisions.
- **Control over ideation is the most valued AI capability**: Designers want AI tools that give them more control over the generation process — adjustable parameters, style guidance, and iterative refinement — rather than black-box generation they cannot steer.
- **Copyright and ownership concerns are less salient for UI/UX designers than expected**: Despite public discourse about AI and intellectual property, participants rarely raised copyright concerns, possibly because UI design is perceived as more derivative and compositional than fine art or writing.
- **AI facilitates collaboration but current tools lack collaboration-specific features**: Designers expressed desire for AI tools that could facilitate real-time collaborative ideation with other team members, not just solo generation — a gap in current tool offerings.
- **The divergent-convergent distinction becomes more fluid with AI**: AI tools that can rapidly generate many alternatives at varying fidelities challenge the traditional sequencing of design phases, potentially enabling more iterative, non-linear design workflows.

## Limitations & Critiques
The 19-participant sample, while theoretically saturating for qualitative research, represents a specific population of experienced professional UI/UX designers who may not reflect the perspectives of less experienced practitioners or those in less design-mature organizations. Self-reported practices in interviews may not accurately reflect actual AI tool use behavior, as designers may idealize their workflows or selectively remember salient AI interactions.

The study focuses on divergent thinking phases and does not examine how AI use during ideation affects convergent phases (definition, delivery) or final design quality. The finding about low copyright concern may be specific to this sample and context, and may not persist as designers' work becomes more directly dependent on AI-generated outputs. The paper's four AI role taxonomy is descriptive and derived from a single dataset — further validation across different design contexts would strengthen its generalizability.
