# Why Are There Still So Many Jobs - Jo Ann Oravec (2018)

## Summary
Journal of Economic Perspectives—Volume 29, Number 3—Summer 2015—Pages 3–30 Why Are There Still So Many Jobs The History and Future of Workplace Automation† David H Autor T here have been periodic warnings in the last two centuries that automation and new technology were going to wipe out large numbers of middle class jobs.

## Key Concepts
- **Automation anxiety**: Recurring warnings over two centuries that automation would eliminate middle-class jobs (from Luddites to 1960s "automation jobless" to current AI concerns)
- **Labor market polarization**: Post-1980 shift where wage gains went disproportionately to top and bottom of skill distribution, not middle
- **Complementarity vs substitution**: Automation both substitutes for labor and complements it, raising output and demand for labor
- **O-ring production function**: Model where improvements in any production step increase value of all other steps, explaining complementarities
- **Task-based framework**: Focus on specific tasks rather than entire occupations being automated
- **Three mitigating factors**: (1) Which tasks workers supply (complemented vs substituted), (2) Elasticity of labor supply, (3) Output and income elasticity of demand
- **Comparative advantage**: Machines substitute for routine/codifiable tasks while amplifying human advantage in problem-solving, adaptability, creativity

## Theoretical Framework
Builds on human capital theory, labor economics, and task-based models of technology adoption. Draws on Atkinson-Shiffrin model of working/long-term memory. References Baumol's concept of "technologically lagging" sectors. Integrates insights from occupational change research and expert-novice differences in cognition.

## Methods
- Historical analysis of occupational change (1900-2010)
- Quantitative analysis of employment shares across occupational categories
- Case studies (e.g., ATMs and bank tellers)
- Review of empirical literature on automation effects
- Analysis of two distinct epochs: 1940-1980 vs 1980-2010
- Data from Census IPUMS and American Community Survey

## Main Arguments
- **Employment has not declined despite automation**: Employment-to-population ratio rose during 20th century; no apparent long-run unemployment increase
- **Past cannot settle future debates**: Historical patterns don't guarantee current outcomes, especially with emergence of powerful AI, computing, and robotics
- **Automation creates complementarities**: ATM case shows machines reduced costs per branch, leading to more branches and demand for relationship banking skills
- **Occupational change shifted after 1980**: Pre-1980 changes favored skilled white-collar work and reduced physically demanding jobs; post-1980 brought polarization with growth only at top (professional/managerial) and bottom (services), while middle hollowed out
- **Three factors determine impacts**: (1) Whether workers supply complemented or substituted tasks, (2) Labor supply elasticity can temper wage gains, (3) Demand elasticity shapes sectoral effects
- **Consumption demands not satiating**: Rising productivity hasn't led to demand shortfall; consumption and leisure are complements
- **Machine-human comparative advantage**: Computers substitute for routine/codifiable work while amplifying value of problem-solving, judgment, flexibility, common sense
- **Polarization unlikely to continue far**: Current trajectory unsustainable into foreseeable future

## Limitations & Critiques
- Heavy focus on US labor market may limit generalizability
- Historical analysis may not fully capture unprecedented nature of current AI capabilities
- Task-based framework simplifies complexity of how work is actually organized
- Optimistic assumption that complementarities will continue to create demand
- Limited discussion of distributional consequences and policy responses
- May underestimate speed and scope of current automation wave
- Does not fully address Marxian concerns about capital concentration
