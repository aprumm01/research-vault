# The Dynamics of the Self-Regulation Process in Student-AI Interactions: The Case of Problem-Solving in Programming Education

## Summary
This case study analyses 2,376 ChatGPT interactions from 120 undergraduate students in a web programming course, using a theoretically grounded SRL coding scheme combined with Transition Network Analysis (TNA) and Sequence Analysis. The study reveals that students predominantly engage in surface-level regulatory prompting (process monitoring, basic problem-solving) while rarely using deeper metacognitive strategies (reflection, evaluation). This pattern points to a systematic tendency toward cognitive offloading that could undermine the development of independent computational thinking skills.

## Key Concepts
- **Transition Network Analysis (TNA)**: A novel process-oriented learning analytics method that goes beyond descriptive frequency counts to validate significant transitions between interaction states, distinguishing consistent patterns from random variation
- **SRL in Problem-Solving Coding Scheme**: A theoretically grounded taxonomy (from Prasad and Sane's framework) applied to code student-AI interactions into categories such as process monitoring, problem decomposition, debugging, evaluation, and reflection
- **Surface-Level Regulation**: The dominant pattern found — students use AI primarily for process monitoring (checking if code runs) and basic problem-solving, without engaging in deeper metacognitive processes like reflecting on their approach or evaluating alternative strategies
- **Cognitive Offloading in Programming**: The tendency to delegate problem decomposition, debugging, and solution generation to AI without critical engagement, bypassing the core computational thinking skills that programming education aims to develop
- **Web Programming as Authentic Context**: The choice of web programming reflects real-world development practices (iterative debugging, library consultation, functional artefact production) rather than decontextualised algorithmic tasks
- **Co-adaptive Process**: Reconceptualising human-AI interaction not as tool use but as a co-adaptive process where both parties influence each other's subsequent behaviour — emphasising the importance of studying interaction dynamics rather than just inputs and outputs
- **Sequence Analysis**: Temporal analysis method used to examine how regulation behaviours unfold throughout complete student-AI conversations, capturing longitudinal patterns within and across interactions

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) theory, specifically Zimmerman's iterative cycle of planning, monitoring, and evaluation, and Prasad and Sane's adaptation for AI-assisted problem-solving in computing education. This provides the taxonomy for coding interactions beyond surface-level prompt analysis.

The paper also invokes Cabitza et al.'s framing of AI as a "knowledge artifact" — a mediating structure supporting collective decision-making — and Ziegler and Donkers' dialogic perspective, where human-AI interaction is a co-constructed, ongoing process rather than a series of discrete transactions. This shift from product to process orientation is the paper's central methodological contribution: it attends not just to what AI helps produce but to how students engage with, reflect on, and adapt to AI over time.

## Methods
Case study with mixed methods. 2,376 interactions from 120 undergraduate students using ChatGPT for an open-ended web programming assignment were collected. Interactions were qualitatively coded using the SRL-in-problem-solving taxonomy, then analysed using (1) Sequence Analysis to examine temporal unfolding of interaction patterns, and (2) Transition Network Analysis (TNA) to map statistically validated transitions between regulatory states. TNA distinguishes significant from random transitions, enabling identification of stable interaction structures. Analysis compared patterns across students who successfully completed the assignment versus those who struggled.

## Main Arguments
- Students predominantly use AI for surface-level regulation (process monitoring, executing instructions) rather than deeper metacognitive engagement (reflection, evaluation, strategic planning), confirming a tendency toward cognitive offloading in authentic programming contexts
- Deep metacognitive strategies such as reflection on approach and evaluation of alternatives are rarely observed in the interaction logs, despite being theoretically central to effective problem-solving and the development of programming expertise
- TNA reveals that interactions are not random but follow structured patterns, with certain regulatory states strongly predicting subsequent states — indicating that students develop habitual engagement patterns with AI early in the task
- The surface-level regulation pattern poses a long-term risk to the development of independent, lifelong computational thinking: students who consistently outsource problem decomposition and debugging to AI may not develop the skills to tackle novel or advanced tasks without AI assistance
- Web programming is identified as a particularly revealing context because it demands the integration of multiple skills (debugging, library use, functional output) and mirrors real-world developer practices, making it a more ecologically valid test of AI-assisted learning than introductory algorithmic tasks
- The study calls for pedagogical interventions that make self-regulatory processes explicit within AI-assisted programming tasks — for example, requiring students to articulate their reasoning before querying AI, or reflect on AI responses before implementing them

## Limitations & Critiques
The study is a single case study in one course at one institution, limiting generalisability. The coding scheme, while theoretically grounded, requires interpretive judgment that may introduce inter-rater variability (though inter-rater reliability was assessed). The study observes what students do with AI but cannot directly assess what they learn as a result — connecting interaction patterns to learning outcomes remains an important open question. The web programming context, while authentic, may produce different patterns than introductory or advanced programming courses. The study also does not account for individual differences in prior programming experience, which may significantly moderate the relationship between AI use patterns and learning outcomes.
