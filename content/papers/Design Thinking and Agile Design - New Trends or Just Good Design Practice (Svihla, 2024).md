# Design Thinking and Agile Design

## Summary
This chapter examines design thinking and agile methods as alternatives or complements to traditional instructional design approaches like ADDIE. Unpacks design thinking history from design research field, tracing emergence across disciplines. Design thinking characterized as abductive reasoning—filling knowledge gaps through precedent and problem framing—contrasting with scientific inductive/deductive thinking. Argues design problems are tentative conjectures jointly framed with solutions, not predetermined. Agile design with rapid prototyping addresses iterative problem reframing needs. Human-centered and participatory design engage stakeholders early but require support for visionary thinking beyond incremental changes. Considers lessons for instructional designers, challenges linear "waterfall" models despite documented expert practice being messier and iterative.

## Key Concepts
- **Design thinking**: Methods, practices, processes involving abductive reasoning, creativity, and innovation; treating problems as tentative conjectures jointly framed with solutions
- **Abductive reasoning**: Filling knowledge gaps using precedent (past work, preferences, experiences) and problem understanding, contrasting with scientific induction/deduction
- **Agile design**: Rapid prototyping, testing, and iteration embracing problem reframing based on insights from low-fidelity prototypes
- **Waterfall model**: Linear, sequential design process originally proposed as flawed (Royce 1970) yet still common in practice despite known limitations
- **ADDIE**: Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation—ubiquitous instructional design model sometimes misapplied as rigid sequence
- **SAM (Successive Approximation Model)**: Agile method for instructional design emphasizing iterative cycles
- **Human-centered design**: Engaging stakeholders in varied ways including early problem framing to incorporate user perspectives
- **Precedent in design**: Past design work, preferences, and experiences shaping current design decisions—can be generative or constraining
- **Problem-solution co-evolution**: Design problems aren't fully understood until solved, requiring embrace of iterative problem changes

## Theoretical Framework
Roots in design research field studying how designers work, differentiating design knowledge from scientific knowledge (Cross). Builds on abductive reasoning theory (Dorst, Kolko) as suppositional if-then/what-if scenario exploration. Draws from Manifesto for Agile Software Development (Beck et al.) emphasizing iteration in "living human world." References Schön's reflection-in-action and Chai et al.'s design thinking definition (improving ideas into artifacts vs. belief mode). Connects to participatory design traditions (Muller & Druin, Schuler & Namioka) and human-centered design (Rouse). Critiques naive idealizations in traditional instructional design models (Tripp & Bichelmeyer). Emphasizes problem-solution co-evolution where problem isn't truly known until solved (Cross et al., Kimbell).

## Methods
Historical analysis tracing design thinking definitions over time and across fields (Figure 2 timeline documenting conceptual evolution). Literature review of prominent instructional design journals (TechTrends, ETR&D, BJET, JAID) examining 35 papers citing design thinking—categorized into four uses: IDEO approach as aspiration, investigating designer approaches, teachers as designers, design thinking as student learning outcome. Comparative analysis contrasting design thinking/agile methods with traditional waterfall/ADDIE models. Case examples (Jeff and Paloma working with Mr. Yazzie) illustrating lessons for instructional designers. Pedagogical approach with embedded "LIDT in the World" lessons and learning checks for textbook integration.

## Main Arguments
- **Linear waterfall contradicts expert practice**: Most instructional design representations depict linear process contradicting documented expert practice which is actually iterative and precedent-based
- **Problems are tentative conjectures**: Design thinking treats problems as jointly framed with solutions through iterative checking whether solutions satisfy requirements, not predetermined givens
- **Precedent shapes design for better or worse**: Past experiences (including uninspired/ineffective instruction) influence current design; must critically examine how precedent constrains vision
- **Agile methods manage reframing**: Rapid prototyping enables efficient problem reframing through early stakeholder testing before heavy investment
- **Low-fidelity enables reframing but risks perception**: Early rough prototypes allow reconceptualization before high commitment but risk appearing unprofessional versus finalized
- **Stakeholder engagement increases ownership but needs vision support**: Involving users early builds buy-in and accesses perspectives but they may lack imagination for transformative change (smartphone keyboard example showing limits of incremental thinking)
- **Design thinking recently emerging in instructional design**: About half of citations reference IDEO popularization versus design research origins, showing field's recency to practice
- **Problem-solution co-evolution essential**: Can't truly know design problem until solved, requiring embrace of multiple problem changes during iteration rather than fixed specification
- **Instructional designs are short-lived**: Products subject to iteration adapting to emergent changes; agile embrace of flux more realistic than waterfall stability assumption
- **Need to support visionary stakeholders**: Must scaffold participants to think beyond incremental improvements while grounding in learning research and evidence

## Limitations & Critiques
- **No unified definition**: No single agreed-upon definition of design thinking; no agreement on outcomes of design thinking adeptness (Rodgers), creating conceptual muddiness
- **Blurred boundaries**: Differences between participatory, human-centered, and agile approaches blur as they enter practice, losing theoretical distinctions
- **Limited guidance on approach selection**: Doesn't specify when waterfall/ADDIE appropriate versus agile/design thinking; practitioners need decision frameworks
- **Resource constraints underaddressed**: Making rapid iteration infeasible in time-limited, budget-constrained contexts not fully explored with practical alternatives
- **Stakeholder limitation solutions unclear**: Smartphone example demonstrates user incrementalism but doesn't explore systematic strategies for overcoming this barrier
- **Precedent can perpetuate problems**: Precedent-based reasoning may replicate flawed patterns if precedents themselves are biased, ineffective, or exclusionary
- **Low-fidelity challenges underdev eloped**: Perception issues (appearing unprofessional, focus on superficial feedback) acknowledged but solutions remain underspecified
- **Scholarship agenda unstated**: Chapter raises need for research, teaching, practice agenda but doesn't detail what that agenda should entail
- **Limited comparative empirical evidence**: Lacks systematic studies comparing design thinking/agile outcomes to traditional approaches in instructional design contexts
- **Cultural and contextual variation**: Approaches developed in specific cultural/organizational contexts; transferability across settings unclear
