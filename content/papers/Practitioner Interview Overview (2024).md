# Practitioner Interview Overview: UX Designers on AI, Value, and Professional Practice

## Summary
This document presents a comparative analysis of two practitioner interviews with UX designers (Gyuree and Ken) exploring their perspectives on UX practice, AI integration, collaboration, and professional identity. The interviews were conducted as part of a research study on AI and design education.

Gyuree is a UX design specialist at Concur working on meetings/events and admin platforms, while Ken is a senior UX designer with consulting and in-house experience in banking, CRM, and large mobile applications. Both designers emphasize making complex systems easier and smoother for users, connecting UX value to business outcomes rather than just aesthetics.

Key similarities include: both view UX as making systems easier and clearer for users; both rely on collaboration with PMs, developers, and stakeholders; both use validation through feedback and testing; both acknowledge mistakes and adjust; and both prefer conversation and understanding over conflict when handling difficult situations.

Key differences include: Gyuree focuses more on AI's impact on UX (personal and professional), organizational perception of UX, and is more emotional and reflective about the future of UX roles. Ken focuses more on classic UX process, requirements gathering, and big-system delivery, and is more pragmatic and operational with minimal AI emphasis.

## Key Concepts
- **UX Value**: Both practitioners see UX as about making systems easier for users while delivering business value, not just aesthetics
- **Collaboration Style**: Both emphasize working closely with PMs, engineers, and stakeholders through validation and feedback loops
- **AI in UX Practice**: Gyuree provides rich narrative about AI's impact including using ChatGPT for content design, synthesis, and parenting activities; Ken barely mentions AI
- **Organizational Perception**: Gyuree discusses evolving company attitudes toward UX, while Ken focuses on how he embeds UX practically in teams

## Theoretical Framework
The analysis uses a comparative case study approach examining practitioner perspectives across multiple dimensions: UX value perception, collaboration styles, uncertainty tolerance, conflict handling, AI relationships, organizational positioning, requirements processes, and motivations.

## Methods
Semi-structured interviews with two UX practitioners. Gyuree's interview was framed as a research study on AI and design education. Ken's interview was structured as a portfolio/process interview focused on requirements gathering, project structuring, and design system implementation.

## Main Arguments
- UX practitioners share fundamental views about making systems easier and connecting design to business reality
- AI adoption varies significantly among practitioners; some integrate it deeply while others maintain traditional methods
- Both practitioners balance experimentation and learning rather than rigid certainty
- Organizational perception of UX continues to evolve; engineers increasingly ask for user research even if they don't fully believe it should precede building
- Human interviews and deep user understanding are viewed as not easily replaceable by AI
- Future UX roles may shift toward more research focus while directing AI to generate mockups

## Limitations & Critiques
- Only two practitioners interviewed, limiting generalizability
- Different interview framings (research study vs. portfolio interview) may affect response comparability
- Ken's interview section did not include AI-focused questions, making direct comparison on AI attitudes incomplete
- Single point-in-time interviews do not capture evolving practitioner perspectives
- Both practitioners work in enterprise/business contexts; perspectives from other domains may differ
