# Mastering Knowledge: The Impact of Generative AI on Student Learning Outcomes

## Summary
This study investigates how different approaches to using generative AI relate to varying student learning outcomes in higher education, applying the Achievement Goals Framework to analyze 192 student reflections using quasi-experimental design and quantitative content analysis (QCA). The central finding is that higher-order learning occurs when students use GenAI to construct and augment knowledge (mastery approach), while lower-order outcomes result from procedural, unreflective use. The paper offers practical recommendations for curriculum and assessment design that promote mastery goal structures.

## Key Concepts
- **Mastery approach**: Using GenAI to actively construct and augment knowledge, engaging critically with AI outputs rather than reproducing them—associated with higher-level learning outcomes
- **Procedural approach**: Using GenAI in a rote, procedural manner without critical engagement or knowledge augmentation—associated with lower-level learning outcomes
- **Achievement Goals Framework**: Theoretical distinction between mastery goal structures (developing competence, pursuing understanding) and performance goal structures (demonstrating competence, outperforming others)
- **Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)**: Vygotsky's concept applied to understand how GenAI may scaffold students from basic knowledge construction to complex augmentation
- **Quantitative Content Analysis (QCA)**: Systematic coding methodology applied to qualitative student reflections to identify patterns across a large dataset
- **Curriculum scaffolding**: Designing course structures that guide students from basic GenAI-assisted tasks toward increasingly complex knowledge synthesis
- **Assessment design for GenAI contexts**: Structuring assessments to reward mastery engagement with AI rather than simple reproduction of AI-generated content

## Theoretical Framework
The paper applies Elliot's (2005) and Nicholls' (1984) Achievement Goals Framework to understand how students' goal orientations shape their engagement with GenAI. Mastery goal structures prioritize developing competence and deep understanding, while performance goal structures focus on outperforming others and demonstrating existing competence. The authors argue that this framework helps explain why different students extract different learning value from the same AI tools—students with mastery orientations are more likely to critically engage with AI outputs and use them as scaffolds for higher-order thinking.

Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development is invoked to conceptualize how GenAI may function as a "more knowledgeable other," expanding students' capacity to engage with complex material. However, the authors caution that this scaffolding function is only realized when students actively engage with and augment AI-generated content rather than treating it as a finished product. The study also engages with paradoxes of GenAI in education, including the tension between AI as a "friend" (filling knowledge gaps) and "foe" (enabling avoidance of genuine learning).

## Methods
Quasi-experimental study using quantitative content analysis (QCA) applied to 192 student reflections collected from higher education courses. The reflections documented students' experiences using GenAI for learning tasks. Coding categories were developed based on the Achievement Goals Framework to distinguish mastery versus procedural engagement patterns. The quasi-experimental lens allowed comparison of learning outcomes associated with different AI usage approaches without random assignment. Published in Studies in Higher Education (2026), conducted at RMIT University, Australian Catholic University, and Swinburne University in Australia.

## Main Arguments
- Students who use GenAI to construct and augment knowledge—asking AI to explain, extend, and help them understand—achieve higher-level learning outcomes than those who use AI procedurally to complete tasks without critical engagement
- The Achievement Goals Framework explains differential learning outcomes from identical AI tools: mastery-oriented students leverage AI as a scaffold while performance-oriented students may use it to shortcut the learning process
- Curriculum design can proactively shape how students engage with AI by designing tasks that scaffold progression from basic knowledge construction to complex knowledge augmentation
- Assessment design should promote mastery goal structures by requiring students to critically engage with, evaluate, and extend AI outputs rather than simply reproduce them
- The institutional framing of GenAI as separate from students' intellectual contributions creates a false dichotomy; effective integration requires viewing GenAI as a collaborative tool whose value depends on how it is engaged
- Higher-level learning outcomes in GenAI contexts require developing students' capacity to evaluate AI-generated content, identify gaps and inaccuracies, and synthesize AI outputs with their own prior knowledge

## Limitations & Critiques
The quasi-experimental design limits causal inference about the relationship between goal structures and learning outcomes; the associations identified may reflect pre-existing differences between students rather than AI-induced changes in learning. The sample of 192 student reflections from Australian universities may not generalize to other educational contexts, and the QCA methodology depends on coding decisions that introduce interpretive variability.

The study does not directly measure learning outcomes through assessments but relies on student self-reflection, which may be subject to social desirability bias. The framework may also underrepresent the structural and contextual factors (course design, instructor support, assessment pressures) that influence whether students can adopt mastery approaches to AI use.
