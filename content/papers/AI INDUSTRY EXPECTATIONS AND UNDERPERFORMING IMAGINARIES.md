# AI INDUSTRY EXPECTATIONS AND UNDERPERFORMING IMAGINARIES

## Summary
Selected Papers of #AoIR2024: The 25th Annual Conference of the Association of Internet Researchers Sheffield, UK / 30 Oct - 2 Nov 2024 AI INDUSTRY EXPECTATIONS AND UNDERPERFORMING IMAGINARIES Natalia Stanusch University of Amsterdam Richard Rogers University of Amsterdam Nancy Baym Microsoft Research Chuncheng Liu Microsoft Research Ryland Shaw Microsoft Research Christian Katzenbach University of Bremen Vanessa Richter.

## Key Concepts
- **Sociotechnical imaginaries**: Assemblages of materiality, meaning, and morality that constitute robust forms of social life propagated by organized groups (Jasanoff & Kim)
- **Public imaginaries**: Publicly constructed visions of (un)desirable socio-technical futures that guide action, mobilize resources, and layout trajectories for materialization or prevention
- **AI industry dominance**: The concentration of AI development and discourse within Big Tech companies, shaping future visions of technology
- **Counter-imaginaries**: Alternative visions of AI that engage with and intervene in dominant industry narratives
- **Issue preclusion**: The AI industry's strategy of internalizing critiques to prevent external regulation and governance
- **Premediation**: Externalizing future concerns to distant horizons (e.g., AGI, longtermism) to deflect present accountability
- **Stakeholder typology**: Classification of AI discourse participants including industry, government, academia, media, and civil society
- **AI sustainability**: Social-ecological-economic justice considerations in AI development, training, and deployment

## Theoretical Framework
- Sociotechnical imaginaries framework (Jasanoff & Kim, 2013, 2015)
- Public imaginaries framework (Brause et al., 2023)
- Actor-Network Theory (Latour, 2005)
- Issue mapping and controversy mapping methodologies

## Methods
Panel study with multiple methodological approaches:
- Expert surveys reaching AI researchers and public discourse participants in US and Germany
- Longitudinal Twitter analysis (2012-2021) mapping stakeholder engagement patterns
- Semi-structured interviews with 10-15 AI experts per country across industry, government, academia, media, and civil society
- Co-hashtag analysis and visual network analysis on LinkedIn and X/Twitter
- Situational analysis (Clarke, 2019) and critical discourse analysis (Wodak, 2015)
- Topic modeling and qualitative thematic analysis of newspaper coverage (US, Germany, China)
- Qualitative content analysis of AI organization websites

## Main Arguments
- **Industry dominance shapes discourse**: AI industry framings dominate media coverage, with Big Tech acting as primary knowledge producers about AI futures
- **Culturally particular imaginaries**: AI imaginaries differ significantly across national contexts—US commercialism, Chinese national development, German hesitancy—reflecting different discursive opportunity structures
- **Sovereignty as counter-imaginary**: In Germany, developing counter-imaginaries around sovereignty challenge industry dominance in AI regulation
- **Strategic issue management**: The AI industry employs premediation (projecting concerns to distant futures like AGI) and preclusion (absorbing regulatory critiques internally) to maintain control
- **Metaphorical instability**: Even within industry (Microsoft employees), AI relationships are unstable—alternating between tool, copilot, friend, puppy, toddler—revealing unsettled understandings
- **Temporal asymmetry**: Industry hopes are short-term and individualized while fears are long-term, relational, and societal, creating strategic ambiguity
- **Sustainability as peripheral**: Despite severe ecological, economic, and social effects, sustainability rarely appears explicitly in AI imaginaries; when present, focuses on efficiency and openness rather than justice
- **Geographic clustering**: AI discourse occurs in localized geographic centers with distinct focal points (Berlin: regulation/sovereignty; Silicon Valley: innovation/dominance)

## Limitations & Critiques
- **Limited geographic scope**: Focus on US, Germany, and China may miss important AI imaginaries from Global South contexts
- **Platform-specific analysis**: Twitter/X and LinkedIn may not capture imaginaries circulating in other spaces or offline contexts
- **Temporal snapshot**: Rapid AI development means imaginaries captured in 2023-2024 may quickly become outdated
- **Industry insider bias**: Microsoft employee study may overrepresent perspectives of those already committed to AI adoption
- **Sustainability criteria ambiguity**: Efficiency can be interpreted as both sustainability criterion and progress metric, creating analytical confusion
- **Missing voices**: Limited representation of impacted communities, workers displaced by AI, and marginalized stakeholders in imaginary construction
- **Methodological challenges**: Distinguishing between performative corporate discourse and genuine organizational commitments
- **Counter-imaginary fragmentation**: Alternative AI organizations may replicate Big Tech logics despite framing themselves as alternatives
