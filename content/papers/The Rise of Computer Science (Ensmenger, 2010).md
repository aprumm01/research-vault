# Ensmenger-The Rise of Computer Science

## Summary
This chapter explores the rise to dominance of theoretical computer science as the representative science of modern computing between 1955 and 1975. The narrative traces how early computer programmers from diverse backgrounds struggled to establish academic legitimacy for their discipline, facing self-doubt and professional uncertainty. The chapter argues that the emergence of computer science reflects messy compromises about what academic computing should look like, what subjects it should address, and how it should relate to established disciplines and the commercial computer industry. The advocates of theoretical computer science pursued a strategy emphasizing algorithms and mathematical theory that served them well within universities but increasingly alienated them from industry practitioners who viewed academic programs as disconnected from commercial computing realities.

## Key Concepts
- [[Algorithm as foundational concept]] - Abstract computational procedures as the essence of computer science
- [[Trading zones in computing]] - Interdisciplinary spaces where researchers from different backgrounds interact productively
- [[Black art mentality]] - Programming viewed as idiosyncratic genius work rather than systematic knowledge
- [[Professionalization through science]] - Using theoretical knowledge to distinguish professionals from technicians
- [[Normal science in computing]] - Establishment of disciplinary identity through textbooks, curriculum, and puzzles
- [[Theory-practice divide]] - Tension between academic computer science and commercial programming needs
- [[Computing centers as service providers]] - Early institutional position limiting academic legitimacy
- [[Von Neumann architecture]] - Logical abstraction of computer design from physical implementation

## Theoretical Framework
- [[Kuhn's normal science]] - Scientific revolutions require textbook histories and shared puzzles (Kuhn)
- [[Galison's trading zones]] - Interdisciplinary collaboration without universal agreement on significance
- [[Sociology of professions]] - Professional development requires control over organized body of knowledge
- [[Technological determinism vs abstraction]] - Tension between computer-as-tool vs computer-as-abstraction paradigms
- [[Sciences of the artificial]] - Legitimacy of studying human-made phenomena scientifically (Simon, 1967)

## Methods
- Historical analysis drawing on ACM publications, curriculum documents, and Turing Award lectures
- Institutional history examining Harvard, MIT, Princeton, Columbia, University of Pennsylvania computing programs
- Discourse analysis of debates about computer science legitimacy in Communications of the ACM
- Examination of curriculum development (Curriculum '68) as disciplinary boundary work

## Main Arguments
- Computer science's rise was not inevitable - many objected that computers were merely tools like microscopes or telephones, not deserving separate departments
- Early programmers (like Dijkstra) faced profound professional uncertainty, unsure if programming was “respectable” without a “sound body of knowledge” to support it
- The ACM deliberately styled itself as academic organization emphasizing theory over practice, establishing peer review, educational standards, and the Turing Award
- Computer scientists succeeded by abstracting computing from physical machines - focusing on algorithms rather than hardware, following von Neumann's logical architecture approach
- The algorithm emerged as computer science's fundamental unit of analysis, linking the discipline to al-Khwārizmī and positioning it alongside fundamental sciences
- Curriculum '68 embedded theoretical approach (computability, automata, formal languages) while treating programming skill as mere “by-product” rather than main purpose
- This theoretical turn alienated industry - employers complained graduates were interested in “playing games, making fancy programs that really do not work” rather than practical engineering
- Computer science succeeded academically (20% annual growth in degrees, 5% of male undergraduates) but failed to professionalize data processing - employers preferred MBAs over CS graduates
- The emergence of computer science represents agenda-setting for professionalization, but the scientific model was only one competing paradigm alongside others like certified public accountant

## Limitations & Critiques
- Chapter focuses primarily on elite research universities (Harvard, MIT, Princeton, Stanford), potentially missing development at teaching colleges and regional universities
- Limited discussion of gender dynamics despite noting feminization of early computing labor and shift to male-dominated academic discipline
- The narrative privileges ACM perspective and theoretical computer science advocates, giving less voice to industry practitioners and alternative visions
- Insufficient attention to funding mechanisms and government/military influence on curriculum development
- The chapter ends abruptly at 1975 without fully exploring how theory-practice tensions resolved (or didn't) in subsequent decades
- Regional and international variations in computer science development are mentioned (informatik vs computer science) but not thoroughly analyzed
- The role of specific corporate actors (IBM, RAND) in shaping academic programs deserves more systematic treatment
- Limited discussion of pedagogical methods - focuses on what was taught, not how teaching actually occurred
- The chapter's focus on professionalization as primary driver may underplay genuine intellectual excitement about computational ideas
- Doesn't fully account for why some traditional disciplines (mathematics, electrical engineering) ceded territory to computer science despite having institutional advantages
