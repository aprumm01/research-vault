# Artificial Intelligence for Design Education: A Conceptual Approach to Enhance Students' Divergent and Convergent Thinking in Ideation Processes

## Summary
This paper theoretically conceptualizes the integration of AI as a facilitator within Creativity Support Tools (CSTs) to enhance both divergent and convergent thinking during ideation in design higher education—a gap in current CSTs, which predominantly support only divergent thinking. Using concept-driven design research methodology and qualitative interviews with 10 design students, the authors develop and iteratively refine a theoretically-grounded design concept. The key contribution is a conceptual framework for AI facilitation that supports both thinking phases while preserving student agency, structured around four themes: clarity and comprehension requirements, visualization of user journeys, the role of AI, and the balance between predictable and unpredictable interactions.

## Key Concepts
- **Creativity Support Tools (CSTs)**: Digital tools designed to support creative work (Jamboard, Miro, Canva, Figma)—identified as predominantly supporting divergent thinking while neglecting convergent thinking
- **Divergent Thinking in CSTs**: The generative phase of ideation—generating multiple, varied ideas—currently well-supported by most CSTs through brainstorming features and idea generation scaffolds
- **Convergent Thinking in CSTs**: The refinement phase—narrowing ideas to viable solutions through evaluation, systematic definition, and structured decision-making—identified as underserved by current CST design
- **AI as Facilitator**: The conceptual role for AI proposed by the paper—not as a content generator replacing student creativity, but as a structural guide that scaffolds both phases while students retain creative agency
- **Motivational Role of AI**: AI supports divergent thinking by challenging designers with goals, creative constraints, and perspective prompts—encouraging exploration without dominating the creative flow
- **Cooperative Role of AI**: AI supports convergent thinking by providing relevant insights, constructive suggestions for refinement, and data-driven analysis—working alongside designers rather than directing them
- **Human-AI Symbiosis**: The collaborative vision underpinning the framework—humans and AI working in joint effort, with shared agency, where AI extends rather than replaces human cognitive capabilities
- **Concept-Driven Design Research**: The methodological approach—developing theoretically-grounded design concepts through iterative refinement with empirical validation, positioned between pure theory and empirical case studies

## Theoretical Framework
The paper integrates two theoretical streams: AI collaboration theory (human-AI symbiosis, distributed cognition, the motivational/cooperative roles of AI in creative work) and creativity theory (Guilford's divergent thinking dimensions, the balance of divergent and convergent phases in design ideation). The concept-driven design research methodology (Stolterman and Wiberg, 2010) is used to move from theoretical foundations to a concrete design concept through iterative refinement—a methodology appropriate for proposing new design directions rather than evaluating existing systems.

The paper identifies a critical theoretical gap: while research on divergent thinking support in CSTs is extensive, convergent thinking support is poorly theorized and under-designed. AI's potential to hold and process complex design criteria, recall previous decisions, and provide structured evaluation guidance positions it as uniquely suited to fill this gap.

## Methods
Concept-driven design research with two phases: (1) theoretical synthesis to develop an initial AI-enhanced CST design concept, drawing on HCI, creativity, and AI education literature; and (2) qualitative interviews with 10 design students to evaluate and refine the concept. Interviews were structured around the proposed design concept, eliciting student responses to specific design features and their perceived value for different ideation phases. Thematic analysis of interview data yielded four key themes that informed a revised, empirically-refined design concept.

## Main Arguments
- Current CSTs fail to support the convergent thinking phase—evaluation, decision-making, and systematic refinement—despite this being as important to design quality as divergent exploration; this gap represents a significant opportunity for AI-enhanced CST design
- AI is most appropriately conceptualized as a facilitator rather than a content generator in design education contexts—a role that preserves student creative agency while providing structural guidance across both ideation phases
- The motivational role of AI (prompting exploration, challenging assumptions, expanding perspective) is most valuable in divergent phases, while the cooperative role (refining, analyzing, evaluating against criteria) is most valuable in convergent phases—and ideal AI facilitation must support both
- Students' four key requirements for an AI-enhanced CST were: clarity and comprehension (the system must explain its reasoning transparently), visualization of user journeys (students needed to see their process trajectory), appropriate AI role definition (not too directive, not too passive), and balance between predictable and unpredictable AI interactions
- Unpredictable AI outputs—suggestions the student didn't anticipate—were valued for stimulating creative exploration, while predictable structured guidance was valued for convergent refinement; effective AI facilitation requires dynamic modulation between these modes
- The framework extends existing CST design theory by providing explicit conceptual grounding for AI integration that goes beyond interface convenience to address the cognitive structure of creative design work

## Limitations & Critiques
The paper proposes a theoretical design concept that has not been implemented or empirically tested as an actual system—the study evaluates a described concept, not a working prototype, which limits claims about real-world effectiveness. The small interview sample (N=10) may not capture the diversity of design students' ideation approaches. The concept-driven methodology produces theoretical richness but relatively weak empirical evidence compared to experimental studies.

The paper focuses on higher education design students specifically; the transferability of the framework to professional designers, K-12 contexts, or other creative disciplines is unaddressed. The distinction between motivational and cooperative AI roles, while conceptually useful, may be difficult to operationalize in practice without careful system design.
