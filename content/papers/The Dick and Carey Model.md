## Summary
Walter Dick reviews two decades of change to the Dick and Carey instructional design model and assesses its prospects for continued relevance. He examines how the model has adapted to incorporate constructivist influences in its 1996 edition, addresses potential threats from alternative ID textbooks and declining interest in systematic ID, and argues that the model's empirical grounding and flexibility position it to remain useful—if updated—in an evolving educational landscape.

## Key Concepts

## Theoretical Framework
The Dick and Carey model synthesizes behavioral, cognitive, and emerging constructivist learning theories into a systematic design process, and this chapter evaluates the theoretical tensions involved.

## Methods
Critical review and historical analysis; examination of the model's four editions and their theoretical evolution; assessment of competing pressures from constructivism and alternative approaches.

## Main Arguments
- The Dick and Carey model has successfully incorporated constructivist elements while maintaining systematic rigor
- Declining interest in systematic ID within academic programs poses a challenge to the model's future
- Constructivist and objectivist models serve different learning goals; neither is universally superior
- The model's long track record and adaptability provide a foundation for continued relevance
