# Poets Over Quants: Automation and AI Threats Increase the Value People Place on Creativity

## Summary
This empirical research paper examines how perceived automation and AI threats (the belief that advanced technology threatens humans' career prospects) shape workers' strategies for career preparation. Across nine studies (N = 2,320; three preregistered), the authors find that perceived automation threat drives people to prioritize creative skills over technical and social skills. A pilot study revealed that people view creativity as less prone to automation and more likely to complement automation.

The subsequent experiments confirmed that automation threat leads people to highlight creativity in job applications (Studies 1a-1c), leads STEM students and professional graphic designers to cultivate creative abilities through course selection (Studies 2a-2b), and increases jobseekers' interest in companies that champion creativity (Study 3). Critically, people continue to value creative skills in response to the automation threat even when reminded of generative AI's ability for creativity (Studies 4a-4b). These results suggest that advanced technology steers individuals to prioritize creativity as a skill necessary to compete in the labor market.

The findings have important implications for higher education curriculum design, organizational talent retention, and understanding how people psychologically adapt to technological disruption in the workplace.

## Key Concepts
- **Automation Threat**: The perception that advanced technology such as robots and AI will threaten humans' career prospects and employment opportunities
- **Skill Substitutability**: The extent to which people believe new technologies will be capable of replacing human workers for a particular skill
- **Skill Complementarity**: The extent to which people perceive each skill as increasing in importance for job performance alongside technological advancement
- **Creative Skills**: Skills emphasizing novelty and usefulness including innovation, imagination, originality, inventiveness, and creativity
- **Technical Skills**: Job-related skills such as software development, programming languages, technical writing, data analysis, and mathematics
- **Social Skills**: Skills essential for face-to-face interactions including negotiation, teamwork, communication, leadership, and conflict management

## Theoretical Framework
The paper builds on economic frameworks examining which skills are truly automatable and integrates lay perceptions of machine versus human capabilities. Drawing from dehumanization research (humans and machines share cognitive capabilities but humans possess superior emotional capabilities) and mind perception research (machines have agency but lack emotional experience), the authors theorize that creativity occupies a unique position: it is associated with emotionality (a distinctively human trait) and reflects nonroutine tasks that traditionally face less susceptibility to automation. People view creativity as not only resistant to automation but also as a skill that may thrive alongside technological advancements.

## Methods
The research employed a multi-study experimental design using primarily Amazon Mechanical Turk and targeted samples (STEM students, professional graphic designers):

- **Pilot Study**: Assessed beliefs about skill substitutability and complementarity for 15 skills (5 social, 5 technical, 5 creative)
- **Studies 1a-1c**: Participants imagined being job applicants and selected skills to highlight in cover letters after reading about automation vs. foreign labor competition
- **Studies 2a-2b**: Examined skill acquisition through course/training selection among STEM students and graphic designers
- **Study 3**: Tested company preferences between creativity-focused vs. analytically-focused organizations
- **Studies 4a-4b**: Included conditions describing generative AI's creative capabilities to test whether awareness of AI creativity diminishes the preference for creative skills

All studies used random assignment to conditions (automation competition, foreign labor competition, and/or control), with mixed-model ANOVAs analyzing skill type preferences.

## Main Arguments
- People perceive creative skills as both less substitutable by automation AND more complementary to automation than technical or social skills
- When faced with automation threats, individuals consistently prioritize creative skills in job applications, skill acquisition, and employer preferences
- This preference for creativity persists even when people are informed that AI can produce creative content, suggesting creativity is viewed as irreplaceably human
- The automation threat specifically (not generic labor market threats like foreign competition) drives creativity prioritization
- These lay beliefs about skill value may not align with expert predictions about which skills are actually automation-proof, creating potential mismatches between worker preparation and labor market realities

## Limitations & Critiques
- The studies focused primarily on college-educated individuals; those lacking higher education may respond differently to technological threats (potentially prioritizing technical skills as basic employment criteria)
- Highly specialized individuals (e.g., computer scientists vs. artists) might view skill trade-offs differently depending on their field
- The restricted range of skills participants could highlight may not capture all valuable workplace competencies
- While the studies document preference shifts, they do not assess whether emphasizing creativity is actually an effective labor market strategy
- The research does not examine whether these preference changes reflect genuine belief in creativity's value or simply a psychological strategy to preserve one's sense of humanity in an automated world
- Self-reported skill preferences may not translate to actual behavioral changes in career development
