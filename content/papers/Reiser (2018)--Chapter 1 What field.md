# Reiser (2018)--Chapter 1 What field

## Summary
Reiser's 2018 chapter "What Field Did You Say You Were In? Defining and Naming Our Field" examines the contested identity of the field variously called instructional design, instructional technology, educational technology, and learning design. Reiser traces the field's historical development from its roots in audiovisual education and programmed instruction through the systems approach era and into contemporary e-learning and performance technology contexts. He argues that the field's recurring identity crisis reflects genuine tensions between academic and practitioner communities, between learning-focused and performance-focused orientations, and between the field's roots and its current scope.

## Key Concepts
- **Field identity crisis**: The recurring difficulty the field faces in defining, naming, and bounding itself
- **Instructional design vs. educational technology**: The tension between a practice-oriented label (ID) and a broader field label (ET)
- **Historical evolution**: Audiovisual → programmed instruction → systems approach → e-learning → performance technology
- **Learning vs. performance orientation**: Whether the field's primary purpose is facilitating learning or improving human and organizational performance
- **Practitioner-academic divide**: Different communities within the field emphasize different purposes, methods, and identities

## Theoretical Framework
Historical and analytical review of the field's development; draws on prior AECT definitions, published histories of instructional design and technology, and analysis of professional associations, journals, and program titles.

## Methods
Narrative historical review synthesizing primary and secondary sources on the field's development from the early 20th century through 2018.

## Main Arguments
1. The field's inability to settle on a name reflects genuine substantive disagreements about its purpose, scope, and identity
2. The shift from "audiovisual" to "instructional design" to "educational technology" to "learning design" reflects changing theoretical and practical emphases
3. The performance technology orientation (HPT) represents a significant departure from the field's learning-centered roots
4. Resolving field identity requires grappling with these substantive tensions, not merely settling on a marketing label

## Limitations
Historical narrative necessarily involves selection and interpretation; alternative periodizations and emphases are possible. The chapter reflects primarily U.S.-centric perspectives on field development.
