## Summary
Argues that AI practitioners—developers, data scientists, engineers—suffer from a "sociotechnical gap" caused by technical training that renders them "environment blind" to human and social implications of the systems they build. The article calls for the HCI community to develop standardized metrics for measuring practitioner attitudes and evaluating the effectiveness of interventions designed to shift those mindsets.

## Key Concepts
- Sociotechnical gap
- Environment blindness
- Responsible and ethical AI
- Kirkpatrick evaluation model (adapted)
- Practitioner culture

## Theoretical Framework
Draws on Harbers and Overdiek's distinction between high-level principles and concrete practices; positions responsible AI not as a compliance problem but as a culture and attitude problem requiring measurable change.

## Methods
Positional/argumentative article; reviews existing HCI and responsible AI literature; proposes a research agenda rather than reporting original empirical data.

## Main Arguments
- AI practitioners trained in technical fields tend to overlook sociotechnical considerations, producing a persistent gap between technical output and human outcomes
- Existing interventions (toolkits, guidelines) lack standardized evaluation metrics to assess their impact on attitudes
- Interdisciplinary collaboration and participatory design can surface values and needs that mono-disciplinary settings miss
- A culture shift—not just new tools—is required for practitioners to embrace human-centered AI

## Limitations & Critiques
No original empirical data; the proposed metrics framework is outlined conceptually but not yet developed or validated; the characterization of practitioner cultures may not reflect the diversity of AI development contexts globally.
