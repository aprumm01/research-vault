# Teaching Digital Design in an AI Driven Era: Exploring Curriculum Pedagogy and Student Perspectives

## Summary
This study investigates how first-year university students in digital design modules at Manchester Metropolitan University engage with AI tools in practice-based learning contexts emphasizing interactive and human-centered development. Using a mixed-methods approach combining Likert-scale surveys and open-ended qualitative responses, the paper maps student experiences with AI across dimensions of ease of use, perceived usefulness, creativity impact, and ethical reflection. It develops an integrated four-lens conceptual model (TAM, Creative Cognition Theory, Digital Literacy, AI Ethics) and argues for curriculum strategies that balance computational capabilities with human creativity through reflective, critically-grounded practice.

## Key Concepts
- **Integrated Conceptual Model**: The paper's theoretical contribution—a four-lens framework combining Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), Creative Cognition Theory, Digital Literacy, and AI Ethics to understand how students adopt, use, and reflect on AI in design learning
- **Practice-Based Learning**: The pedagogical approach used in the study—students learn AI integration through doing rather than instruction, making authentic design projects the site of AI literacy development
- **AI Literacy in Design**: The competency framework developed, encompassing: technical understanding of how AI works, critical evaluation of AI outputs, ethical awareness of implications, and reflective practice that integrates all dimensions
- **Shift from Graphic to Digital Design**: The broader pedagogical context—design education has transitioned from print-focused to digitally immersive (UX, UI, motion graphics, interactive media), with AI representing the latest phase of this ongoing evolution
- **Five Institutional Principles**: A framework the institution established for responsible AI integration—covering critical engagement, AI literacy development, academic regulation, pedagogical risk mitigation, and professional preparedness
- **Reflective Practice**: The Schön-inspired capacity for students to pause, examine their own use of AI tools, and develop principled stances on when and how AI assistance is appropriate
- **Efficiency vs. Depth Tension**: The core pedagogical tension identified—students value AI for speed and productivity but risk surface-level reliance that bypasses the deeper learning that comes from wrestling with design problems directly

## Theoretical Framework
The study deploys a four-lens conceptual model that functions as both an analytical and curriculum design framework. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM, Davis) explains adoption patterns through perceived usefulness and ease of use. Creative Cognition Theory (Finke, Ward, Smith) explains how external tools scaffold or extend ideation. Digital Literacy frameworks explain the evaluative, critical, and reflective competencies required to work with digital systems responsibly. AI Ethics (transparency, accountability, authorship, integrity) bounds responsible practice. The integrated model shows a sequential conceptual flow: TAM-governed adoption enables tool access; Creative Cognition can amplify ideation; but this must be mediated by Digital Literacy critical use and bounded by AI Ethics responsible practice.

The study also integrates Mishra's Teaching Compass in the Age of AI and the PNAS framework for Protecting Scientific Integrity in an Age of Generative AI as institutional-level frameworks for grounding curriculum policy.

## Methods
Mixed-methods design collecting survey data from students across multiple digital design courses at Manchester Metropolitan University. Quantitative data: Likert-scale items assessing ease of use, perceived usefulness, impact on creativity, and workflow effects. Qualitative data: open-ended survey responses eliciting student perspectives, concerns, and ethical reflections. The survey was structured around the four theoretical lenses. Statistical analysis included ANOVA for between-group comparisons; thematic analysis was used for qualitative data. The study spans multiple digital design courses including introduction to design, web development, UX, UI, creative digital technologies, concept and prototype development, and immersive design.

## Main Arguments
- The integrated TAM/Creative Cognition/Digital Literacy/AI Ethics model provides a comprehensive analytical framework that avoids the common limitation of treating AI adoption in design education through any single theoretical lens
- Students value AI primarily for efficiency (speed, productivity) but risk developing surface-level reliance—the critical pedagogical challenge is redirecting students from passive AI use toward active, reflective, critically-informed engagement
- First-year students show particular vulnerability to uncritical AI adoption—without structured scaffolding, they tend to prioritize outputs over process, undermining the foundational design learning objectives of the introductory courses
- Educators play a constitutive role in fostering the collaborative mindsets that balance computational capabilities with human creativity—this is not a role AI can assume and not an outcome that emerges naturally from unguided AI access
- The curriculum must embed ethical AI use from the start, treating questions of authorship, bias, data provenance, and academic integrity as integral to design competence rather than external compliance requirements
- The evolution from graphic to digital design provides pedagogical continuity—each technological shift has required design education to develop new critical competencies, and AI literacy is the current iteration of this ongoing adaptive challenge

## Limitations & Critiques
The paper does not specify the sample size, student demographics, or response rates, making it difficult to evaluate the representativeness of findings. The mixed-methods design is described but detailed statistical results are not fully reported in the extracted sections, limiting assessment of effect sizes and significance. The single-institution UK context may not generalize to other national educational systems, institution types, or cultural contexts.

The four-lens conceptual model, while comprehensive, may be too theoretically dense to serve directly as a practical curriculum design tool without significant operationalization work. The paper advocates for reflective practice without specifying concrete assessment mechanisms for evaluating whether students have developed it.
