# The Capitalist Machine Computerization, Workers& 039 Power, and the Decline in Labor& 039 s Share within Kristal, T. 2013

## Summary
This article addresses an important trend in contemporary income inequality—a decline in labor’s share of national income and a rise in capitalists’ profits share Since the late 1970s, labor’s share declined by 6 percent across the U As I will show, this overall decline was due to a large decline (5 to 14 percent) in construction, manufacturing, and transportation combined with an increase, albeit small (2 to 5 percent), in labor’s share within finance and services industries.

## Key Concepts
- Labor's share of national income declining 6% since late 1970s in U.S. private sector
- Class-biased technological change through computerization favoring employers over employees
- Workers' positional power indicators (unionization, capital concentration, import penetration, unemployment)
- Diverse sectoral trends: 5-14% decline in construction/manufacturing/transportation vs 2-5% increase in finance/services
- Direct and indirect effects of computer technology on labor's share
- Error correction models with fixed-effects estimators
- Within-industry variation over time as analytical focus

## Theoretical Framework
[[frameworks/Class Conflict Theory]] - Power relations between capital and labor

## Methods
longitudinal quantitative analysis, error correction models, fixed-effects estimators

## Main Arguments
- Main factor in declining labor's share was erosion of workers' positional power
- Computerization was class-biased technological change that strengthened employer position
- Aggregate 6% decline conceals diverse trends: large decline in unionized core industries vs stability/increase in services
- Labor's share decline only in core unionized industries despite computer technology across all sectors suggests class conflict central
- Fixed-effects estimators using within-industry variation provide unbiased estimates when industry effects correlated with predictors
- Combines data from 43 two-digit nonagricultural private industries and 451 four-digit manufacturing industries (1969-2007)

## Limitations & Critiques
- Data availability constraints: unionization data only from 1968, computer investment data from 1977
- Major industry classification change in 1997 required separate datasets breaking continuity
- Focus on U.S. private sector may not generalize to public sector or international contexts
- Computer technology measured through investment may not capture full complexity of technological change
- Fixed-effects estimators focus on within-industry variation, may miss between-industry dynamics
- Does not fully unpack mechanisms through which computerization strengthened employer power
- Limited attention to worker resistance or adaptation strategies beyond unionization
