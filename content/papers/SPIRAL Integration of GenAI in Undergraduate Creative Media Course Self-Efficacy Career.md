# SPIRAL Integration of Generative AI in an Undergraduate Creative Media Course: Effects on Self-Efficacy and Career Outcome Expectations

## Summary
This mixed-methods study evaluates a novel SPIRAL pedagogical strategy (Skills Practiced Independently, Revisited with AI Later) for integrating generative AI into an introductory creative media and technology course (n=31, Fall 2023). Students first developed domain skills for half the semester, then revisited earlier material using GenAI with explicit critical and ethical instruction. The study finds positive changes in creative media self-efficacy and AI use self-efficacy, a "demystification" trajectory where initial career fears about AI displacement transition to more nuanced positions, and either neutral or positive effects on career outcome expectations.

## Key Concepts
- **SPIRAL Integration**: A novel pedagogical sequencing strategy — Skills Practiced Independently, Revisited with AI Later — designed to build domain self-efficacy before AI exposure, ensuring students have the foundational competence to critically evaluate AI outputs rather than uncritically depend on them
- **Self-Efficacy (Bandura / SCCT)**: Belief in one's ability to accomplish tasks; central to Socio-Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) as a predictor of career interest, task engagement, and persistence — the primary outcome variable of the study
- **Socio-Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT)**: The theoretical framework linking self-efficacy, career outcome expectations, and environmental factors (vicarious experience, social support) to explain how students form and revise career interests in computing fields
- **Demystification Trajectory**: A qualitative finding that students transitioned from initial fear about AI replacing their fields and jobs, to doubting AI's capability to do so and/or expecting societal pushback, through personal use of AI and vicarious observation of others using it
- **Generative AI Use Self-Efficacy**: A new construct introduced in the study (no validated instrument exists) measuring students' confidence in using GenAI critically and ethically — distinguished from general AI familiarity
- **Ethical Generative AI Use Self-Efficacy**: Mixed changes observed — some students became more confident in ethical AI use, others showed decreased confidence as they became more aware of AI's complexity and limitations
- **Career Outcome Expectation**: Students' beliefs about what outcomes (career options, job security) will result from pursuing a given career path; found to be either neutral or positively influenced by the SPIRAL integration

## Theoretical Framework
The paper grounds its analysis in Socio-Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT, Lent et al.), which provides a well-validated account of how self-efficacy and outcome expectations shape career development. SCCT posits a feedback loop: task performance influences self-efficacy, which influences career interests, which influence future engagement. Environmental factors — vicarious experience (observing peers), social support, and perceived labor market conditions — also shape this process.

The paper applies SCCT to argue that generative AI introduces a novel challenge: when AI can outperform novices on tasks they are still learning, how can instructors develop self-efficacy without triggering learned helplessness or career abandonment? The SPIRAL strategy is proposed as a structural answer: by building foundational self-efficacy before AI exposure, students enter the AI phase with a more robust sense of their own capabilities, making AI a partner rather than a competitor.

## Methods
Mixed-methods study with both quantitative and qualitative strands. Quantitative strand: pre-post surveys using validated instruments (MSLQ for creative media self-efficacy; MAWESS for academic writing self-efficacy) plus custom generative AI critical and ethical use self-efficacy questions. Qualitative strand: longitudinal interviews with nine students at multiple points during the semester, plus pre-post survey responses on career interests, analysed using thematic analysis. The course was implemented in Fall 2023 with 31 students in an introductory undergraduate creative media and technology course at the University of Maine.

## Main Arguments
- The SPIRAL integration strategy — building domain skills first, then introducing AI with explicit critical and ethical instruction — produces positive gains in creative media self-efficacy and AI use self-efficacy, suggesting that sequencing matters for how AI affects learner development
- Students undergo a "demystification" process during the course: initial fears about AI replacing their careers give way to more nuanced views, either doubting AI's ability to do so or expecting societal resistance — a shift attributable to direct personal use and vicarious observation of AI's actual limitations
- Career outcome expectations are either neutral or positively influenced by the SPIRAL approach, contradicting concerns that AI integration universally threatens students' sense of career viability; instead, direct engagement with AI demystifies the threat
- Ethical AI use self-efficacy shows mixed changes, with some students becoming less confident as they recognise the complexity of responsible AI use — interpreted as productive epistemic awareness rather than a negative outcome
- The study provides evidence that pedagogical sequencing is a consequential variable: integrating AI from the start without prior skill development risks triggering dependence and illusion of competence, while the SPIRAL approach supports both skill development and critical AI fluency
- Creative media and technology education is identified as an underexplored but valuable site for AI integration research, offering insights about non-traditional computing pathways that existing programming-focused research may miss

## Limitations & Critiques
The small sample size (n=31) significantly limits statistical power and generalisability, and the lack of a control group means the pre-post changes cannot be definitively attributed to the SPIRAL strategy versus other course factors. The study is conducted at a single institution and in a course type (creative media) not widely studied in AI education research, reducing comparability. Custom instruments for AI use self-efficacy have not been validated, limiting the reliability of those measures. The qualitative strand (9 interviews) provides rich but limited coverage of student experiences. The study was conducted in Fall 2023, before many of the most significant generative AI developments and widespread adoption; replication in updated contexts is warranted.
