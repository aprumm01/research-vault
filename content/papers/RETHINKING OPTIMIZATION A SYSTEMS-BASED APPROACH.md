## Key Concepts
- Systems thinking combined with economic externalities framework for optimization critique
- Social externalities as costs or benefits to third parties outside direct optimization processes
- Stakeholder characterization beyond traditional economic agents directly involved in optimization
- Feedback loops and interconnected nature of optimization consequences requiring holistic analysis
- Poor implementation practices in socioeconomic contexts producing unintended harmful consequences
- Normative perspective on externalities beyond descriptive economic quantification approaches
- Optimization efficiency valued but insufficient when excluding affected third-party stakeholders
- Systems-based approach identifying "who was affected, how, and where to include them" in optimization

## Theoretical Framework
The framework synthesizes systems thinking with economic externalities theory to address optimization failures in socioeconomic domains. Traditional optimization focuses narrowly on defined objectives and direct stakeholders, treating externalities as peripheral side-effects rather than central design considerations. Economic externalities provide quantification methods for identifying impacts on third parties but lack normative guidance on interconnections and feedback dynamics. Systems thinking contributes holistic perspective examining: (1) boundary definition determining which actors and effects are considered, (2) feedback loops where optimization consequences create second-order effects, (3) interconnected externalities that compound or counteract each other, and (4) emergent system behaviors invisible to narrow optimization. The integrated approach enables characterizing what went wrong, identifying affected parties, and determining intervention points for incorporating externalities into optimization processes.

## Main Arguments
- Optimization widely used for decision-making but poor implementation produces significant unintended consequences in socioeconomic contexts
- Affected stakeholders frequently fall outside direct optimization focus making externalities invisible to traditional approaches
- Economic frameworks describe externalities but fail to address normative implications or interconnected feedback dynamics
- Systems thinking provides holistic perspective identifying relationships, feedback loops, and emergent behaviors traditional optimization misses
- Stakeholder characterization requires going beyond direct participants to include those bearing external costs or benefits
- Incorporating externalities demands more than post-hoc correction, requiring fundamental rethinking of optimization problem formulation
- Combining economic quantification methods with systems thinking enables both identification of impacts and normative intervention design

## Limitations & Critiques
- Framework remains primarily theoretical without extensive empirical demonstration across diverse optimization contexts
- Complexity of systems thinking may make framework implementation challenging for practitioners trained in traditional optimization
- Stakeholder identification still requires subjective boundary judgments about which third parties to include
- Quantification of interconnected externalities and feedback loops may prove computationally intractable in complex systems
- Does not provide clear decision procedures when externalities create trade-offs between different stakeholder groups
- May underestimate resistance from organizations benefiting from narrow optimization that excludes externalities
- Systems thinking's holistic ambition risks analysis paralysis if all possible connections must be mapped
- Limited guidance on transitioning existing optimization systems to externality-inclusive approaches without disruption
