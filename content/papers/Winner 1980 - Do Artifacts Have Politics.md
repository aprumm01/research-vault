# Winner 1980 - Do Artifacts Have Politics

## Summary
The whale and the reactor: a search for limits in an age of high technology Chicago, University of Chicago Press, 19-39 ] No idea is more provocative in controversies about technology and society than the notion that technical things have political qualities.

## Key Concepts
- **Technical artifacts have political qualities**: Machines, structures, and systems can embody specific forms of power and authority
- **Two types of political artifacts**: (1) Specific designs that settle issues in particular communities, (2) Inherently political technologies that require or are compatible with particular political relationships
- **Social determination of technology**: Corrective view that technology is shaped by social/economic forces, but insufficient for understanding technology's political significance
- **Technological politics**: Theory examining momentum of sociotechnical systems, technological imperatives, and transformation of human ends through technical means
- **Robert Moses bridges example**: Low overpasses on Long Island parkways deliberately designed to exclude buses, thus discriminating against poor people and racial minorities
- **Authoritarian vs democratic technologies**: Mumford's distinction between system-centered/powerful/unstable and human-centered/weak/durable technologies
- **Politics definition**: Arrangements of power and authority in human associations and activities within those arrangements

## Theoretical Framework
Builds on Lewis Mumford's sociotechnical analysis, Science and Technology Studies (STS), and political philosophy. Challenges both naive technological determinism and pure social constructivism. Integrates concepts from critical theory about how material culture embodies power relations. References Husserl's "back to the things themselves" phenomenology.

## Methods
- Philosophical analysis and argumentation
- Historical case studies (Robert Moses bridges, Cyrus McCormick's factory, Baron Haussmann's Parisian boulevards)
- Conceptual distinction between two forms of political technology
- Analysis of pro- and anti-technology political rhetoric (nuclear power, solar energy, TVA)
- Critical examination of social determination vs technological determinism theories

## Main Arguments
- **Things have politics, not just people**: Physical arrangements can systematically embody social inequality and power relations
- **Social determination insufficient**: While corrective to naive determinism, maxim that "only social/economic system matters" misses how technologies are politically significant in their own right
- **Technologies as settlements of political issues**: Design choices (Moses bridges, campus buildings for crowd control, Haussmann's boulevards) resolve political questions through material form
- **Not all about efficiency**: Technological change expresses panoply of human motives, including desire for dominion, even at cost of efficiency
- **Enduring political consequences**: Once built, technical systems continue shaping power relations long after their creators are gone
- **Technological determinism has limits**: But so does social determination—need theory of technological politics as complement
- **Attention to characteristics matters**: Must examine technical objects and meaning of their characteristics, not just reduce to social forces
- **Two analytical approaches needed**: Both study of how technologies settle community issues AND analysis of inherently political systems
- **Standard social science models inadequate**: Interest-group politics, bureaucratic politics, class struggle don't fully account for what's distinctive about technology's political role

## Limitations & Critiques
- 1980 publication predates digital technologies that raise new questions about political artifacts
- Robert Moses example may be exceptional case rather than typical pattern
- Limited discussion of how to identify political qualities in less obvious cases
- Does not fully develop theory of "inherently political technologies" (promises but doesn't deliver full analysis in excerpt)
- May overstate intentionality in design—some political consequences may be unintended
- Limited attention to how artifacts' political meanings can change over time and context
- Does not extensively address possibilities for democratizing technology design processes
- US-centric examples may limit generalizability across different political cultures
