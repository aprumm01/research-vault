# Beyond Replacement or Augmentation - how creative workers reconfigure division of labor with genAI

## Study Partner Enrichment

### Overview of the Document

This paper comes from two researchers with complementary institutional perspectives: Michael F. Clarke, an independent researcher in Canada, and Michael Joffe from Google Inc., Canada. Both bring unique vantage points to the question of how generative AI reshapes creative work. Clarke's independence suggests grounding in ethnographic traditions free from corporate constraints, while Joffe's position at Google provides insider knowledge of how AI tools are developed and deployed in industry contexts. Their combined perspective is neither purely academic nor purely corporate, but rather bridges both worlds.

This is an empirical qualitative study published in proceedings for CSCW, the flagship conference for Computer-Supported Cooperative Work and Human-Computer Interaction research. It sits squarely in the tradition of workplace ethnography and ethnomethodology, drawing on the work of Harold Garfinkel and other sociologists who study the practical, everyday methods people use to make their workplaces function. The document matters because it refuses the dominant narrative that AI will either replace workers or simply augment them. Instead, it shows how creative professionals actively reconfigure and repair the division of labor with generative AI tools like ChatGPT and Midjourney in real time, drawing on tacit workplace knowledge to make AI outputs accountable to stakeholders.

### Research Overview

The central research question asks: How do creative professionals practically organize and configure a division of labor with generative AI in their workplace? More specifically, how do they delegate roles to AI tools, repair boundaries when things break down, and manage stakeholder impressions when AI is involved in creative output?

Clarke and Joffe themselves conducted this study. They interviewed 17 participants working at 12 different creative agencies (ranging from 50 to over 75,000 employees) in the United States and Canada between April and August 2024. The agencies produce media, advertising assets, and brand strategy for clients. Data collection involved semi-structured remote interviews lasting 20 minutes to one hour, recorded via Zoom. Some interviews included follow-up deep dives examining specific generative AI prompts and responses.

The researchers used an ethnomethodologically informed approach. Ethnomethodology is a sociological tradition that examines the commonsense methods people rely upon to accomplish ordinary work. As Clarke and Joffe explain, "we subscribe to the 'phenomenological' variant of ethnomethodology, using interviews as a commonsense resource for data" (p. 141). This means they treat participant accounts as practical descriptions of how work gets done, not as claims to universal truth. The interviews accessed home work environments, which removed overhead and increased candor on a sensitive topic.

Key concepts to understand include reflexive delegation, where workers interpretively configure AI as a collaborator by assigning it roles like "35 year old pharmaceutical writer" or "junior analyst"; boundary repair, where workers discursively adjust AI's role when outputs fail to meet workplace standards; and templatized trust, where workers create repeatable prompts and workflows to scale AI outputs while managing client anxieties about brand safety and AI usage.

Clarke and Joffe write: "Through 17 ethnomethodologically informed interviews with international creative agency workers we demonstrate how roles are assigned to generative AI tools, how their contributions are modified and remediated, and how workers practically manage their outputs to reflect assumptions of internal and external stakeholders" (p. 10).

### Theories of Knowledge

The paper is deeply rooted in ethnomethodology, a theoretical framework developed by sociologist Harold Garfinkel in the 1960s. Ethnomethodology studies the everyday methods people use to produce and recognize socially organized activities. As Garfinkel argued in *Studies in Ethnomethodology* (1967), people rely on commonsense reasoning and contextual knowledge to make their actions accountable and intelligible to others. Accountability here means that actions are recognizable as sensible, appropriate, and competent within a given setting.

Clarke and Joffe cite Garfinkel extensively to frame their analysis. They explain that workers must "know the settings in which he is to operate if his practices are to serve as measures to bring particular, located features of these settings to recognizable account" (Garfinkel, 1967, p. 8, quoted in the paper). In other words, creative workers can only make AI outputs work because they possess tacit workplace knowledge about what counts as good work, who the stakeholders are, and what those stakeholders expect.

The paper also draws on Computer-Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW) theory, particularly the CSCW tradition of studying how technologies are embedded in and shaped by workplace practices. Harper and Randall (cited in the paper) argue that "dealing with culture where technologies are used is the business of CSCW...the approach speaks naturally to what is done with AI and ML, not claims about the technologies" (p. 130). This means the paper refuses technological determinism—it doesn't assume AI has fixed effects, but rather examines how AI is made to work through human interpretive labor.

Typifications and membership categorization devices, concepts from phenomenological sociology and ethnomethodology, also play a key role. Typifications are idealized categories (like "junior analyst" or "35 year old marketing executive") that people use to organize their understanding of others. When a creative worker tells ChatGPT to act like a "35 year old millennial marketing executive," they are invoking a typification that carries assumptions about tone, expertise, and cultural sensibility. As Harvey Sacks argued, membership categorization devices are methods for navigating culture by identifying people and concepts as types tied to specific contexts (Sacks, 1974, referenced in the paper).

The authors also reference socio-technical systems theory implicitly throughout, showing how technical resources (AI tools) and non-technical resources (workplace knowledge, client relationships) are artfully arranged to accomplish creative outcomes. The division of labor is not preordained by technology but emerges through situated, reflexive practices.

**Why this theoretical stance matters:** Ethnomethodology reveals that AI's role in the workplace is not fixed by its technical capabilities but constructed through practical reasoning. Workers shape AI, and AI in turn shapes the possibilities for work, but neither determines the other. This reframes debates about AI replacing or augmenting workers by showing that both framings miss the interpretive work required to make AI function in actual workplaces.

### Central Arguments

The central argument of this paper is that working with generative AI in creative settings involves reflexively respecifying a division of labor. Clarke and Joffe reject the binary framing of AI as either replacing or augmenting workers. Instead, they argue that creative professionals actively configure generative AI as a workplace collaborator through three interconnected practices: reflexive delegation, boundary repair, and templatized trust.

**Reflexive delegation** means workers assign roles to AI by drawing on typifications and membership categorization devices. They craft AI personas like "junior analyst" or "35 year old pharmaceutical writer" that reflect contextual workplace assumptions about competence, tone, and expertise. As the authors write, "prompts are situated tools to a local organizational challenge. They are reflexive tools, and the shape that they take is dependent on the worker collaborating with them" (p. 185). This is not just instructing a machine—it's configuring a coworker whose contributions must be made accountable to internal and external stakeholders.

One art director explained: "I always start by kind of baking in like the requirements ahead of time…I want you to be…a professional writer in the pharmaceutical field...take your 35 year old" (p. 207). The specificity of age and domain is not arbitrary—it invokes expectations about writing style, professional maturity, and subject matter knowledge that the worker needs for the task at hand. Another participant described prompting Claude rather than ChatGPT because "it's a little bit less businessy. It's a little bit more creative and a little bit more human" (p. 275), demonstrating how workers interpretively match AI tools to different creative tasks.

**Boundary repair** describes the work of adjusting AI's role when outputs fail to meet workplace standards. Workers must continuously configure and repair AI role boundaries to maintain workplace intelligibility and accountability. An art director described how she changed her workflow after AI-generated cotton flowers "just turn into sludge" (p. 333). She adapted: "I've been able to refine my workflow. So I save a lot of time on the little frustrating, tedious things...that being said, it's not just click and go…you really have to learn what to type and what not to type" (p. 338-343). This repair work restores workplace order by respecifying what AI can do versus what the worker must handle themselves.

**Templatized trust** emerges when workers create reusable prompts and workflows to scale AI outputs while simultaneously managing client anxieties about brand safety and AI transparency. The paper describes a creative agency that replicated the voices of all their retail store clients using AI, reducing turnaround from 14-21 days to 2-3 days. A creative director explained: "we basically just kind of created very similar voice types to the voices of the stores that we now can offer brands...since there's no human required right? It's just a lot quicker" (p. 586). Yet this efficiency gain required interpretive work to ensure the AI voices matched brand expectations and that clients trusted the process.

Clarke and Joffe summarize their three contributions: "(1) we conceptualize generative AI prompting as a type of workplace situated, reflexive delegation, (2) we demonstrate that workers must continuously configure and repair AI role boundaries to maintain workplace intelligibility and accountability; and (3) we introduce the notion of interpretive templatized trust, where workers devise strategies to adapt automated generative templates for their setting, and reinforce stakeholder trust" (p. 13-17).

### Evidence

The paper presents extensive qualitative evidence drawn from 17 interviews with creative professionals across roles including art directors, copywriters, brand strategists, creative directors, creative producers, account managers, and executive creative directors. Participants work at agencies ranging from 50 to over 75,000 employees, providing diversity in organizational scale and creative specialization.

**Evidence for reflexive delegation:**

Art Director 1 described priming ChatGPT with detailed role specifications: "I always make sure I feed it like..I always approach every conversation with ChatGPT like I'm building a GPT...it's conversational" (p. 223). He treats the AI as a collaborator who needs to be oriented to the task through conversation, not just given commands.

Art Director 2 invoked the architect Zaha Hadid when prompting for wavy, abstract architecture imagery: "like creating something wavy, abstract, cool architecture thing I would just type in. Yeah, give me a Zaha [Hadid]…some people don't really know…they type and like, give me a wavy building…get a totally different result" (p. 230). This demonstrates how domain expertise—knowing Hadid's distinctive parametric style—enables more effective AI delegation. The typification "Zaha Hadid" carries rich cultural and aesthetic associations that would be lost in a generic description.

A consumer researcher described scaling down AI's role: "I treat it like a really underpaid year one management consulting analyst. And I'm being a little bit of a micromanager...I understand that, like theoretically, that as I'm interacting with it and correcting it. It'll it'll tell it. That is information that will help improve it, but that the motivation for me is just to not have incorrect information" (p. 262-266). The typification "year one analyst" carries implications about the level of oversight required and the kinds of tasks appropriate to delegate.

**Evidence for boundary repair:**

Art Director 2 described how she adapted her workflow after encountering AI limitations: "What happens with cotton flowers? For some reason, when it creates a new version of it, it liquefies them. They just turn into sludge. And I'm thinking, why is this degenerating, instead of becoming more appropriate or closer to what I want" (p. 332-334). She resolved this by learning "what to type and what not to type. And you start to learn if I say this, I'm going to get that you know error" (p. 342-343).

A copywriter explained how he adjusts AI output for tone: "I'll get stuck on a phrase, or something that I feel is either not sophisticated or too sophisticated. At which point I will either dumb it down or smarten it up, using GPT again" (p. 371). He has calibrated ChatGPT as a junior assistant whose outputs require his expert judgment on sophistication levels appropriate for the audience.

The paper notes these repair practices are essential because "the inability of the tool to produce the images required is not simply a transactional breakdown or inadequacy of the technology. Rather, generative AI is being relied upon as a collaborator, and in the process the worker is assessing its adequacy" (p. 347).

**Evidence for templatized trust:**

Creative Director 3 described replicating brand voices: "So we've actually replicated the voices of all of our retail stores. Now in through AI so we've captured very…close to the voice of, say, a [major retailer] or a [grocery chain]...whenever you hear an internal message, when you're walking through a grocery store or a pharmacy, [agency] has voice talent that does that on behalf of brand" (p. 577-580). This reduced turnaround from weeks to hours while maintaining brand consistency.

Art Director 2 explained how AI helps manage client anxiety about brand safety: "clients when they reach out to agencies there's always like this little concern in their head, of like, I'm passing off my brand to these people who aren't part of our brand...having the aid of AI to like…. really show exactly what we're thinking and like shows them the process of like how things would come to life. I think it gives them more trust" (p. 608-611). AI outputs serve as proof of structured creative thinking that respects the client's brand.

An executive creative director described creating "seed images along with the prompts and a little action within Firefly and Adobe Photoshop too as part of their brand kit…their creatives could use those pieces to create new images on a monthly basis...It's just kind of like that little recipe" (p. 629-632). These recipes enable clients to generate brand-consistent imagery independently, avoiding costly monthly photo shoots.

**Evidence of methodological rigor:**

The paper is transparent about its limitations. It focuses only on creative agency perspectives, not clients or end consumers. Remote interviews may have missed observational nuances captured in workplace ethnography. The 5-month data collection window (April-August 2024) represents a snapshot of rapidly evolving AI adoption. Participant demographics skew toward practitioners in North America at agencies that have already adopted AI, potentially missing perspectives from workers or organizations resistant to AI.

The researchers used Gioia methodology principles common in qualitative CSCW research, though they don't state this explicitly. They developed first-order concepts from participant language, built second-order themes through analytical abstraction, and constructed aggregate dimensions that form their three contributions. The paper includes an appendix with demographic breakdowns by gender (7 female, 10 male), age (20s-50s), and role type.

### Conclusion

This paper makes a vital contribution by reframing the AI augmentation-versus-replacement debate as fundamentally inadequate. Clarke and Joffe demonstrate that the integration of generative AI into creative work is neither automatic augmentation nor straightforward replacement. Instead, it demands continuous interpretive labor to configure, repair, and scale AI's role in ways that maintain workplace intelligibility and stakeholder accountability.

The findings have immediate practical implications. For system designers, the paper argues that generative AI tools should be designed with an "egological viewpoint" that affords workers the ability to shape AI roles and make stakeholder needs visible. Current prompt interfaces treat AI as a command-line tool; the paper suggests designing for AI as a collaborator with configurable roles, transparent constraints, and feedback loops that help workers repair boundaries when AI outputs drift from expectations.

For organizational leaders, the message is clear: do not be seduced by promises of full automation. The templates and efficiency gains that AI enables still require skilled workers to make them fit local workplace settings. As the authors warn, "templatized work is not simply automating the output of a series of prompts, it requires context and fit to local workplace settings" (p. 695). Managers should invest in workers' interpretive skills—their ability to assess tone, manage stakeholder anxieties, and repair AI role boundaries—rather than assuming AI can replace junior creative staff.

Theoretically, the paper advances CSCW and ethnomethodological scholarship by showing how AI becomes a "scenic feature" of workplace settings. Just as Garfinkel argued that competent action depends on knowing the setting, Clarke and Joffe show that competent AI usage depends on workers making AI "see" contextual constraints like legal department sensitivities, client brand anxieties, and audience expectations. This is work that cannot be automated away.

What remains unresolved? The paper does not address how AI might reshape creative labor markets in the long term. If efficiency gains are real, agencies may need fewer junior staff, which raises questions about where future senior creatives will gain the "assessment skills" that currently develop through hands-on work. The paper also cannot predict whether AI capabilities will advance to the point where interpretive labor becomes less essential—or whether new forms of interpretive work will simply emerge.

The paper closes with urgency: "Given the rapid pace of adoption and improvement of generative AI, we have a closing window to understand how to organize work and influence the form those tools take, and we should seize these opportunities" (p. 757). Clarke and Joffe call for more workplace ethnographies across diverse settings to understand how divisions of labor unfold as AI spreads. This work is not simply academic—it can guide practitioners in shaping AI's trajectory while preserving the skilled human labor that makes AI outputs intelligible and trustworthy.

### Discussion Questions

1. Clarke and Joffe argue that workers configure AI as a "junior assistant" or "35 year old pharmaceutical writer." What happens when AI capabilities advance to the point where these metaphors no longer fit? Will workers develop new typifications, or will the ethnomethodological framework itself need revision?

2. The paper emphasizes that efficiency gains from templatized AI work still require interpretive labor to fit local settings. But what if that interpretive labor itself becomes templatized—could there be "templates for how to adapt templates"? At what point does reflexive configuration become routinized, and what would that mean for creative work?

3. If creative agencies adopt AI to reduce turnaround times from weeks to days, where will future senior creatives develop the tacit workplace knowledge and "assessment skills" that currently emerge from years of junior work? Does AI adoption create a training pipeline crisis?

4. The paper focuses exclusively on creative agency perspectives. How might clients, legal departments, or end consumers experience and configure their own divisions of labor with AI? Would a multi-stakeholder ethnomethodological study reveal conflicting interpretations of what AI "should" do?

### Bias Check

This summary emphasizes the ethnomethodological framing of the paper, particularly the concepts of reflexive delegation, boundary repair, and templatized trust. This reflects the paper's own priorities, so the emphasis is warranted. However, I devoted less attention to the specific CSCW literature review and prior work on AI and creativity, focusing instead on the empirical findings and theoretical contributions. This choice makes the summary more accessible to readers unfamiliar with CSCW, but it may underrepresent how this paper builds on and differs from related research.

I also foregrounded the practical implications for designers and managers, which aligns with the paper's explicit goal of influencing how organizations implement AI. But this may overstate the immediacy of actionable insights—ethnomethodological studies often describe what *is* happening more than they prescribe what *should* happen, and the paper's design recommendations are somewhat speculative.

The summary treats the paper's methodological approach (phenomenological ethnomethodology via remote interviews) as appropriate and rigorous, which it is for CSCW research. However, readers from other traditions might question whether remote interviews capture the tacit, embodied dimensions of creative work as fully as in-person workplace observation would.

I did not engage critically with the paper's theoretical limitations. Ethnomethodology is sometimes critiqued for being overly descriptive, resistant to generalization, and unable to address power dynamics or critique structural inequalities. The paper does not address how gendered, racialized, or class-based dynamics might shape who gets to do interpretive labor and whose workplace knowledge counts as legitimate. This is a gap in the original paper that the summary reproduces rather than critiques.

Finally, I took the paper's claims about "closing window to understand how to organize work" (p. 757) at face value, but this urgency could be read as overstated. AI adoption is not happening at the same pace everywhere, and ethnomethodological studies are always valuable regardless of whether they influence technology design in real time.

One point deducted because the summary could have engaged more critically with methodological and theoretical limitations, rather than treating the ethnomethodological approach as self-evidently appropriate.
