# Making the Case for Introducing Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) into Design Curricula

## Summary
This paper surveys 74 design students from an Australian university to examine their current GenAI utilization and projections for future design practice, building a comprehensive argument for structured GenAI integration in design curricula. Findings confirm ad hoc student use primarily for speeding up ideation, tempered by skepticism about AI's creative output quality. The paper situates GenAI within design's broader history of technological disruption, argues that design educators must move proactively rather than reactively, and proposes a curriculum framework of GenAI training competencies grounded in both research evidence and student survey findings.

## Key Concepts
- **Curriculum Integration Framework**: A proposed structured list of GenAI training areas for design programs, including AI literacy, prompt engineering, critical evaluation, ethical considerations, workflow integration, and collaborative AI practices
- **Ad Hoc vs. Structured Use**: The distinction between current student practice (self-directed, irregular, without pedagogical scaffolding) and the structured integration the author advocates for
- **Professional Landscape Shift**: The argument that design industry has already adopted GenAI (Adobe Firefly, Microsoft Copilot Designer, Midjourney in professional workflows), making curriculum integration a professional preparedness obligation
- **Deskilling Risk**: The concern that GenAI may create a "second tier" of non-professional designers who can produce adequate design artifacts without craft knowledge, threatening professional design value
- **Creative Co-pilot Model**: The industry framing (Adobe's characterization of Firefly as a "co-pilot") that positions AI as amplifying rather than replacing designer expertise—a framing Fleischmann adopts for curriculum design
- **Design Profession Co-evolution**: The historical argument that design professions always co-evolve with disruptive technologies, creating new specializations (UI/UX design from the internet, prompt engineering from GenAI) rather than eliminating the field
- **AI Literacy**: A prerequisite competency encompassing understanding of how GenAI works, its limitations, biases, and ethical implications—distinct from technical proficiency with specific tools

## Theoretical Framework
The paper is structured around a historical disruption framework: the Jacquard loom, desktop publishing, and the internet each transformed design practice and education, each generated fears of replacement, and each ultimately produced new design specializations. This historicizing argument serves both to normalize current anxiety and to justify proactive curriculum response rather than reactive policy. Fleischmann draws on Auernhammer's (2020) philosophical work on human-AI interaction and Matthews et al.'s literature review of AI's impact on the design industry to frame the professional stakes.

The research design is explicitly pragmatist, treating student attitudes and projections as actionable curriculum design inputs rather than purely descriptive data.

## Methods
A survey of 74 undergraduate and graduate design students at an Australian university (Griffith University), examining: current GenAI tool use and frequency; attitudes toward GenAI's creative output; projections for GenAI's role in future professional practice; and priorities for curriculum integration. The survey combined quantitative items (frequency scales, tool inventories, attitudinal ratings) with qualitative open-ended questions. Data collection occurred against the backdrop of rapid professional GenAI adoption, grounding student projections in observable industry trends.

## Main Arguments
- GenAI is already being used by design students in an unstructured, ad hoc manner—primarily for ideation speed—with widespread skepticism about AI's quality of creative output, a productive ambivalence that structured curriculum can build upon rather than needing to create from scratch
- Design educators who do not proactively integrate GenAI risk graduating students unprepared for a profession that has already adopted these tools, creating a dangerous gap between professional practice and educational preparation
- Prompt engineering is not merely a technical skill but a new form of creative direction that requires design knowledge to execute well—"the key to success" according to industry practitioners, suggesting it belongs at the center of updated design curricula
- The ethical challenges of GenAI (copyright violations, racial bias in training data, academic integrity risks) require explicit, structured curriculum attention rather than policies that prohibit use without teaching critical engagement
- The proposed curriculum framework addresses gaps identified in both the literature and student survey data: the list includes competencies from AI literacy through workflow integration, organized around both technical and critical dimensions
- Design educators' professional obligation is to ensure students can "ethically collaborate with generative AI tools and still maintain their agency in the creative process"—agency maintenance is as important as tool proficiency

## Limitations & Critiques
The study population (74 students at one Australian university) limits generalizability across disciplines, institutions, and cultural contexts. The survey captures attitudes and intentions rather than actual behaviors or outcomes—the relationship between stated attitudes and learning needs may not be straightforward. The curriculum framework proposed is not empirically validated; it represents expert synthesis rather than evidence-based curriculum design.

This paper is closely related to Fleischmann's companion 2024 CJLT paper (Generative AI in Graphic Design Education: A Student Perspective) and builds on similar survey methodology with a larger sample. Together they represent a coordinated research program, but the findings are essentially consistent rather than independently validating.
