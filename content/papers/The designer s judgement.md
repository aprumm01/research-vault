# The designer s judgement

## Summary
The designer's judgement J Holt, Department of Mechanical Engineering, The University of Queensland, 4072, Australia Judgement is ubiquitous in engineering work but the demand for the exercise of good judgement is particularly evident in engineering design Every advance, or change of direction, in the design process is the result of the designer's judgement.

## Key Concepts
- Designer's judgement: Personal yet obligated decision-making seeking "rules of rightness"
- Three types of judgement: Reality judgements (current state), value judgements (desired state), action judgements (what to do)
- Appreciation: Readiness to see things one way rather than another
- Three design domains: Formative (style/structure), commercial (market/profit), instrumental (technical realization)
- Rules of rightness: Context-specific standards emerging from precedent and practice
- Good practice: Accumulated knowledge in design texts, codes, standards, handbooks

## Theoretical Framework
- Vickers' judgement theory: Reality, value, and action judgements through appreciation
- Engineering heritage: Cultural conditioning of acceptable forms and practices
- Evolutionary engineering progress: Building on proven practice rather than revolutionary leaps
- Mentor system: Professional development through guided practice

## Methods
Conceptual analysis drawing on Vickers' work on judgement, applied to engineering design practice

## Main Arguments
- Judgement ubiquitous in engineering, particularly in design - every advance results from designer's judgement
- Design begins with recognition of opportunity requiring reality and value judgements
- Good judgement not common sense - requires special knowledge grounded in engineering heritage
- Formative domain: Cultural fit with contemporary fashion, style, acceptable forms (visual, nonverbal thinking)
- Commercial domain: Market viability, profit potential, managing design effort vs. "good enough"
- Instrumental domain: Practical realization through proven methods and empirical recommendations
- Good judgement about precedent/heritage reinterpreted for contemporary context
- Capacity for judgement developed over time through broad professional education and experience
- Engineering education shifted from practice to science focus, diminishing attention to judgement development
- Industry mentor systems more effective at developing judgement than contemporary academe
- Need to restore balance: scholarship of application alongside discovery

## Limitations & Critiques
- Purely conceptual/theoretical paper without empirical validation
- Draws heavily on Vickers' work which may have limited direct applicability to engineering design
- Specific to engineering design context, may not generalize to other design disciplines
- Focus on mechanical engineering perspective may not apply to emerging design fields
- No systematic methodology for teaching judgement development provided
- Acknowledges good judgement takes "considerable time" but provides limited guidance on acceleration
- Mentor system effectiveness assumed but not empirically verified
- Cultural and temporal specificity (1997 Australian engineering context) may limit contemporary relevance
- Assumes engineering heritage and precedent are universally beneficial without addressing when they may constrain innovation
- Limited discussion of how judgement operates in interdisciplinary or non-traditional design contexts
- Does not address tensions between individual judgement and collaborative/distributed design processes
- Lacks consideration of how computational tools and AI might change nature of designer judgement
