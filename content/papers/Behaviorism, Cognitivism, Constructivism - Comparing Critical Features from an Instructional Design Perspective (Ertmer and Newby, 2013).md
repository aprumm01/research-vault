# Behaviorism, Cognitivism, Constructivism: Comparing Critical Features From an Instructional Design Perspective

## Summary
This foundational article compares three major learning theories from the perspective of instructional designers. Originally published in 1993 and reprinted in 2013, it addresses the gap between learning theory and instructional practice by providing designers with a structured framework for understanding how different theories inform instructional strategies.

The authors argue that instructional designers need to understand learning theories because they serve as a source of verified instructional strategies, provide foundation for intelligent strategy selection, and enable prediction of instructional effectiveness. The article examines behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism through seven key questions: How does learning occur? Which factors influence learning? What is memory's role? How does transfer occur? What types of learning are best explained? What basic principles are relevant to instructional design? How should instruction be structured?

The article traces the historical evolution from empiricism (knowledge from experience) to rationalism (knowledge from reason), showing how behaviorism emerged from the empiricist tradition while cognitivism drew from rationalist roots. Constructivism represents a more recent development that questions the objectivist assumptions of both prior theories, arguing that learners create meaning through experience rather than acquiring pre-existing knowledge.

## Key Concepts
- **Behaviorism**: Learning theory focusing on observable behavior changes through stimulus-response associations and reinforcement
- **Cognitivism**: Learning theory emphasizing internal mental processes, knowledge acquisition, and information processing
- **Constructivism**: Learning theory holding that learners actively construct meaning from experience rather than acquiring external knowledge
- **Empiricism**: Philosophical position that knowledge derives from sensory experience
- **Rationalism**: Philosophical position that knowledge derives from reason and mental structures

## Theoretical Framework
The article provides a comparative framework examining learning theories through the lens of instructional design practice. It uses seven diagnostic questions derived from Schunk (1991) to systematically compare theories on dimensions relevant to designers.

## Methods
Conceptual/comparative analysis of learning theory literature, synthesizing key features of each theoretical perspective as they relate to instructional design practice.

## Main Arguments
- Learning theories provide instructional designers with strategies, selection criteria, and predictive power
- Behaviorism is most useful for teaching facts, conventions, and basic skills through reinforcement and practice
- Cognitivism is most applicable for teaching reasoning, problem-solving, and conceptual knowledge through cognitive strategies
- Constructivism is most relevant for ill-structured domains requiring learners to construct personal meaning
- As designers move along the behaviorist-cognitivist-constructivist continuum, instruction shifts from teaching to facilitating learning
- Different theories are not mutually exclusive; designers should match theory to learning goals and context

## Limitations & Critiques
- Less than 2% of educational technology courses emphasize theory as a key concept
- The article acknowledges that theories have similarities despite their differences
- The comparative framework may oversimplify complex theoretical nuances
- The article was written before the widespread adoption of digital learning technologies
- Constructivism's emphasis on learner-constructed knowledge may be challenging to implement in structured educational settings
