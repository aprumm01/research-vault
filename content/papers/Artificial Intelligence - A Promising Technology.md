# Artificial Intelligence: A "Promising Technology"

## Summary
This paper examines the historical development of artificial intelligence from a social science perspective, focusing particularly on AI development in Germany since the 1970s. Hirsch-Kreinsen challenges the technology-centric explanation that AI's trajectory is driven primarily by advances in information technology. Instead, he argues that AI should be understood as a "promising technology" whose development has been fundamentally shaped by technological promises about its potential to solve economic and societal challenges.

The author identifies four distinct stages of AI development: (1) the 1970s scientific niche phase, (2) the commercial dawning of the 1980s, (3) the crisis at the end of the 1980s ("AI winter"), and (4) consolidation through the 2000s, leading to the current boom driven by deep learning and machine learning. Throughout these phases, technological promises made by the AI community have played a crucial role in mobilizing resources, attracting policy support, and sustaining interest even during periods of limited practical success.

The paper concludes by analyzing the rhetoric of the technological promise, identifying mechanisms such as ambiguous metaphors, claims of inevitability, accelerated growth narratives, and generalizations of individual success cases that make AI promises persuasive to policymakers and the public.

## Key Concepts
- **Promising Technology**: A concept from social science technology research where actors are guided by initially general technological promises that open up innovation and research paths, justify expectations, and mobilize resources
- **AI Winter**: Periods of reduced funding and interest in AI following disappointed expectations, occurring notably at the end of the 1960s and 1980s
- **Technological Promise**: A narrative that offers visions, opens innovation paths, and justifies expectations while remaining credible and linked to current technological potential
- **Deep Learning**: The technological basis for the current AI boom, involving artificial neural networks and machine learning methods with large data availability
- **Socio-technical Field**: The institutionalized network of actors including science, business, and policy that emerges around AI development

## Theoretical Framework
The paper employs the concept of "promising technology" from social science technology research (van Lente and Rip, 1998; Bender, 2005; Borup et al., 2006). This framework posits that:
- Expectations about new technologies strongly influence innovation trajectories
- Technological promises reduce uncertainty and open concrete development paths
- Promises must be credible and linked to current research while highlighting future potential
- A reciprocally reinforcing process develops between promise concretization and technological development

## Methods
The methodological basis consists of:
1. Qualitative analysis of "grey" documents, preprints, policy statements, and specialist publications from national and international contexts
2. Reinterpretation of the author's existing research on digitalization
3. Analysis of 19 interviews with AI experts in business and academia conducted between October 2021 and February 2022

## Main Arguments
- AI's development cannot be explained solely by technological advances; social and societal factors are equally important
- Technological promises have sustained AI development through multiple "boom and bust" cycles
- The AI community, coordinated through organizations like Germany's acatech, plays a central role in formulating and renewing promises
- Policy support has been crucial, with AI framed as a "key enabling technology" for economic competitiveness
- Current challenges include knowledge transfer difficulties, limited performance of machine learning, data quality issues, and the need for explainability
- The technological promise of AI has the character of an "unquestionable technological utopia" presenting AI as the means to overcome societal challenges
- Promises are made persuasive through ambiguous metaphors, inevitability claims, accelerated growth narratives, and generalization of individual cases

## Limitations & Critiques
- The study focuses primarily on Germany, which may limit generalizability to other national contexts
- Current AI limitations include: poor handling of "open worlds" and unpredictable situations, difficulty incorporating everyday knowledge, insufficient data quality for many applications, and lack of explainability
- Some experts interviewed suggest the common shortcut of equating AI with machine learning may be a mistake
- The paper acknowledges debates about whether strong AI or artificial general intelligence can ever be achieved
- There is tension between optimistic promises and critical assessments from both technical and philosophical perspectives (Dreyfus, Weizenbaum)
