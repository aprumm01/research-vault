# Evaluating LLMs in Generating Synthetic HCI Research Data - Hamalainen et al - 2023

## Overview of the Document

This paper represents empirical research published in the proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems, one of the premier venues for HCI scholarship. The authors Perttu Hämäläinen and Mikke Tavast are affiliated with Aalto University in Espoo, Finland, while Anton Kunnari works at the University of Helsinki. Their research explores the intersection of artificial intelligence, computational user modeling, and HCI research methodology. The document is a case study examining whether large language models, specifically GPT-3, can generate synthetic research data that resembles real human responses to open-ended questionnaires. This work matters because collecting high-quality user research data is time-intensive and expensive, creating a bottleneck in HCI research. If LLMs can produce believable synthetic data for piloting studies or exploring research questions, they could accelerate early-stage research, although any findings would still require validation with real participants.

The paper arrives at a pivotal moment when generative AI capabilities are rapidly expanding but their application to HCI research remains largely unexplored. The authors position their work within computational user modeling, a tradition in HCI that typically focuses on behavioral measures like typing speed or interaction gestures. By contrast, this research pushes into subjective territory: user experiences, emotions, and aesthetic responses. The case study focuses on a specific domain, art experiences in video games, chosen because it represents fundamentally human subjective experience that would challenge any computational approach. The authors compare GPT-3 generated responses to a published dataset from Bopp et al. about experiencing games as art, ensuring the real data was not included in GPT-3’s training corpus.

## Research Overview

The central research question asks whether GPT-3 can generate synthetic HCI research data that is indistinguishable from real human responses and whether such data can provide plausible answers to actual research questions. The study was conducted entirely by the three named authors using a multi-experiment design. Their first experiment recruited 155 participants via the Prolific crowdsourcing platform to evaluate whether people could distinguish between GPT-3 generated text and real human responses. As the authors state, "GPT-3 can, in this context, yield believable accounts of HCI experiences" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 1).

The researchers used three complementary methods across their experiments. Experiment 1 employed quantitative methods through an online study where participants classified 20 text passages as either human-written or AI-generated. Experiment 2 conducted qualitative error analysis of 200 GPT-3 responses, identifying distinct failure modes and anomaly types. Experiment 3 deployed computational analysis methods including automatic qualitative coding using GPT-3 itself, semantic embedding vectors, dimensionality reduction via UMAP, and clustering with HDBSCAN to compare topic distributions between synthetic and real data.

The authors explain their prompt engineering approach: "The prompts were formulated as a partial in silico replication of Bopp et al., including questions directly from the study preceded by some additional context" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 375). They generated synthetic interview data by prompting GPT-3 to role-play as a study participant answering questions about experiencing video games as art. The research team used OpenAI’s public API with multiple GPT-3 model variants (ada, babbage, curie, davinci, and text-davinci-002) to assess how model size affects data quality. They implemented automatic filtering heuristics to remove obviously flawed responses, such as those containing repetitive text or falling below minimum word counts.

## Theories of Knowledge

The research draws primarily on signal detection theory (SDT), a psychophysical framework for measuring sensitivity and bias in detection tasks. The authors explain: "From an SDT point of view, the current experiment can be considered as a Yes-No Experiment, where the participants’ ability to distinguish between two categories of stimuli is measured" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 546). Signal detection theory provides the mathematical foundation for analyzing how well participants discriminated between human and AI text through measures of d’ (discriminability) and c (response bias). This theoretical lens allows the researchers to move beyond simple accuracy scores to understand both perceptual sensitivity and decision-making biases.

The paper also engages with computational learning theory, particularly the concept of next-token prediction as the learning objective for language models. The authors cite recent theoretical work explaining how language models might implicitly learn a wide range of tasks through this objective (Sanh et al., 2022). They reference mathematical frameworks for understanding in-context learning, the phenomenon where models can operate on information provided in prompts rather than only on training data (Olsson et al., 2022). The Transformer architecture underlying GPT-3 is described as Turing-complete in its hard-attention variant (Pérez et al., 2021), providing theoretical justification for the model’s computational expressiveness.

From HCI theory, the paper draws on traditions of computational user modeling, citing Oulasvirta’s (2019) call for rediscovering computational models in HCI research. The authors note that previous computational user models in HCI focused on objective behavioral measures like Fitts’ law for motor control or models predicting touchscreen typing behavior. Their work extends this tradition into subjective experience, which represents new theoretical territory. They invoke the concept of latent variables from psychological modeling, suggesting that language models might learn to represent psychological constructs like emotion, intent, and style because these variables influence large portions of training data.

The research also engages with information theory through its discussion of language modeling as conditional probability distribution over tokens. They explain: "Text generation is approached statistically as sampling each token conditional on previous tokens" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 174), situating their work within the information-theoretic tradition of treating language as a statistical process.

## Central Arguments

The paper’s central argument is that GPT-3 can generate synthetic HCI research data that is not only superficially human-like but also substantively useful for initial research exploration and pilot testing, albeit with important limitations that require validation with real participants. The authors build this argument across three complementary experiments, each addressing a different aspect of data quality.

Their first major claim, established through Experiment 1, is that GPT-3 responses are difficult to distinguish from real human responses. Surprisingly, they found that "GPT-3 texts were deemed more human-like based on the d’ values" with participants showing "more false alarms (i.e. GPT-3 text are written by humans) than correct hits (i.e. human texts are written by humans)" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 619). This finding exceeded the authors’ expectations and suggests that for this context, synthetic data can achieve behavioral verisimilitude. They note that participants frequently used emotional content as a criterion for identifying human writing, observing that "54.19% of the responses to the question ‘What made you consider an answer as written by a human?’ contained either the string ‘emotion’ or the string ‘feeling’" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 652).

The second major argument, developed through Experiment 2’s qualitative analysis, is that GPT-3 produces identifiable error patterns that fall into distinct categories. The authors categorized errors into eight types plus an "other" category, finding that validity rates varied significantly based on prompt design. They state: "For the default prompt, the three annotators considered 54%, 62%, and 76% as valid responses (mean 64%). For the more specific prompt ending with ‘I’m thinking of the game’ 64%, 79%, and 94% were considered as valid (mean 79%)" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 696). This demonstrates that while GPT-3 makes errors, many are detectable and some can be mitigated through improved prompting. They identify particularly problematic errors like imaginary gameplay descriptions that are "well-written and coherent, but describe imaginary gameplay" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 706), requiring domain knowledge to detect.

The third major argument, supported by Experiment 3’s computational analyses, is that synthetic data discusses largely similar topics to real data but with reduced diversity. The authors found that "highly similar groups/topics emerge from both real and GPT-3 data" including aspects of story and music as top-frequency groups in both datasets (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 1065). However, they also discovered significant diversity problems, particularly with the newer text-davinci-002 model where "151 out of 178 answers discuss Journey (only 7 mentions in the real data)" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 1106). They conclude that "only 17.3% of games in the human data are mentioned in GPT-3 davinci data" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 1078), suggesting synthetic data has narrower coverage.

The authors also argue that larger models produce more human-like data, observing that "human-likeness grows with model size from ada to davinci" based on Frechet distance and precision-recall metrics (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 1094). This scaling relationship suggests that future, more capable models may address current limitations.

## Evidence

The distinguishability study (Experiment 1) provides quantitative evidence through a between-subjects online experiment with 155 participants after exclusions for careless responding. Participants evaluated 20 text passages each, split evenly between GPT-3 generations and real human responses from the Bopp et al. dataset. The research team implemented rigorous quality control, excluding participants based on implausibly fast reading speeds (over 664 words per minute) and shallow responses to open-ended reflection questions. The authors report: "On aggregate, human written texts were correctly recognized 54.45% of the time, with 95% confidence interval excluding the chance level of 50% (95% CI: 51.97%-56.93%). The average accuracy of recognizing GPT-3 generated texts as AI-written was below chance level 40.45% (95% CI: 38.01%-42.89%)" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 582).

Signal detection analysis revealed a negative d’ value of -0.15, indicating participants were more likely to incorrectly classify GPT-3 text as human than to correctly identify human text as human. The statistical test showed this effect was significant: "t(154) = -2.52, p = 0.013, d = -0.2" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 618). Participants also showed liberal response bias with "c = -0.2, with participant bias values differing significantly from zero in a one-sample t-test (t(154) = -7.74, p < 0.001)" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 613), meaning they were predisposed to classify texts as human-written. This evidence directly supports the claim that GPT-3 generates highly believable synthetic data, though the study’s limitation to native and fluent English speakers on Prolific means results may not generalize to laboratory settings or different participant populations.

The error analysis (Experiment 2) provides qualitative evidence through systematic coding of 200 generated responses by three independent annotators. The most common error categories included: clearly too long or rambling incoherently (27.5% of anomalies), not answering or dodging the question (20.5%), incomplete answers ending before the point (15.2%), and imaginary game or gameplay descriptions (7.6%). The authors provide concrete examples, such as a response about Journey that describes "taking care of a baby" when "there is no baby to take care of in the real game" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 842). Inter-annotator agreement on validity judgments varied considerably, with the three annotators classifying between 54% to 76% of default-prompt responses as valid, suggesting that error detection involves subjective judgment.

This qualitative evidence reveals important limitations in current synthetic data quality. Some errors like dodging questions are easily detectable, while others like plausible-sounding but factually incorrect gameplay descriptions require domain expertise to catch. The significant improvement in validity rates from better prompting (64% to 79% mean validity) demonstrates that prompt engineering can partially mitigate quality issues, though perfect reliability remains elusive.

Experiment 3 provides computational evidence through multiple quantitative analyses. The researchers generated 178 complete synthetic interviews to match the size of the human dataset, using five different GPT-3 model variants. They developed a novel automatic coding procedure where GPT-3 itself extracted qualitative codes from responses, which were then embedded as high-dimensional vectors, dimensionality-reduced, and clustered to identify thematic groups. The Frechet distance metric showed synthetic data quality improving with model size, while precision and recall metrics revealed text-davinci-002 achieved high precision (0.8) but low recall (0.4), compared to davinci’s more balanced performance.

Game frequency analysis provided striking evidence of diversity limitations. The authors report: "The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild was mentioned 10 times in human data but only appeared in the ‘and 65 other games’ category for davinci. Journey was mentioned 7 times in human data but 44 times in GPT-3 davinci data, and 151 times in text-davinci-002 data" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 1230). This demonstrates that while synthetic data can discuss appropriate topics, it exhibits strong mode collapse, repeatedly generating similar content rather than capturing the full diversity of human responses.

Consistency analysis using cosine similarity of text embeddings showed that synthetic data maintained reasonable internal coherence. The authors found that for all model variants and human data, intra-participant similarity (between a participant’s response to different questions) exceeded inter-participant similarity (between random pairs), indicating responses from the same synthetic participant cohered more than random pairings. However, GPT-3 similarities were somewhat higher than human similarities, which the authors interpret as another signal of reduced diversity.

The primary methodological limitations include the narrow focus on a single research domain (art experiences in video games), reliance on a single LLM family (GPT-3 variants), and sensitivity to prompt wording. The authors acknowledge: "We only examined one HCI context: art experiences in video games, thus the generalizability of our results is unclear" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 1489). Additionally, their automatic coding approach using GPT-3 itself introduces potential circularity, as the same model family evaluates its own outputs, though the authors argue this ensures consistent biases across compared datasets. The sample for Experiment 1 included non-native English speakers and used online crowdsourcing with incentives favoring fast responses, potentially affecting distinguishability scores compared to laboratory conditions.

## Conclusion

This research demonstrates that large language models have reached a threshold where they can generate synthetic HCI research data that appears remarkably human-like to evaluators and discusses substantially similar topics to real human responses. The findings should matter to researchers six months from now because they establish both the potential and the boundaries of using LLMs for early-stage research exploration. The key takeaway is that synthetic data from GPT-3 can be useful for ideating research questions, piloting data analysis pipelines, and exploring hypothetical scenarios, but it exhibits reduced diversity compared to real data and requires domain knowledge to detect subtle factual errors.

The work contributes methodologically by providing a three-pronged evaluation framework combining human distinguishability studies, qualitative error analysis, and computational content comparison. This template can be applied to evaluate synthetic data quality in other domains. The automatic coding approach using GPT-3 to extract qualitative codes from responses, followed by embedding, dimensionality reduction, and clustering, represents a novel method for comparing large text datasets that could prove valuable beyond this specific application.

What remains open is the generalizability of these findings to other research contexts. The authors studied subjective aesthetic experiences in video games, a domain where personal emotional responses dominate. How well do these results transfer to usability testing, information-seeking behavior, or decision-making studies where factual accuracy matters more? The paper also raises but does not resolve the question of whether synthetic data could be useful for understanding demographic differences in user experiences, noting they observed GPT-3 adapting output based on age and gender in prompts but not systematically investigating this phenomenon.

The ethical implications remain partially unresolved as well. The authors warn that "if employed by malicious users of crowdsourcing services, LLMs may make crowdsourcing of self-report data fundamentally unreliable" (Hämäläinen et al., 2023, p. 32), but they do not propose concrete solutions beyond noting that detection may become increasingly difficult as models improve. This poses a genuine threat to online research methodologies that the HCI community will need to address through new validation approaches or shifts toward laboratory-based data collection.

Looking forward, the paper suggests that as language models continue scaling, their capacity to generate diverse, accurate, and contextually appropriate synthetic data will improve. The finding that larger models produce more human-like data supports this trajectory. However, the diversity problem observed with text-davinci-002 suggests that model improvements focused solely on average sample quality through feedback-based finetuning may come at the cost of reduced diversity, creating new challenges even as old ones are solved. The field needs research on how to maintain diversity while improving quality, possibly through techniques that explicitly encourage varied responses.

## Discussion Questions

1. If synthetic data from large language models becomes indistinguishable from real human responses, what implications does this have for the epistemological foundations of user research in HCI? Can we maintain confidence in findings from online crowdsourced studies, or does this necessitate a return to more controlled laboratory environments?

2. The authors found that synthetic data exhibits less diversity than real data, particularly with newer models like text-davinci-002 that have been trained with human feedback. How might this bias toward "average" or "expected" responses systematically distort our understanding of user populations if researchers increasingly rely on synthetic data for piloting studies?

3. The paper demonstrates that participants frequently used emotional content as a criterion for identifying human-written text, yet GPT-3 successfully generated emotional responses that fooled evaluators. What does this reveal about common beliefs regarding the boundaries between human and machine capabilities, and how might these beliefs evolve as generative AI becomes more prevalent?

4. Consider the proposed use case of synthetic data for exploring research questions before investing in real participant recruitment. What safeguards or methodological standards should the HCI community establish to prevent researchers from over-relying on synthetic data findings or inadvertently allowing synthetic data biases to shape research agendas?
