# Design Capability in Social Innovation: A Synthesis of Professional Skills and Design Attitude

## Summary

This paper investigates the capabilities designers need when engaging with social innovation practices, arguing that traditional education-based professional design skills are insufficient for tackling complex social challenges. Through a case study of Nice Commune, a social innovation organization in Shanghai, China, the researchers explore how design capabilities in social innovation contexts represent a synthesis of professional skills and what they term "design attitude" - the implicit and generative capabilities that emerge through practice rather than formal training.

The study employs Buchanan's "four orders of design" framework to analyze how design teams contribute across different levels: symbols and images (first order), physical objects (second order), activities and services (third order), and complex systems and environments (fourth order). The findings reveal that in social innovation practice, designers no longer limit their work to single disciplines but view design objects more systematically. Traditional skills like prototyping and sketching become integrated into holistic consideration of design objects rather than being emphasized individually.

The research identifies three main categories of design attitude: capabilities developed through interactions with various stakeholders (valuing communication, empathy, being present), systems thinking (ambiguity tolerance, holistic view, connecting multiple perspectives, knowledge sharing), and design expertise (creativity, engagement with aesthetics). These dimensions are interrelated and formed through continuous interaction and reflection with others and design objects.

## Key Concepts

- **Design Attitude**: A set of diverse abilities that designers apply when engaging in design or social innovation projects, encompassing skills, capacities, and aptitudes that emerge through practice
- **Four Orders of Design**: Buchanan's framework categorizing design objects into symbols/images, physical objects, activities/services, and systems/environments
- **Social Innovation**: New ideas concerning products, services, and models that effectively meet social needs and foster creation of new social relationships
- **Ambiguity Tolerance**: The designer's capacity to handle uncertainties and navigate complex, evolving situations while granting collaborators freedom
- **Holistic View**: A mode of thought requiring designers to approach problems comprehensively and systematically, attending to the entirety of the system

## Theoretical Framework

The paper builds on Buchanan's four orders of design theory, Cross's "designerly ways of knowing," and Michlewski and Amatullo's conceptualizations of design attitude. It positions design capability as comprising both explicit professional skills acquired through education and tacit knowledge developed through engagement with complex social challenges. The framework emphasizes that design attitude is gradually developed and mastered as designers confront uncertain situations.

## Methods

Single case study using mixed qualitative methods:
- Online workshop with 5 design team members using Buchanan's four orders of design framework
- Semi-structured in-depth interviews with 18 key stakeholders (6 team members, 3 participants, 9 co-creators)
- Thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's approach
- Grounded theory coding (open, axial, and selective coding)
- First author participation observation as design team member and activity participant

## Main Arguments

- Design skills acquired through traditional educational settings are insufficient for addressing challenges in social innovation
- Design capability in social innovation is a synthesis of professional skills and design attitude
- Design attitude includes three main dimensions: interacting with stakeholders, systems thinking, and employing design expertise
- Designers in social innovation must possess less visible abilities deeply intertwined with complex social processes
- Traditional design skills like prototyping become embedded within synergistic functioning at the systems level rather than being standalone capabilities
- Professional skills and design attitudes integrate in practice to address challenges encountered in social innovation

## Limitations & Critiques

The study has several acknowledged limitations: it is a single-case study within one social innovation organization, which may limit generalizability. The grounded theory approach relies on researcher interpretation and carries subjective bias. The study primarily focuses on design attitudes emerging from practice rather than deeply exploring their relationship with professional design skills. Future research should explore different social innovation contexts and further investigate how professional skills and design attitudes interact.
