# Instructional Design and Creativity: A Response to the Critics

## Summary
In this conceptual essay, Walter Dick responds to critics from the Situated Cognition/Constructivist/Anchored Instruction movement who argue that systematic instructional design (ISD) produces boring instruction. Writing from his position as a leading instructional design theorist and co-author of the Dick and Carey model, Dick examines the historical roots of ISD, analyzes whether the design process itself leads to boring outcomes, and proposes conditions under which creative instruction can be produced within systematic approaches.

Dick traces ISD's origins to Skinnerian programmed instruction, which despite its behavioral foundations provided personalized learning at individual paces. He acknowledges that programmed instruction "died" due to student boredom with small steps and forced writing, but argues that the systematic approach evolved significantly through incorporation of Robert Mager's work on objectives, Robert Glaser's criterion-referenced testing, and especially Robert Gagné's events of instruction. The author contends that boring instruction can result from any design approach, not specifically from systematic methods.

The essay identifies four major factors enabling creative instruction within ISD: client criteria demanding engaging instruction, climate supportive of creative approaches, participatory design involving learners and instructors, and technology implementation. Dick argues that ISD and Constructivism are compatible, and that designers who augment systematic methods with judicious use of Constructivist principles will create instruction that is both engaging and produces required learning outcomes.

## Key Concepts
- **Instructional Systems Design (ISD)**: A systematic approach to designing instruction involving analysis, strategy selection, development, and evaluation, often represented as the Dick and Carey model
- **Effectiveness and Efficiency**: Traditional ISD evaluation criteria focused on whether learners achieve objectives (effectiveness) and do so in minimal time (efficiency)
- **Creativity Criterion**: A proposed third evaluation criterion asking whether instruction engages learners and goes beyond their expectations
- **Formative Evaluation**: The process of testing instruction with learners and revising based on feedback, which Dick identifies as a key area for addressing creativity

## Theoretical Framework
Dick draws on the historical evolution of instructional design from behavioral psychology (Skinner) through cognitive psychology (Gagné) to emerging constructivist perspectives (Bednar, Cunningham, Duffy, Perry). He positions his argument within the ongoing debate between systematic/objectivist and situated/constructivist approaches to learning design, ultimately advocating for an integrative position that maintains systematic rigor while incorporating constructivist insights.

## Methods
This is a conceptual/theoretical article rather than an empirical study. Dick employs:
- Historical analysis of ISD development
- Survey of four Masters students at Florida State University about creative instruction
- Logical analysis responding to critics' claims
- Reference to the quality movement in business as an analogy for client-centered design

## Main Arguments
- Boring instruction is not unique to systematic design; it can result from any approach including teacher-led, TV-based, or computer-based instruction
- The ISD process provides significant freedom in how content is organized, what activities are used, and what strategies are employed - there is no evidence designers blindly follow a linear model
- Three areas of the ISD process are critical to creative outcomes: (1) learner analysis to understand and match instruction to the target population; (2) instructional strategy choices about content organization and practice activities; (3) formative evaluation questions asking learners about interest and engagement
- The quality movement principle that "the customer is always right" applies to instruction - clients may prioritize creativity alongside or above effectiveness and efficiency
- Four conditions support creative instruction: client criteria, supportive climate, participatory design with learners, and appropriate technology implementation
- ISD and Constructivism can coexist; designers should balance systematic fundamentals with selected constructivist principles focused on relevance, context, and problem-solving

## Limitations & Critiques
- The essay is conceptual and opinion-based rather than empirically supported with data on learning outcomes
- The informal survey of four Masters students is anecdotal and not generalizable
- Dick acknowledges he cannot present data proving that systematic designers produce creative instruction
- The integration of Constructivist principles is suggested but not operationalized or tested
- Written in 1995, the essay predates digital technologies and AI that have transformed instructional design possibilities
- The defense of ISD comes from its co-creator, presenting potential bias in evaluating criticisms
- The article doesn't fully address Constructivist concerns about decontextualization and transfer of learning
