# Future Imaginaries in the Making and Governing of Digital Technology: Multiple, Contested, Commodified (Mager & Katzenbach, 2021)

## Summary
Astrid Mager (Austrian Academy of Sciences) and Christian Katzenbach (HIIG Berlin) introduce a special issue of New Media & Society (Vol. 23, No. 2, pp. 223-236) arguing that sociotechnical imaginaries (SIs) are better understood as multiple, contested, and commodified rather than as the monolithic, state-actor-driven visions in Jasanoff's original framing. Drawing on 11 case study papers spanning corporate imaginaries, global-to-local translation, and counter-imaginaries, Mager and Katzenbach conclude that technology companies have supplanted state actors as the primary agents of dominant imaginaries -- taking over not only the imaginative work of shaping future society but also the governing capacity of public institutions, through their rhetoric, products, and expanding infrastructures.

## Key Concepts
- SIs are multiple: competing imaginaries co-exist simultaneously; the monolithic picture of one national imaginary is the exception not the rule
- SIs are contested: actors with differing resources compete to translate their visions into dominant imaginaries; contestation and counter-imaginaries are normal, not aberrant
- SIs are commodified: corporate actors (not state actors) increasingly drive dominant digital technology imaginaries by embedding utopian visions in product narratives
- Corporate SI absorption: tech companies "partly absorb public institutions' ability to govern these very futures with their rhetoric, technologies, and business models"
- Making as governing: "The making and governing of digital technology are not two separate spaces" -- governance of digital tech happens largely through the act of building it
- Counter-imaginaries: grassroots and activist visions exist but struggle to proliferate against hegemonic corporate narratives

## Theoretical Framework
Sociotechnical imaginaries (Jasanoff & Kim 2009, 2015) -- particularly broadens beyond state-actor focus; sociology of futures (van Lente); contested futures (Brown et al. 2000); social imaginary (Appadurai, Taylor, Flichy); algorithmic imaginaries (Mager 2017); Internet governance scholarship; feminist speculative practice; published as introduction to New Media & Society special issue 2021, Vol. 23(2).

## Methods
Special issue introduction and synthesis: reviews and situates 11 empirical papers using diverse methods (ethnography, discourse analysis, platform data analysis, participatory intervention, qualitative content analysis of heterogeneous documents); provides theoretical framework and organizes papers along a scale from corporate visions through global-to-local translation to counter-imaginaries; authors draw on their own prior empirical work on search engine policy and GDPR.

## Main Arguments
- The dominant concept of SIs as state-actor-driven and monolithic requires extension: corporate actors, civil society, and research communities also produce and enact imaginaries in processes far more complex than a state-nation model captures -- and Jasanoff herself (2015) acknowledges this need for refinement
- SIs are multiple and contested: competing imaginaries circulate at different scales (corporate, national, local, grassroots) with unequal power, not as a single national vision
- Commodification is now the dominant dynamic in digital technology imaginaries: tech companies embed future visions in products and infrastructures, effectively governing society while claiming only to be building tools -- a form of "governance through making"
- Counter-imaginaries are real but structurally disadvantaged: despite creative grassroots alternatives, it is "hard for participants to cede control to external, often corporate stakeholders" (Markham's findings), making the task of enabling genuinely alternative futures a political as much as a technological problem

## Limitations & Critiques
Special issue introduction rather than standalone empirical study; the theoretical frame is primarily derived from European and North American cases; the commodification argument does not fully account for state-capitalist contexts like China's AI imaginary where state and corporate actors are deeply intertwined; the three-part frame (multiple/contested/commodified) risks becoming a list of properties rather than a causal account; short piece (14 pages).
