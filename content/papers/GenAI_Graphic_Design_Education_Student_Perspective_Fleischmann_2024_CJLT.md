# Generative Artificial Intelligence in Graphic Design Education: A Student Perspective

## Summary
This study surveys 17 graphic design students at an Australian university to explore their attitudes toward GenAI, frequency of use, and perceptions of its impact on their future careers. The findings reveal a pragmatic acceptance that GenAI will fundamentally change how design is practiced, combined with a willingness to learn to use it effectively and ethically. The paper argues that design educators must adopt a "critical engagement" approach to GenAI integration, helping students understand both its affordances and ethical challenges while maintaining creative agency.

## Key Concepts
- **Critical Engagement**: The pedagogical approach proposed by Fleischmann—using critical analysis as the entry point for introducing GenAI in studio courses to ensure students understand strengths, weaknesses, and ethical dimensions
- **Pragmatic Acceptance**: The dominant student attitude identified—neither uncritical enthusiasm nor technophobia, but practical recognition that GenAI will change professional practice and a desire to develop competence with it
- **Prompt Engineering**: Identified as a new design skill emerging from GenAI practice, analogous to how desktop publishing and the internet created new design specializations in earlier technological eras
- **Design Studio Pedagogy**: The traditional hands-on, iterative model of design education that GenAI disrupts by automating tasks previously requiring extensive practice
- **AI Design Training Curriculum**: A proposed list of GenAI competencies for integration into design programs, derived from both survey findings and existing literature
- **Curator vs. Maker**: A conceptual shift in the designer's role—from hands-on maker of artifacts to director and curator of AI-generated content
- **Mechanophobia**: Historical fear of machines that provides context for current anxieties about AI displacing designers, helping students locate current disruption within a longer historical arc

## Theoretical Framework
The paper frames GenAI within a historical trajectory of technology-driven disruption in design practice—from the Jacquard loom to desktop publishing to the internet—arguing that design professions always co-evolve with disruptive technologies rather than being replaced by them. This historicizing move contextualizes student anxiety and provides a basis for pragmatic adaptation rather than resistance.

Fleischmann draws on the concept of mechanophobia (fear of machines originating in the Industrial Revolution) to normalize current anxieties while arguing for a proactive, critical engagement approach. The paper also references Auernhammer's (2020) philosophical work on human-AI interaction as a starting point for thinking about "delightful" machine-human relationships that center human values.

## Methods
A mixed-methods survey of 17 undergraduate graphic design students at Griffith University, Australia. The survey included both quantitative items (frequency of GenAI use, tool preferences, attitudes) and qualitative open-ended responses about perceptions and future projections. Though small, the cohort was selected as a representative sample from a single design program. Data collection occurred in the context of expanding professional GenAI adoption in the graphic design industry.

## Main Arguments
- Students demonstrate pragmatic acceptance of GenAI as a transformative force in design practice, neither resisting it nor uncritically embracing it—this middle position suggests readiness for structured critical engagement rather than prohibition
- GenAI is being used by students in an ad hoc, self-directed manner primarily to speed up ideation, but with a "sceptical view of its creative output," indicating students have intuitions about quality gaps that educators can build on
- Design educators risk falling behind unless they proactively develop structured GenAI curriculum, just as previous technological disruptions (desktop publishing, web) required educators to integrate new skills
- Prompt engineering represents a genuinely new design competency—not merely a technical skill but a form of creative direction that requires design knowledge to execute well
- The ethical challenges of GenAI (copyright, racial bias in training data, academic integrity) require explicit curriculum attention rather than reactive policy responses
- A proposed list of GenAI training areas for design curricula includes: AI literacy, prompt engineering, critical evaluation of AI outputs, ethical considerations, and workflow integration

## Limitations & Critiques
The extremely small sample (n=17) from a single Australian institution severely limits generalizability. The snapshot design cannot capture attitudinal change over time as students gain more experience with GenAI. The paper is primarily descriptive and advocacy-oriented, offering curriculum recommendations without empirical validation of their effectiveness.

The study predates the explosion of multimodal AI tools (e.g., image generation, video generation) that have rapidly changed the GenAI landscape, potentially limiting the current relevance of specific tool recommendations. The "critical engagement" pedagogical approach is proposed but not operationalized with sufficient detail for direct implementation.
