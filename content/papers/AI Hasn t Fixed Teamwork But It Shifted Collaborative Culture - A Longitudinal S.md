# AI Hasn t Fixed Teamwork, But It Shifted Collaborative Culture - A Longitudinal Study in a Project-Based Software Development Organization

## Summary
Xiao et al. present a two-year qualitative longitudinal interview study (2023–2025, N=15, 10 re-interviewed) examining how distributed project-based software development teams envisioned and experienced AI in teamwork. In 2023, participants imagined AI as intelligent coordinators that could surface accountability issues, track progress, and mediate interpersonal frictions. By 2025, AI was used primarily to accelerate individual tasks (coding, writing, documentation) via ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot, leaving persistent collaboration challenges of performance accountability and fragile communication unresolved. Yet AI reshaped collaborative culture: efficiency became normative, transparent and responsible use emerged as markers of professionalism, and AI was normalized as integral to teamwork despite not fixing core team dynamics.

## Key Concepts
- Sociotechnical imaginaries: Collectively held visions of AI as ambient monitor and social mediator projected onto future technology
- Domestication of technology: Transition from disruptive promises to normalized, individualized routines through appropriation, incorporation, and conversion
- Performance accountability challenges: Difficulty detecting underperformance until disruptive in distributed, asynchronous teams
- Communication breakdown patterns: Fragmented updates, misaligned priorities, lack of shared visibility in remote collaboration
- Techno-performative culture: Cultural shift where AI-augmented efficiency sets pace/tone and responsible use defines credibility
- Individual vs collective AI adoption: Gap between personal productivity tools and collaborative systems supporting group awareness
- Diagnostic triangulation: AI synthesizing fragmented data (chat, commits, milestones) into holistic team profiles
- Relational nature of teamwork: Empathy, negotiation, trust-building as deeply human processes beyond AI foundational architecture
- Role boundary expansion: AI enabling non-technical roles to perform technical tasks, raising expectations across team positions

## Theoretical Framework
Draws on sociotechnical imaginaries theory (Jasanoff & Kim) positioning technology as carrier of collective visions for desirable futures shaped by professional identities and practical challenges. Applies domestication theory (originally media studies) through appropriation-objectification-incorporation-conversion phases to trace AI's transition from hype to routinized practice. Builds on Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) critiques, arguing organizational AI adoption is interdependent rather than individual dyadic trust. Engages CSCW literature on group support systems, distributed collaboration fragility, and algorithmic management concerns. Positions study as response to Stanford HAI/Google DeepMind calls for organizational model reinvention alongside AI tool evolution.

## Methods
Qualitative longitudinal interview study with 15 members of remote-first, distributed project-based software development organization. Phase 1 (Feb-Mar 2023): Semi-structured interviews (60-90 min, Zoom, audio recorded) shortly after ChatGPT release; explored teamwork frictions, AI familiarity with predictive/generative systems, and forward-looking speculation on AI roles; reflexive thematic analysis of sociotechnical imaginaries. Phase 2 (Feb-Mar 2025): Follow-up interviews with 10 participants (9 synchronous Zoom, 1 asynchronous Google Docs); examined actual AI use patterns, cultural shifts in efficiency norms/transparency/trust, and revisiting of 2023 expectations; independent thematic analysis of 2025 data followed by linked-pair longitudinal analysis across timepoints. Participants: software engineers, architects, product/project managers, designers, consultants; 3-35 years experience (avg ~16); early AI adopters using GitHub Copilot, ChatGPT, Claude in distributed workflows.

## Main Arguments
- AI was imagined in 2023 as intelligent coordinator solving longstanding collaboration challenges (milestone tracking, sentiment detection, diagnostic dashboards) but adopted by 2025 as personal productivity assistant for individual tasks
- Core teamwork challenges persist despite AI adoption: underperformance detection, fragmented communication, accountability gaps remain unaddressed by individual-focused tools
- AI reshaped collaborative culture through bottom-up domestication rather than top-down design: efficiency expectations rose, responsible use became professionalism marker, normalization occurred without solving collaboration problems
- Gap between imaginaries and reality reflects divergence from collective aspirations to private routines as disruptive promises were tamed through everyday appropriation
- Most critical teamwork elements—empathy, negotiation, trust, motivation, contextual judgment—remain beyond AI's foundational architecture (statistical pattern prediction without concrete reality understanding)
- Individual AI productivity gains created systemic consequences: role boundaries expanded (non-technical staff performing technical work), expectations escalated (managers demanding faster output), coordination time extended despite acceleration
- Transparency and contextual understanding in AI use emerged as new benchmarks of professional judgment in distributed teams where outputs speak louder than process
- Relational foundations of collaboration (group awareness, shared understanding, interpersonal dynamics) require human labor that AI note-takers and summaries cannot substitute

## Limitations & Critiques
- Small sample size (N=15, 10 re-interviewed) limits generalizability; attrition bias as 5 participants not reachable for follow-up
- Strategic venue selection: Distributed project-based software development organization with early-adopter culture may not represent traditional hierarchical enterprises with slower AI diffusion
- Two-year interval may miss shorter-term fluctuations; middle-ground temporality between rapid adoption studies and decade-long trajectories
- Self-reported use rather than observational data on actual AI integration patterns; lacks behavioral metrics of collaboration effectiveness
- Asynchronous interview format for P13 may produce qualitatively different reflection depth than synchronous Zoom conversations
- Participants worked in industry with high AI literacy and enthusiasm—findings may not transfer to contexts with resistance or limited access
- Phase 1 interviews framed AI broadly (predictive + generative) while Phase 2 focused on generative AI tools; shifting definition may confound longitudinal comparison
- Lacks control group without AI adoption to isolate effects from general remote work evolution (2023-2025 included continued pandemic recovery)
- Cultural shift findings may reflect organizational norms specific to this company rather than universal AI domestication patterns
- No triangulation with manager perspectives, client feedback, or productivity metrics to validate claims about efficiency expectations and performance outcomes
