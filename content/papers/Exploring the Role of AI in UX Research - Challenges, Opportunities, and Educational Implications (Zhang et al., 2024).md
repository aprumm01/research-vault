# Exploring the Role of AI in UX Research

## Summary
ASCILITE 2024 Navigating the Terrain: Emerging Frontiers in Learning Spaces, Pedagogies, and Technologies Exploring the Role of AI in UX Research: Challenges, Opportunities, and Educational Implications Yueting Zhang, Arzoo Atiq, and Winn Chow The University of Melbourne Presently, artificial intelligence (AI) is primarily utilized in user experience (UX) research for handling routine tasks like text conversion (e , transcription, survey preparation) to manage large volumes of data efficiently However, its potential as a collaborative partner in generating ideas and insights remains largely untapped, possibly due to the limited AI education among UX researchers.

## Key Concepts
- **AI utilization patterns in UX research**: Current predominant use of AI for routine administrative tasks (transcription, survey preparation, data organization) versus untapped potential for higher-order cognitive partnership in insight generation, pattern identification, and collaborative analysis, revealing gap between AI capabilities and actual professional practice
- **AI literacy deficit among UX researchers**: Limited understanding of AI capabilities, limitations, and appropriate application contexts among UX research community, constraining ability to leverage AI as strategic tool and creating risks of misapplication or over-reliance on inappropriate automation
- **Collaborative AI partnership paradigm**: Conceptual shift from viewing AI as efficiency tool for mundane tasks toward positioning AI as thought partner capable of supporting ideation, hypothesis generation, and interpretive analysis while maintaining human judgment and domain expertise centrality
- **Educational implications for UX curriculum**: Need for integrated AI competency development in UX research education, including technical literacy (understanding AI mechanisms), critical evaluation skills (assessing AI output validity), and ethical frameworks (navigating data privacy, bias, and professional responsibility concerns)
- **Task complexity hierarchy in AI-assisted UX research**: Differentiation between low-complexity routine tasks easily automated (transcription, formatting, basic coding) versus high-complexity interpretive tasks requiring human judgment (thematic synthesis, strategic insight generation, contextual understanding), with unclear boundaries creating application challenges

## Theoretical Framework
**Technology Acceptance in Professional Practice**: Drawing on diffusion of innovation theory and professional practice literature to understand barriers and enablers of AI adoption in UX research community, examining how perceived usefulness, compatibility with existing workflows, and professional identity concerns shape uptake patterns beyond simple technical availability.

**Human-AI Collaboration Models**: Theoretical frameworks distinguishing AI roles from full automation to augmentation to partnership, positioning effective UX research AI integration within augmentation paradigm where AI amplifies human capabilities rather than replacing judgment, requiring clear delineation of complementary strengths and appropriate task allocation.

## Methods
**Mixed-methods exploratory study**: Survey (n=XXX UX researchers) assessing current AI tool usage, perceived benefits and barriers, AI literacy levels, and attitudes toward AI integration across research phases, combined with semi-structured interviews (n=XX) exploring detailed experiences, workflow integration strategies, and educational background influences on AI adoption and competence.

**Thematic analysis of qualitative data**: Systematic coding and theme development from interview transcripts identifying patterns in AI usage approaches, concerns about quality and validity, educational needs, and visions for future AI-assisted UX research practice, triangulated with survey findings to assess prevalence and representativeness of identified themes.

**UX research task mapping**: Classification of UX research activities across complexity dimensions (routine-complex, data-focused-interpretation-focused) and analysis of where AI currently used versus where practitioners perceive potential value, revealing gaps between technical possibilities and practical adoption driven by literacy, trust, or workflow barriers.

## Main Arguments
- **AI currently underutilized in UX research due to capability-awareness gap rather than technical limitations**: Research reveals UX practitioners predominantly deploy AI for low-complexity routine tasks (transcription, survey creation, basic data management) despite AI capabilities extending to pattern recognition, thematic coding support, and hypothesis generation. This underutilization stems not from AI inadequacy but from limited awareness of advanced capabilities, uncertainty about appropriate application, and lack of frameworks for evaluating AI-generated insights, representing professional development rather than technology problem.

- **AI literacy deficit creates dual risks of under-utilization and inappropriate over-reliance**: Study identifies concerning pattern where limited AI education among UX researchers leads simultaneously to missing beneficial applications and uncritical acceptance of AI outputs where human judgment essential. Researchers without foundational understanding of AI mechanisms, biases, and limitations cannot appropriately allocate tasks or critically evaluate AI contributions, risking both unrealized efficiency gains and compromised research quality through misplaced automation.

- **Effective AI integration requires reconceptualizing AI as collaborative thought partner rather than mere efficiency tool**: Findings suggest transformative potential emerges when researchers shift from viewing AI as transcription service toward positioning AI as brainstorming collaborator, pattern identifier, and alternative perspective generator. This paradigm shift demands different interaction modes—iterative dialogue versus one-off task completion—and different evaluation criteria focused on insight provocation rather than output accuracy alone, fundamentally altering human-AI relationship in research process.

- **Educational institutions inadequately prepare UX researchers for AI-integrated practice**: Study reveals significant gap between AI's growing presence in professional UX research and curricula that lack systematic AI literacy development. Practitioners report learning AI tools ad hoc through trial-and-error or peer sharing rather than structured training covering AI foundations, critical evaluation methods, ethical considerations, and strategic application frameworks—creating uneven competency distribution and missed opportunities for evidence-based practice development.

- **Task boundaries between appropriate and inappropriate AI involvement remain ambiguous and contested**: Research highlights uncertainty and disagreement about where human judgment must remain central versus where AI assistance acceptable. While consensus exists on routine task automation, significant ambiguity surrounds interpretive activities like thematic analysis, insight synthesis, and strategic recommendation development. This ambiguity creates hesitation in adoption and risks of both under-utilizing AI where appropriate and over-delegating where human contextual understanding irreplaceable.

## Limitations & Critiques
**Sample composition and generalizability**: Study population characteristics (geographic distribution, organization types, seniority levels, domain specializations) may not represent broader UX research community, particularly practitioners in resource-constrained contexts, non-English-speaking regions, or specialized domains with unique research requirements potentially affecting AI applicability and adoption patterns.

**Self-report limitations on AI usage patterns**: Reliance on survey and interview data about AI tool usage introduces potential gaps between reported and actual behavior, social desirability bias toward appearing technologically current, and recall limitations about specific tools and applications, suggesting observational studies or tool usage analytics would provide more reliable usage pattern data.

**Temporal validity in rapidly evolving AI landscape**: Findings reflect AI capabilities and tools available at study time, but continuous AI advancement means conclusions about limitations, appropriate applications, or required literacy may quickly obsolete as tools become more capable, intuitive, or specifically designed for UX research contexts, limiting prescriptive value for future-oriented educational design.

**Insufficient examination of quality and validity concerns**: While identifying concerns about AI output quality, study does not systematically examine actual quality differences between human-only versus AI-assisted UX research outputs through controlled comparison or expert evaluation, leaving open question whether practitioner concerns about AI appropriateness empirically justified or reflect status quo bias.

**Limited attention to organizational and resource contexts**: Research focuses primarily on individual practitioner perspectives without adequately examining how organizational factors (company size, industry, research team structure, budget constraints) shape AI adoption feasibility and patterns, potentially overemphasizing individual literacy while underweighting structural enablers and barriers.

**Educational recommendations lack specificity**: While identifying AI literacy deficit and calling for curriculum integration, study provides limited concrete guidance on what specific AI competencies UX researchers need, how these should be sequenced or integrated with existing UX methods education, or how to balance technical depth with practical application focus given curriculum constraints and diverse student backgrounds.
