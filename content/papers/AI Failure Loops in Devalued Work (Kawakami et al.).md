# AI Failure Loops in Devalued Work (Kawakami et al.)

## Summary
Kawakami et al. examine how systemic devaluation of worker expertise in feminized occupations (social work, K-12 teaching, home healthcare) creates "AI Failure Loops"—recurring patterns where undervaluation of workers leads to flawed AI deployments, which further erode recognition of worker skills. Drawing on literature review and field research across feminized labor contexts, they argue that occupational devaluation drives over-estimation of AI capabilities and under-recognition of worker expertise, creating negative feedback loops that deepen worker marginalization.

## Key Concepts
- AI Failure Loops
- Feminized labor and occupational devaluation
- Worker expertise undervaluation
- Workplace AI deployment failures
- Hidden integration labor / "patchwork"
- Participatory design in AI

## Theoretical Framework
Draws on Science and Technology Studies (STS), Computer-Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW), labor sociology, and feminist theory to analyze how gender-based devaluation shapes AI design and deployment. Builds on Suchman's work on situated action, Star's work on invisible work, and England's "prisoners of love" framework for feminized labor.

## Methods
Focused literature review of academic and grey literature on AI deployments in three feminized occupations, informed by authors' ten years of direct field research including ethnographic observations and interviews with social workers, home healthcare workers, and K-12 teachers.

## Main Arguments
- Workplace AI failures in feminized occupations stem from systemic devaluation of worker expertise, not just poor design
- AI Failure Loops create negative feedback: devaluation → flawed AI → further erosion of worker recognition
- Developers consistently over-estimate AI capabilities and under-estimate complexity of feminized labor
- Even well-intentioned worker-complementary AI fails when occupational devaluation is not addressed
- Current participatory design approaches are insufficient without addressing power dynamics and labor protections

## Limitations & Critiques
Focus on US context where labor protections differ from Nordic participatory design origins; case selection limited to three feminized occupations; relies partly on literature review rather than new empirical data for all cases; does not deeply examine intersectional dimensions beyond gender (race, class, immigration status).
