# AI Failure Loops in Devalued Work (Kawakami et al.)

## Summary
Kawakami et al. conceptualize "AI Failure Loops" — self-reinforcing sociotechnical failures that occur when workplace AI is deployed in feminized labor contexts (social work, K-12 teaching, home healthcare) where worker expertise is systematically devalued. The paper argues that overconfidence in AI capabilities and underconfidence in worker expertise compound each other, producing AI systems that fail to enhance labor and further erode workers' visibility. Understanding occupational devaluation is essential to diagnosing why workplace AI so consistently disappoints.

## Key Concepts
- AI Failure Loops (interwoven sociotechnical failure modes)
- Feminized labor (historically devalued care/service occupations)
- Occupational devaluation
- Cognitive offloading and worker expertise erosion
- AI governance and evaluation failures

## Theoretical Framework
Draws on Science and Technology Studies (STS), organizational sociology of labor, and HCI literature on AI deployment failures, centering the concept of occupational devaluation as the explanatory mechanism that prior AI failure accounts have overlooked.

## Methods
Focused literature review of academic and grey literature on AI deployments in three feminized labor domains in the US (K-12 education, home healthcare, social work), synthesized through a conceptual framework-building approach.

## Main Arguments
- Worker expertise devaluation shapes AI design assumptions, creating systems that fail to capture or support tacit knowledge
- AI Failure Loops are cyclical: poor AI design → reduced trust → reduced use → further devaluation of worker input → worse AI design
- Three case domains show consistent patterns of AI tools that add burden, reduce autonomy, and are ultimately withdrawn or ignored
- Responsible AI governance must explicitly account for labor power dynamics and occupational status

## Limitations & Critiques
The review is confined to US feminized labor contexts, limiting generalizability, and does not include primary empirical data from workers themselves, relying instead on synthesized prior literature.
