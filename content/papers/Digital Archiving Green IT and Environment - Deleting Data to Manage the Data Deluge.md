# Digital Archiving, Green IT and Environment: Deleting Data to Manage Critical Effects of the Data Deluge

## Summary

Van Bussel, Smit, and van de Pas authored this 2015 paper published in the Electronic Journal of Information Systems Evaluation, representing collaboration between HvA Amsterdam University of Applied Sciences, BECIS Information Management, and Saxion University of Applied Sciences in the Netherlands. The paper develops and validates a "Green Archiving Model" that integrates Green IT principles with Digital Archiving (DA) theories to reduce both data storage volumes and associated electricity consumption. The authors bring expertise in information management, archival science, and sustainable technology. This research is significant as one of the few papers directly connecting data management practices to environmental sustainability outcomes, providing empirical case study evidence that methodical data deletion based on information value assessment can reduce storage by 45% and electricity costs by 35%. The paper bridges traditionally separate fields—archival science's retention scheduling and IT's energy efficiency concerns—offering a practical framework for organizations to address the "data deluge" while meeting sustainability objectives.

## Research Overview

The central research question is "if it is possible to methodically reduce the amount of stored data and records in organizations based on the value of information, using the Green Archiving Model we have developed" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 189). The objective extends to demonstrating that "reducing the amount of data and records in organizations helps in allowing organizations to fight the data deluge and to realize the objectives of both Digital Archiving and Green IT" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 190).

The methodology combined desk research, qualitative interviews, focus groups, and exploratory case studies. The authors "collected literature with a key word search in Google Scholar, Microsoft Academic Search and in the Digital Library of the University of Amsterdam" using terms including "'Green Computing', 'Green IT', 'IT power use', 'IT power costs', 'information value', 'archival appraisal', 'archival disposal' and 'environmental awareness'" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 190). This was supplemented by "individual, semi-structured interviews with ten ICT, information management and archival science experts" and a focus group of six experts including Green Computing consultants, information managers, and storage managers (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 190).

Key concepts include **data deluge** ("the annual growth rate in the amount of data is almost 40%, creating a 'data deluge'"), **Green IT** (defined as "'the study and practice of designing, manufacturing, using, and disposing of computers, servers, and associated subsystems... efficiently and effectively with minimal or no impact on the environment'"), and **Information Value Chain (IVC)** for assessing data worth.

## Theoretical Framework

The paper integrates two primary theoretical frameworks. First, **Archival Retention Levels (ARLs)** from archival science, which "define detailed functional (organizational) responsibilities for the retention, storage and archiving of unique, authentic, relevant and contextual data and records" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 191). ARLs determine the time periods data should be retained based on "economic, social, cultural, financial, administrative, fiscal and/or legal value" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 191).

Second, the **Information Value Chain (IVC)** theory "defines the utilization of the informational and evidential value of data and records in business processes to improve trusted information management and the performance of business processes" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 192). The IVC encompasses "data generation, data identification, data capture, data storage, data processing, data distribution, data structuring, data publication, data (re-) use, data appraisal, data selection, data disposal, data retention, data security, data auditing and data preservation" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 192).

**Green IT** theory provides six components: "(1) product longevity, (2) software and deployment optimization, (3) power management, (4) materials recycling, (5) telecommuting, and (6) low power IT" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 190-191). The Green Archiving Model synthesizes these by arguing that "operationalizing Green Archiving, organizations curb power consumption, lower needs for storage capacity by permanently deleting data and records based on their value" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 190).

**Digital Archiving (DA)** manages four dimensions: "[1] quality, [2] context, [3] relevance, and [4] survival" ensuring that organizational records remain accessible and trustworthy over time.

## Central Arguments

The paper's central argument is that organizations can simultaneously achieve environmental sustainability and information management goals by systematically deleting data based on assessed value rather than retaining everything indefinitely. The authors contend that "the data deluge (and the use of more and more ICT resources to manage this deluge) threatens [1] to drown all positive effects of Green IT and [2] to raise energy costs exponentially" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 189).

Supporting sub-claims include:

1. **Data storage is a significant energy consumer**: "In 2008, storage networks were responsible for 15 percent of total ICT energy costs... This percentage had, in our estimate, doubled in 2011" and "electricity costs can approach 50 percent of the overall energy costs for an organization" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 189).

2. **Most stored data has no long-term value**: "Almost 75 percent of all data and records in an organization can be permanently deleted over time" according to retention schedule analysis, and "only 5 percent of all data would have to be retained for longer than twenty years" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 194).

3. **Deduplication alone is insufficient**: While "deduplication lets an organization keep 20 times more data in a given amount of storage" and reduces storage needs "up to a minimum of 30 percent," this technical solution must be combined with value-based disposal for maximum effect (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 192).

4. **Environmental and economic alignment**: "Methodically deleting data and records should reduce the consumption of electricity for data storage. As a consequence, the organizational cost for electricity use should be reduced" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 190).

## Evidence

The paper presents three exploratory case studies in an international trade corporation with European, Asian, and South American subsidiaries.

**Case Study 1** (ICT infrastructure scan, November-December 2013): Examined IT department awareness of Green Computing components. Found that "only the components Software Deployment and Optimization (virtualization of storage servers) and Power Management were implemented" while the corporation acknowledged other Green Computing components "would be a significant contribution to CSR" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 194).

**Case Study 2** (ARL Schedules pilot, March-May 2014): Analyzed file systems, storage area networks, and database management systems. Results: "Global data storage capacity was 45 TB (including the headquarters' 18 TB storage capacity). The conclusion of this pilot study was that the use of ARL checklists would diminish global data storage capacity with 30 percent. 37 percent of the company's data storage capacity was used for duplicate files" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 194). The IT department calculated "such a reduction of the amount of data would result in less electricity use for data storage and would diminish electricity costs with 25 percent" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 194).

**Case Study 3** (Records Schedule analysis, January-March 2015): The Chief Information Officer "concluded that almost 10 percent (3.5 TB) of all data and records stored in February 2015 could be disposed of immediately, because they had no 'value' anymore for the organization as all possible retention periods had passed" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 194).

Combined findings: "The model can be used to reduce [1] the amount of data (45 percent, using ARLs and Retention Schedules) and [2] the electricity consumption for data storage (resulting in a (calculated) cost reduction of 35 percent)" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 195).

**Limitations** include: case studies were exploratory with "provisional results" requiring further confirmation; environmental impact measurements (carbon dioxide reduction, e-waste) were not directly measured but inferred from electricity reduction; and findings are specific to one organization's context.

## Conclusion

For recall six months from now, this paper provides rare empirical evidence connecting data management practices to environmental sustainability. The Green Archiving Model combines archival science (Archival Retention Levels, Information Value Chain) with Green IT principles to systematically reduce data storage and associated energy consumption. Key findings from case studies in an international corporation: 45% storage reduction achievable through value-based disposal using ARL checklists; 35% electricity cost reduction calculated from storage reduction; 30% of storage consumed by duplicates addressable through deduplication; 10% of data immediately disposable as retention periods had expired. The model addresses the "data deluge" (40% annual data growth) threatening to overwhelm both storage capacity and Green IT benefits. Six Green IT components identified: product longevity, software optimization, power management, materials recycling, telecommuting, and low power IT—with data disposal as a seventh, often-overlooked component. The paper's ultimate vision is "the development of a low power ICT that will automatically appraise, select and preserve or permanently delete data and records" (van Bussel et al., 2015, p. 195). Critical insight: most organizational data (75%) can eventually be deleted, but organizations lack systematic frameworks and awareness to do so, resulting in unnecessary storage growth and energy consumption.

## Discussion Questions

1. The paper finds that 75% of organizational data can eventually be deleted, yet organizations rarely implement systematic disposal. What cultural, legal, or technological barriers prevent adoption of value-based data deletion?

2. How might the Green Archiving Model need to be adapted for cloud storage environments where organizations have less visibility into infrastructure energy consumption?

3. The case studies calculate electricity cost savings from storage reduction, but don't measure embodied carbon in hardware or e-waste reduction. How might a more comprehensive lifecycle assessment change the sustainability calculations?

4. Given the tension between data minimization (for sustainability and privacy) and big data analytics (which benefits from more data), how should organizations balance these competing imperatives?
