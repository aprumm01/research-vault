# Design Taste vs. Technical Skills in the Era of AI

## Summary

This article from Nielsen Norman Group examines the changing relationship between technical execution skills and design taste (or discernment) in an era where AI tools can generate designs, code, and creative work. The authors argue that while AI democratizes the ability to create artifacts, it simultaneously elevates the importance of taste—the ability to judge quality, make aesthetic decisions, and curate effectively.

The piece uses the analogy of microwaves and cooking: just as microwaves enabled anyone to heat food but did not make everyone a chef, AI enables anyone to create but does not automatically confer good judgment. The authors distinguish between "technical chops" (the ability to execute) and "taste" (the ability to discern quality), noting that historically these were bundled together because developing technical skills naturally exposed practitioners to quality examples.

The article warns of potential negative consequences, including a proliferation of mediocre AI-generated content and the risk that junior designers may skip the learning experiences that traditionally built both skills and taste simultaneously. The authors recommend intentional development of taste through exposure to quality work, practice in critiquing designs, and maintaining human judgment as the ultimate arbiter of quality.

## Key Concepts

- **Design Taste**: The ability to discern quality, make sound aesthetic judgments, and curate effectively; distinct from technical execution ability
- **Technical Chops**: The skills required to execute and create artifacts (code, designs, writing)
- **Microwave Analogy**: AI is to creativity what microwaves are to cooking—democratizes access but doesn't confer mastery
- **Taste-Skill Bundling**: Historical relationship where developing technical skills naturally built taste through exposure and practice
- **Curation as Skill**: The emerging importance of selecting and combining AI outputs effectively

## Theoretical Framework

The article draws on experiential learning theory and the concept of deliberate practice, suggesting that taste develops through repeated exposure to quality examples and intentional critique. It implicitly references the novice-to-expert progression literature, noting that bypassing early learning stages may have consequences for professional development.

## Methods

This is a conceptual/opinion piece rather than empirical research. The authors draw on their expertise as UX researchers and practitioners, using analogies and logical argumentation to make their case. No formal methodology is employed.

## Main Arguments

1. AI tools separate technical execution from judgment/taste for the first time at scale
2. Taste has always been valuable but was previously bundled with technical skill development
3. The democratization of creation will lead to more content overall, but potentially more mediocre content
4. Professionals who develop strong taste will be differentiated from those who merely use AI tools
5. Organizations should invest in developing taste, not just tool proficiency
6. Junior practitioners face particular risk of underdeveloped taste if they skip traditional learning progressions

## Limitations & Critiques

- Lacks empirical evidence for claims about taste development and AI's impact
- Does not address how taste itself might be culturally constructed or biased
- The microwave analogy may oversimplify the complexity of creative AI tools
- Does not engage with counterarguments about AI potentially developing or augmenting human taste
- Limited discussion of domain differences (visual design vs. writing vs. code)
- Assumes a relatively stable definition of "quality" without examining who defines it
