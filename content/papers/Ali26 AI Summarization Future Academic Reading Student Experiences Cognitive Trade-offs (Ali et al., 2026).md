# AI Summarization and the Future of Academic Reading: Exploring Student Experiences and Cognitive Trade-offs

## Summary
This qualitative phenomenological study examines how AI-powered summarization tools (particularly ChatGPT) are influencing academic reading practices, critical thinking abilities, and cognitive engagement of undergraduate students in Karachi, Pakistan. Through semi-structured interviews with nine purposively selected students from diverse disciplines, thematic analysis revealed five key themes: pragmatic adoption driven by academic pressure, surface-level processing and loss of deep reading, decreasing attention span and cognitive patience, growing dependency and diminished independent thinking, and student awareness of trade-offs. Published in Qlantic Journal of Social Sciences (2026).

## Key Concepts
- **AI summarization tools**: ChatGPT and similar LLM-based systems used to generate condensed versions of academic texts, reducing the need for full reading
- **Deep reading**: Active, sustained engagement with academic texts that involves critical analysis, annotation, interpretation, and synthesis—as contrasted with surface-level information extraction
- **Cognitive offloading**: Delegating cognitive work to external tools; AI summarization externalizes the reading comprehension and synthesis work that traditionally develops analytical skills
- **Cognitive patience**: The capacity to sustain attention and engage with long-form, complex academic material; students reported reduced patience for extended reading
- **Surface-level processing**: Engaging with AI-generated summaries rather than source texts, obtaining factual information without developing interpretive and critical capacities
- **Pragmatic adoption**: Students' use of AI driven primarily by academic workload pressure rather than pedagogical preference—using AI despite recognizing its negative effects on learning
- **Pakistani academic context**: The study foregrounds a Global South perspective underrepresented in AI in education literature, with specific attention to how institutional pressures and limited resources shape AI adoption patterns

## Theoretical Framework
The study draws on cognitive load theory (Sweller, 1988) to understand how AI summarization reduces cognitive demand—a benefit in terms of efficiency but potentially problematic for skill development when the cognitive work being offloaded is precisely the kind that builds analytical capacity. The paper engages with Kasneci et al.'s (2023) work on AI in education and Stadler et al.'s (2024) research on cognitive effects of AI summarization.

The phenomenological approach frames the study within lived experience theory, prioritizing students' first-person accounts of how AI tools are changing their relationship to reading and learning. The research also draws on literacy development theory, which holds that reading complex texts is not merely information retrieval but a developmental practice that builds vocabulary, comprehension strategies, critical interpretation, and sustained attention—capacities that may not develop if AI consistently performs this work.

## Methods
Qualitative phenomenological study using semi-structured interviews with nine purposively selected undergraduate students from diverse disciplines at universities in Karachi, Pakistan. Thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's (2021) six-step framework was applied to identify patterns across participant accounts. The purposive sampling strategy targeted students with substantial experience using AI summarization tools in their academic work. The study contributes a Pakistani academic context that is underrepresented in the existing AI in education literature dominated by Global North perspectives.

## Main Arguments
- Students adopt AI summarization tools primarily in response to academic pressure (workload, time constraints) rather than genuine preference for AI-mediated reading, creating a cycle where AI use is pragmatically driven even when students are aware it undermines their learning
- The adoption of AI summarization is associated with gradual erosion of deep reading habits: students reported decreased annotation practices, reduced engagement with textual nuance, and weakening of independent interpretive abilities
- Decreasing attention span and cognitive patience are emerging effects: students reported growing difficulty sustaining engagement with long-form academic material after habitually using AI summaries, suggesting neuroplastic effects of AI mediation
- Students demonstrate meaningful self-awareness of the trade-offs involved in AI summarization use, suggesting that educational interventions that build on rather than ignore this awareness may be more effective than blanket restrictions
- The Pakistani context reveals how structural factors—heavy academic workloads, limited access to full texts, English as a second language—create particular conditions for AI adoption that differ from Global North contexts
- Educators, curriculum designers, and policymakers need to develop explicit frameworks for teaching deep reading alongside AI tools, rather than allowing AI convenience to default into diminished reading practices

## Limitations & Critiques
The sample size of nine participants is small, limiting the depth of thematic saturation and generalizability. The focus on Pakistani students at Karachi universities represents a specific socio-economic and linguistic context; findings may not generalize to other Global South contexts, let alone Global North settings. The phenomenological approach captures students' perceptions of their cognitive changes rather than providing direct measurement of reading comprehension or critical thinking skills.

The study's focus on negative effects of AI summarization, while valuable as a counterweight to predominantly positive framings, may understate cases where AI summarization effectively supports learning. The relationship between reduced attention span and AI use is reported as student perception rather than established through longitudinal cognitive assessment.
