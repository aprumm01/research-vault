# Utilizing generative ai in design education visualizing aesthetic sensibilities through language expression

## Overview of the Document

This paper was authored by Hiyori Miyazaki and Wonseok Yang, both affiliated with Shibaura Institute of Technology in Tokyo, Japan. The work was published in 2025 as part of the Human-Computer Interaction International (HCII) conference proceedings, specifically in the Lecture Notes in Computer Science series (LNCS 15798, pages 79-89). The document represents an empirical study situated at the intersection of design education, artificial intelligence, and aesthetic theory. Both authors work within the context of understanding how emerging AI technologies can reshape traditional pedagogical approaches in art and design.

The paper investigates whether generative AI, specifically DALL·E 3, can effectively visualize abstract aesthetic concepts when given language-based prompts. The research focuses on a particularly challenging domain: eroticism as an aesthetic sensibility. This choice is deliberate, as eroticism represents a deeply subjective, culturally situated, and emotionally complex concept that has historically been central to artistic expression. The authors position their work within ongoing debates about AI's role in creative education, asking whether machines can meaningfully support the development of human sensitivity and aesthetic judgment when those qualities are fundamentally rooted in subjective experience.

The document matters because it addresses a pressing tension in contemporary design education. As AI tools become more integrated into creative workflows, educators and practitioners need to understand both the capabilities and limitations of these technologies. The paper contributes empirical evidence about where AI excels in visualizing abstract concepts and where it falls short, offering practical insights for educators who must decide how to incorporate these tools responsibly into their teaching.

## Research Overview

The central research question asks: To what extent can AI-generated images from abstract verbal prompts accurately convey intended emotional and aesthetic impressions to human viewers? More specifically, the authors investigate whether DALL·E 3 can visualize the concept of eroticism in ways that align with human expectations and sensibilities. The study was conducted by Miyazaki and Yang at Shibaura Institute of Technology between 2024 and 2025.

The research employed a multi-phase methodology. First, the team extracted 74 words related to eroticism using a thesaurus and ChatGPT, categorizing them into five groups: emotional words, sensory words, aesthetic words, physical/physiological words, and psychological words. They conducted an initial survey where participants selected words they associated with eroticism, identifying the top three words from each category (totaling 15 prompts). These 15 prompts were then fed into DALL·E 3 using a specialized model called "Word-form" that generates abstract shape-based visualizations without color or texture, focusing purely on form and shading.

The core data collection involved 113 participants who viewed the 15 generated images and selected which word from the original prompt list best matched each image. As the authors state, "A survey was conducted with 113 male and female participants, asking them to select which word they felt each generated image represented" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 83). The research measured both the match rate (how often participants correctly identified the original prompt) and the variation in responses (whether interpretations were consistent or scattered across multiple words).

Key research concepts include aesthetic sensibility (the developed capacity to perceive and respond to aesthetic qualities), abstract expression (visual representation of intangible concepts like emotions), and prompt consistency (the degree to which AI outputs align with input language). The authors draw on established frameworks from cognitive psychology, noting that "sensory information processing differs from intellectual information processing and is considered to differ among individuals" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 80), while also acknowledging that "emotional processing is closely related to human physiological responses" (p. 80), suggesting both subjective and universal dimensions to aesthetic experience.

## Theories of Knowledge

The paper engages multiple theoretical frameworks, though it does so more implicitly than explicitly. The most prominent is **Maslow's hierarchy of needs**, which the authors invoke to position eroticism as connected to fundamental physiological desires. They write, "According to Maslow's five-step theory of desire, physical desire is the lowest level of 'physiological desire' and is considered to be the most instinctive and latent of all human needs" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 81). This framework helps justify why eroticism serves as a valuable test case: it represents a domain where instinct, emotion, and cultural interpretation intersect.

The authors also reference **Parsons' stages of aesthetic development**, a developmental theory proposing that aesthetic cognition matures through distinct phases. As they note, "Parsons proposes that there are stages in the cognitive development of an aesthetic experience" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 80). This framework supports the paper's broader concern with how sensitivity can be cultivated through education, suggesting that aesthetic judgment is not innate but develops through structured exposure and practice.

**Affective computing**, pioneered by researchers like Rosalind Picard at MIT, provides the technological foundation. The paper situates AI emotion recognition within this tradition, mentioning that "methods for AI to understand human emotions have been studied, including verbal sentiment analysis (Sentiment Analysis) and nonverbal affective computing (Affective Computing)" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 80). This framework assumes emotions can be computationally modeled through patterns in data, whether linguistic or physiological.

**Georges Bataille's theory of eroticism** appears as philosophical grounding. The authors quote Bataille's claim that "Eroticism is the celebration of life until death" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 81), positioning eroticism not merely as sexuality but as a broader aesthetic and existential category that encompasses mystery, transcendence, and the tension between life and mortality. This theoretical lens elevates the study beyond technical questions about AI accuracy to deeper questions about whether machines can engage meaningfully with fundamental human experiences.

Additionally, the paper invokes **design thinking methodology**, specifically the five-stage model from Stanford's d.school (empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test). The authors argue that generative AI can enhance multiple stages of this process, particularly ideation and rapid prototyping, noting that "the idea expansion and rapid visualization techniques of generative AI are effective in the creative process" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 82). This framework positions AI not as a replacement for human creativity but as a collaborative tool within established creative methodologies.

External to the document but relevant here is **Barrett's theory of constructed emotion** (Barrett, 2017), which aligns with the paper's finding that emotions depend on conceptualization through language. The authors touch on this when stating that "while language and concepts have no influence on the conscious representation of one's physical state in the experience of emotions, humans experience emotions as individual feelings when conceptualized through language" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 81). This theory would support the study's premise that language prompts can serve as meaningful inputs for emotion visualization.

## Central Arguments

The authors advance a nuanced central argument: While generative AI demonstrates meaningful capacity to visualize abstract aesthetic concepts through language prompts, its effectiveness depends heavily on the concreteness of the prompt, the universality of associated visual features, and the degree to which individual versus collective sensibilities shape interpretation. The authors state this carefully: "Our findings demonstrate that while AI can visualize abstract concepts, its effectiveness depends on prompts and motifs. Interpretation varies by individual sensitivity, but common aesthetic values reduce variation" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 79).

This argument unfolds through several key sub-claims. First, the authors argue that AI performs well when prompts correspond to concrete, culturally stable visual referents. They found that "concrete prompts (e.g., 'naked,' 'body') showed high consistency and low variation" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 79), meaning participants largely agreed on what these images represented. This suggests AI can reliably visualize concepts that have established visual conventions.

Second, the authors claim that abstract prompts produce more varied interpretations, but some abstract concepts still maintain surprising coherence. They note that "while abstract prompts (e.g., 'mystery,' 'softness') had lower consistency... terms like 'close contact' and 'caress' exhibited relatively high alignment" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 79). This finding suggests that certain abstract concepts may have more stable visual correlates than others, possibly because they reference physical actions or spatial relationships that can be visualized more consistently.

Third, the authors argue that AI-generated images reveal both the subjective and universal dimensions of aesthetic perception. They propose that "subjects try to find features that match the meaning and nuance of the prompts... but their interpretation depends on each person's sensitivity. However, when a feature is based on common values and sensibilities, interpretation is less individualized" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, pp. 86-87). This dual nature suggests that while individual experience shapes interpretation, certain visual elements trigger more universal responses, likely rooted in shared physiological or cultural frameworks.

Fourth, the paper argues that current AI limitations in abstract visualization stem from multiple factors. The authors identify three possible explanations for low overall match rates: "'Features that can be commonly interpreted' were not sufficiently expressed," "AI still lacks the expressive power for abstract prompts," and "Individual differences in human sensitivity cause differences in image interpretation" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 87). This multi-causal explanation acknowledges both technical limitations of current models and inherent challenges in the task itself.

Finally, the authors make a pedagogical argument about implications for design education. They suggest that "this research highlights the need for educational methods accommodating diverse sensitivities and suggests new possibilities for AI-assisted Design education" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 79). The underlying claim is that AI tools should not homogenize aesthetic sensibility but rather support educators in recognizing and working with diverse interpretations, potentially making visible the range of ways students might perceive and respond to abstract aesthetic concepts.

## Evidence

The evidence supporting these arguments comes primarily from the impression survey conducted with 113 participants. The authors generated 15 images using DALL·E 3 based on the top-rated words from five categories of erotic concepts. The survey results revealed that "the average match score was 2.88, with a median score of 3 points" out of 15 (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 84), meaning participants correctly identified the original prompt for approximately 20% of images. While this might seem low, the authors note that "these results suggest that AI-generated abstract expressions can convey impressions aligned with the given prompts" (p. 84), acknowledging that even partial success is meaningful when dealing with highly subjective concepts.

The study identified four distinct patterns in how images performed, based on match rate and response variation. Pattern (iii), characterized by "high agreement rate and biased responses," showed the strongest performance. The authors provide concrete evidence: "The word 'naked' classified in this category was answered as 'naked' or 'body' by 88.5% of the respondents" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 86). This demonstrates that when abstract prompts have strong, culturally stable visual correlates, AI can produce highly consistent and recognizable outputs.

Conversely, pattern (iv) revealed systematic failures where AI outputs triggered unintended associations. The authors found that "'Temptation' categorized in this category was answered as 'curve' by 60.2% of the respondents, resulting in a low percentage of correct responses, although the answers were concentrated in a single category" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 86). This evidence suggests that AI may latch onto specific visual features (curves) that relate to but do not fully capture the intended concept (temptation), revealing gaps between semantic meaning and visual representation.

The initial word extraction survey provided foundational evidence about collective associations with eroticism. From 74 candidate words, participants selected terms across five categories, with the top three from each category becoming experimental prompts. This methodological choice grounds the study in actual human associations rather than researcher assumptions, strengthening the ecological validity of the findings.

The research methods carry inherent limitations that the authors acknowledge. The use of "Word-form" for image generation eliminated color and texture, focusing exclusively on form and shading. The authors state: "To investigate the impression of abstract expressions, we eliminated color and texture information and limited ourselves to two-dimensional representations of three-dimensional objects through shading" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 88). This design choice enhances experimental control but limits generalizability, as color and texture are crucial aesthetic dimensions in actual design practice. Real-world applications of generative AI would include these elements, potentially changing both AI performance and human interpretation.

Another methodological limitation involves the cultural specificity of the sample. All 113 participants engaged with concepts of eroticism that are culturally situated, yet the paper does not report participant demographics beyond binary gender categories. As the authors note, "Eroticism has also been treated as a concept that not only stimulates human senses and emotions but also has deep relevance in the realms of art and religion" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 81), suggesting cultural variation in interpretation. The lack of demographic data makes it difficult to assess how cultural background might influence results.

The study's reliance on DALL·E 3, a commercial model with opaque training data and content filtering policies, introduces another limitation. The authors acknowledge the rapid pace of AI development, noting that "the speed of updates to new prompt construction guidelines and AI development is very rapid" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 88). This means findings may quickly become dated as models evolve. Additionally, DALL·E's content policies likely influenced what could be generated for erotic concepts, potentially constraining the range of visual expressions available for evaluation.

The forced-choice survey design, where participants selected from 15 predetermined words, may have artificially constrained interpretation. Participants could not generate their own descriptors, meaning we cannot know what terms they would have chosen independently or whether the provided options adequately captured their actual impressions. This methodological choice prioritizes quantifiable comparison at the expense of richer qualitative data about lived interpretation.

## Conclusion

This research contributes empirical evidence about the capabilities and limitations of generative AI in visualizing abstract aesthetic concepts, specifically within the domain of eroticism. The study demonstrates that AI can meaningfully translate certain language-based prompts into visual forms that humans recognize and interpret with reasonable consistency, particularly when those prompts correspond to concrete visual referents or actions. However, accuracy declines substantially for highly abstract concepts, revealing gaps between linguistic semantics and visual representation that current models struggle to bridge.

The findings have immediate implications for design education. Educators incorporating AI tools must recognize that these systems work best as collaborative aids rather than autonomous aesthetic agents. The technology can support ideation and rapid prototyping, particularly in early design thinking phases, but cannot replace the developed sensitivity and interpretive capacity that traditional drawing, observation, and critique cultivate. The research suggests a pedagogical approach that uses AI-generated outputs as prompts for discussion about diverse interpretations rather than as definitive visualizations of concepts.

The study also highlights unresolved questions about the nature of aesthetic knowledge itself. The fact that certain abstract concepts (like "close contact" and "caress") generated more consistent interpretations than others suggests that some abstractions may have more stable embodied or spatial correlates. Future research could investigate which categories of abstract concepts are most amenable to visual representation and why, potentially revealing deeper insights about the relationship between language, embodiment, and visual cognition.

What remains open is how rapidly evolving AI capabilities will shift these findings. The authors acknowledge this limitation, noting that continuous research is needed "based on the latest technology" (Miyazaki & Yang, 2025, p. 88). As models incorporate multimodal training, better understand context and nuance, and potentially integrate affective computing advances, their capacity to handle abstract aesthetic concepts may improve substantially. Additionally, the research focused exclusively on form-based visualization; future work incorporating color, texture, animation, and other aesthetic dimensions would provide a more complete picture of AI's expressive capabilities.

The paper opens questions about whether educational focus should shift from developing traditional observational skills to cultivating what might be called "AI collaboration literacy": the capacity to craft effective prompts, critically evaluate outputs, and iterate between human judgment and machine generation. This represents a fundamentally different pedagogical model than reproduction-based art education, though whether it can cultivate the same depth of sensitivity remains an open empirical question that this research begins to address but cannot definitively answer.

## Discussion Questions

1. The study found that concrete prompts like "naked" achieved 88.5% recognition while abstract prompts like "temptation" were frequently misinterpreted as "curve." What does this pattern suggest about the fundamental differences between how humans and current AI models process metaphor, and should design educators prioritize teaching students to work within AI's concrete strengths or to push against its abstract limitations?

2. The authors chose eroticism as their test case because it represents "an emotion rooted in human instincts and closely related to aesthetic sensibilities in the field of art." If AI struggles with this fundamental human domain, what implications does that have for claims that AI can serve as a meaningful partner in developing aesthetic sensitivity rather than merely a technical tool for rapid prototyping?

3. The research stripped away color and texture to isolate form, yet these are crucial aesthetic dimensions in actual design practice. How might the inclusion of these elements change both AI performance and the study's conclusions about AI's capacity to visualize abstract sensibilities, and does isolating form actually test what matters most in design education?

4. The paper argues for "educational methods accommodating diverse sensitivities" rather than seeking convergence toward correct interpretations. How does this pluralistic pedagogical stance intersect with traditional design education's emphasis on cultivating refined judgment, and at what point does celebrating diverse interpretations risk undermining the development of expert aesthetic discernment?
