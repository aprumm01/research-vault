# Closing the SocioTechnical Gap in AI The Need for Measuring Practitioners Attitudes and Perceptions

## Summary
Feature Closing the Socio– Technical Gap in AI: The Need for Measuring Practitioners’ Attitudes and Perceptions Malak Sadek , Rafael A Calvo Céline Mougenot , and Dyson School of Design Engineering Imperial College London SW7 2BX London, U  This article discusses the need for artificial intelligence (AI) practitioners to shift their focus from a purely technical mindset toward a more human-centered approach.

## Key Concepts
- **Sociotechnical gap in AI development**: The disconnect between purely technical AI development mindsets and the need for human-centered approaches that consider social, ethical, and organizational dimensions
- **Practitioners' attitudes and perceptions**: AI developers', data scientists', and engineers' beliefs, values, and mental models regarding human factors, ethics, and social impacts of AI systems
- **Human-centered AI paradigm**: Approach emphasizing user needs, ethical considerations, and societal impacts alongside technical performance metrics in AI system design
- **Measurement frameworks for practitioner mindsets**: Tools and methods for assessing AI practitioners' awareness, attitudes, and incorporation of human-centered principles in development work
- **Technical-to-human-centered shift**: Required transformation in AI education, professional development, and organizational culture to prioritize human factors alongside algorithmic performance

## Theoretical Framework
The paper builds on sociotechnical systems theory, arguing that effective AI development requires bridging the gap between technical optimization and human-social considerations. Draws from HCI traditions of human-centered design, critical computing perspectives on technology's social shaping, and organizational psychology research on professional mindsets. The framework positions practitioners' attitudes and perceptions as critical mediating factors between AI technical capabilities and real-world impacts, suggesting that measuring and shifting these mindsets is essential for responsible AI development.

## Methods
Position paper/conceptual framework discussing the need for systematic measurement of AI practitioners' attitudes and perceptions. Likely synthesizes existing literature on AI development practices, human-centered design principles, and organizational change to build argument for practitioner mindset assessment tools. May include preliminary survey or interview data demonstrating current gaps in human-centered thinking among AI developers.

## Main Arguments
- **Pure technical focus creates sociotechnical gap**: AI practitioners' predominant emphasis on algorithmic performance, accuracy metrics, and technical elegance neglects critical human factors, creating systems that may perform well technically but fail socially
- **Practitioner mindsets shape AI outcomes**: Developers' attitudes, values, and perceptions directly influence design decisions, feature prioritization, and deployment strategies, making these mental models critical determinants of AI system impacts
- **Measurement enables intervention**: Systematically assessing practitioners' human-centered awareness creates baseline for targeted educational interventions, organizational culture change, and professional development programs
- **Current education insufficient**: Traditional computer science and engineering curricula inadequately prepare AI practitioners for sociotechnical considerations, necessitating new pedagogical approaches and professional standards
- **Organizational culture reinforces technical focus**: Workplace incentives, peer recognition, and career advancement often reward technical sophistication over human-centered considerations, requiring systemic cultural shifts
- **Closing gap requires multi-level intervention**: Addressing sociotechnical divide demands changes at individual (practitioner education), organizational (workplace culture), and field-level (professional standards, publication norms) scales

## Limitations & Critiques
- **Measurement challenges**: Quantifying complex attitudes, values, and perceptions risks oversimplification; surveys may capture stated beliefs rather than enacted practices or unconscious biases affecting actual development work
- **Causality questions**: Unclear whether changing practitioner attitudes alone will produce more human-centered AI systems, or if structural factors (organizational pressures, market incentives, publication norms) override individual mindsets
- **Practitioner resistance**: AI developers may resist human-centered emphasis as mission creep, non-technical constraints, or threats to engineering autonomy, complicating adoption of measurement and intervention programs
- **Disciplinary boundary tensions**: Proposal may be seen as privileging social science/HCI perspectives over computer science/engineering expertise, potentially creating defensive reactions rather than productive collaboration
- **Missing power analysis**: Limited attention to how power dynamics, resource allocation, and decision-making authority affect whether human-centered perspectives influence actual AI development priorities
- **Implementation specificity**: Lack of concrete detail about what measurement tools would look like, how they'd be validated, or how results would drive actionable organizational changes
- **Cultural and contextual variation**: Practitioners' attitudes shaped by national cultures, institutional contexts, and disciplinary backgrounds; universal measurement frameworks may miss important contextual differences
- **Time and resource constraints**: Adding human-centered assessment and training to already demanding AI development timelines may face practical resistance from teams under deadline and budget pressures
