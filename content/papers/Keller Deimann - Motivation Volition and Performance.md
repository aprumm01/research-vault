# Motivation, Volition, and Performance

## Summary
This chapter provides an overview of motivation and volition principles for instructional designers, explaining how these concepts affect learner persistence and performance. The authors distinguish between motivation (the desire to pursue goals) and volition (the self-regulatory strategies needed to protect intentions and maintain effort when facing obstacles). Using a vignette of a graduate student named Ashley who struggles to maintain motivation in a demanding program, they illustrate how initial motivation can wane and why volitional strategies become essential.

The chapter presents Keller's ARCS model (Attention, Relevance, Confidence, Satisfaction) as a systematic approach to motivational design, emphasizing that instructional designers must understand and integrate motivational principles to create effective learning experiences. The authors argue that volition has been underemphasized in instructional design and present strategies for helping learners maintain their intentions through self-regulatory mechanisms when facing distractions and competing goals.

## Key Concepts
- **Motivation**: A person's desire to pursue a goal, manifested by choice of goals and effort (persistence plus vigor) in pursuing them
- **Volition**: Self-regulatory strategies that protect intentions and help learners persist when facing obstacles, distractions, or competing goals
- **ARCS Model**: Keller's motivational design framework with four categories: Attention (curiosity, arousal), Relevance (goal orientation, connection to needs), Confidence (expectancy for success), and Satisfaction (intrinsic/extrinsic reinforcement)
- **Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivation**: Intrinsic motivation occurs when engaging in tasks for inherent pleasure; extrinsic motivation involves engagement for external rewards

## Theoretical Framework
The chapter synthesizes multiple theoretical traditions including Berlyne's curiosity theory, attribution theory (Weiner), self-efficacy theory (Bandura), locus of control (Rotter), learned helplessness (Seligman), and self-regulation research (Kuhl). The ARCS model provides an integrative framework that translates these theories into practical design principles.

## Methods
This is a theoretical chapter presenting principles and models for instructional design practice. It draws on empirical research in educational psychology and instructional design to support its recommendations.

## Main Arguments
1. Motivation is promoted when learners' curiosity is aroused by perceived gaps in knowledge
2. Motivation is promoted when knowledge is perceived as meaningfully related to goals
3. Motivation is promoted when learners believe they can succeed in mastering tasks
4. Motivation is promoted when learners anticipate and experience satisfying outcomes
5. Motivation is protected and maintained when learners employ volitional (self-regulatory) strategies
6. Instructional designers can predictably influence motivation and volition through systematic design

## Limitations & Critiques
- The authors note that motivational design models tend to focus more on generating initial motivation than on sustaining persistence through learning
- Volition research has not been fully integrated into instructional design practice
- Motivation is an internal construct that can be difficult to observe and measure directly
- Individual differences in motivational characteristics require adaptive approaches
