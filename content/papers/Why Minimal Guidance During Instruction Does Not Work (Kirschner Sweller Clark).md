## Summary
Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark mount a cognitive-scientific critique of minimally guided instruction, arguing that constructivist, discovery, problem-based, and inquiry-based approaches are less effective than explicit instruction because they ignore how human cognitive architecture works. Drawing on cognitive load theory and research on expert-novice differences, they contend that novices lack the schemas necessary to benefit from minimal guidance, making direct instruction more efficient and effective for initial learning.

## Theoretical Framework
Cognitive load theory (Sweller) and human cognitive architecture provide the empirical basis for arguing that working memory limitations make minimally guided approaches inefficient for novice learners.

## Methods
Theoretical review and synthesis of empirical evidence from cognitive psychology and educational research spanning five decades.

## Main Arguments
- Minimally guided instruction fails because novices lack the schemas to search and process problem spaces effectively
- Cognitive load theory explains why direct instruction is more efficient: it manages working memory constraints
- Constructivist approaches may work for experts who can self-direct, but are counterproductive for novices
- Half a century of empirical research consistently shows explicit instruction produces better learning outcomes
