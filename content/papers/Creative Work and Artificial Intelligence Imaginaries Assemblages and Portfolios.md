# Creative Work and Artificial Intelligence Imaginaries, Assemblages and Portfolios

## Summary
This article analyzes how AI's impacts on creative work have been constructed in UK grey literature (government, charity, foundation, university, commercial sources). Using imaginaries as methodology, Ashton identifies three interconnecting risk imaginaries positioning creative occupations as differentiated from others—they are safe, being complemented, or not being replaced by automation. These imaginaries are challenged in two ways: first, assemblages concept highlights AI's longstanding, everyday role in creative production; second, analysis of portfolio working and multiple job holding problematizes notions of "safe" creative occupations. Argues AI-creative work relationships involve both enhancing creative production opportunities and concerns about uncreative production and non-creative work.

## Key Concepts
- **Imaginaries**: Sociotechnical visions shaping how AI-creative work relationships are understood; provisional frameworks holding together congruity in social understandings of technology
- **Risk imaginaries**: Three interconnecting visions positioning creative occupations as safe from AI automation, being complemented by AI, or not being replaced by automation
- **Assemblages**: Complex everyday arrangements of AI technologies already embedded in creative production, challenging notion of AI as future threat (drawing on Zylinska)
- **Portfolio working**: Creative workers' practice of holding multiple jobs across sectors, problematizing notion of "safe" creative occupations isolated from automation
- **Creative work definition**: Symbol-making occupations in cultural industries—arts and media sectors (Hesmondhalgh & Baker framework)
- **Cultural AI analytical perspective**: Approach focusing on AI's wider social, cultural, economic, political effects rather than technical capabilities (Liu)
- **Grey literature analysis**: Examining policy documents, reports, and briefings from government, charities, foundations, universities, and commercial sources shaping public discourse

## Theoretical Framework
Draws on sociotechnical imaginaries theory (Jasanoff et al., Willim) examining how provisional visions shape technological possibilities and social arrangements. Engages Media/Cultural Studies and STS scholarship on technology description, classification, and uptake (Lister et al., Dovey & Kennedy, Bucher). Uses assemblages theory (Zylinska) to understand complex human-nonhuman arrangements in creative production. Builds on creative industries labor research (Hesmondhalgh & Baker, Ashton) examining precarious cultural work conditions. Positions within cultural AI analytical perspective (Liu) examining social contexts. Connects to future of work debates (Benanav, West) on AI, automation, and labor transformation.

## Methods
Imaginaries analysis examining UK grey literature from diverse institutional sources: government departments, charities, foundations, universities, commercial companies. Focus on publicly available written statements creating links between technology and social life, particularly documents with capacity to frame public understanding of AI-creative work relationships. Methodological approach follows Jasanoff et al. on analyzing sociotechnical imaginaries through documents and verbal texts related to science, technology, and power. Discourse analysis rather than empirical study of actual creative workers' experiences.

## Main Arguments
- **Grey literature constructs differential imaginaries**: Policy documents and reports position creative occupations as special and safe from automation, differentiating them from other work types more vulnerable to AI displacement
- **Three interconnected risk imaginaries emerge**: Creative work framed as (1) safe from AI automation, (2) being complemented by AI tools, (3) not being replaced by AI—visions that overlap and mutually reinforce
- **Assemblages challenge futurity framing**: AI already deeply embedded in everyday creative production through algorithmic recommendation, content management, distribution platforms—not future threat but present complex reality
- **Portfolio working undermines "safe occupation" narrative**: Creative workers typically hold multiple jobs across sectors including non-creative work; notion of protected creative occupations ignores ecological realities of precarious creative labor
- **Replacement discourse misframes relationships**: AI's impact doesn't rest on simple human replacement or special status of creative work; must consider complex everyday sociotechnical arrangements
- **Dual impact requires nuanced understanding**: Relationship involves both enhancing creative production opportunities AND concerns about uncreative production, non-creative work constraints, and labor precarity
- **Imaginaries are provisional but powerful**: These framings are imperfect and contested yet shape public understanding, policy formation, and institutional responses to AI in creative sectors
- **Public discourse requires critical examination**: Media coverage and institutional narratives create (mis)understandings that need interrogation rather than acceptance

## Limitations & Critiques
- **UK-centric geographical scope**: Focus on UK grey literature limits applicability; other regions (US, EU, Asia) may construct different imaginaries reflecting distinct cultural, economic, policy contexts
- **Discourse analysis without lived experience**: Examining texts doesn't capture actual creative workers' experiences engaging with AI in practice; missing practitioner voices and everyday realities
- **Limited empirical grounding**: Unlike complementary qualitative studies (e.g., Taffel's practitioner interviews), doesn't examine creative practices or perceptions directly
- **Definitional complexities underaddressed**: Creative/cultural industries boundaries acknowledged as messy but not fully resolved; what counts as "creative work" remains contested
- **Subsector differences unexplored**: Limited attention to variations across creative domains (visual arts vs. music vs. writing vs. game development); imaginaries may differ significantly
- **Portfolio working gesture incomplete**: Raises important challenge about multiple job holding but doesn't fully develop implications for creative labor precarity and vulnerability
- **Assemblages concept underelaborated**: Applied theoretically but lacks detailed ethnographic examination of actual creative production sociotechnical arrangements
- **Worker agency missing**: Doesn't address how creative workers might contest, resist, or reshape dominant imaginaries through collective action or alternative narratives
- **Temporal dynamics unexplored**: Static analysis doesn't track how imaginaries evolve as AI capabilities change and creative industries adapt
