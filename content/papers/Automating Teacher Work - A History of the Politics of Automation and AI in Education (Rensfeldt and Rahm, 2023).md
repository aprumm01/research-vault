# Automating Teacher Work? A History of the Politics of Automation and Artificial Intelligence in Education

## Summary
This historical analysis examines how automation debates in education have consistently centered on teachers' work, from the 1950s to present. Using Sweden as a case study with international relevance, the authors analyze policy documents, news media, and archival materials to reveal recurring patterns in how automation technologies are positioned in relation to teacher labor.

The authors frame their analysis around two contrasting perspectives: "the exceptional" (transformative promises and fears about automation) and "the mundane" (incremental, already-installed digital infrastructures). They argue that both automation promises (labor-saving, efficiency) and resistance (protecting human judgment, professional autonomy) have been remarkably consistent across decades, despite dramatic changes in technology.

Key historical moments include: the 1950s-60s "teaching machines" era emphasizing programmed instruction; the 1970s-80s emergence of computer-based education and concurrent labor resistance movements; the 1990s-2000s decentralization and platform integration; and the 2010s-present era of AI, adaptive learning systems, and data infrastructures. Throughout, the authors show how debates oscillate between exceptional claims about automation's transformative potential and mundane realities of how technologies become normalized and integrated into everyday practice.

The paper concludes that automation in education aligns with broader "techno-solutionist" narratives while simultaneously generating forms of resistance. The authors call for more critical perspectives that examine whose labor is implied in automation technologies and what political stakes are involved.

## Key Concepts
- **The Exceptional vs. The Mundane**: Analytical frame contrasting transformative automation claims with incremental, normalized technology adoption
- **Policy Assemblage**: Theoretical concept describing how political imaginaries, technologies, infrastructures, and agents gather to shape automation debates
- **Fauxtomation**: Taylor's concept describing how human labor covers for automation shortcomings
- **Digital Labor**: Work performed on platforms that often goes unrecognized but supports automation systems

## Theoretical Framework
The paper draws on Foucauldian analysis of power and governmentality, Science and Technology Studies (STS), and critical AI studies. The authors analyze how discourses, governing rationalities, and inscriptions of automation technologies constitute a "policy assemblage" that shapes what is thinkable about teachers' work.

## Methods
Historical analysis of digital and archival materials from 1957 to present, including government proposals, newspaper articles, teacher-press debates, and popular science reviews. Materials were collected using keywords related to automation, AI, teaching, and education. Analysis focuses on discourses, governing rationalities, and inscriptions of automation technologies.

## Main Arguments
- Automation debates in education are fundamentally debates about teachers' work and labor
- Promises of labor-saving automation have been repeatedly promoted yet research shows automated work rarely reduces overall workload
- The "exceptional" framing of automation (transformative hopes and fears) silences critical debate about mundane, incremental changes
- Automation is not politically neutral; it implies new divisions of labor and arrangements between human and machine work
- Resistance to automation has taken different forms across eras: labor union protests (1970s), data privacy concerns (present)
- Contemporary AI and platform infrastructures make automation both hidden (through APIs) and pervasive

## Limitations & Critiques
- The Swedish context, while internationally aligned, may have specific policy characteristics not generalizable elsewhere
- The historical scope (1957-present) necessarily simplifies complex developments within each era
- The paper notes that demonstrating teacher technology resistance risks characterizing them as obstacles to progress
- Balancing exceptional and mundane perspectives requires making visible both transformative claims and normalized practices
