# Do Learners Really Know Best Urban Legends in Education

## Summary
This article critically examines three pervasive urban legends in education that assume learners know best and should control their own learning: (1) digital natives—a generation naturally skilled at learning from new media; (2) learning styles—belief that instruction should match individual preferred learning styles; (3) self-educators—learners should have maximum control over content and learning trajectory. The authors argue these beliefs, though popular in educational policy, contradict substantial educational psychology research evidence.

## Key Concepts
- Urban legends in education - widely circulated beliefs about learners and learning that sound plausible but lack empirical support from educational psychology research
- Digital natives - claimed generation immersed in technology with distinct characteristics, sophisticated technical skills, and unique learning preferences traditional education cannot address
- Homo zappiens - term describing new generation allegedly learning in fundamentally different ways through inquiry-based, discovery-based, networked, experiential, and collaborative approaches
- Multitasking myth - false belief that current generation can simultaneously perform multiple cognitively demanding tasks effectively and efficiently
- Task switching - actual cognitive process occurring when people attempt multitasking, involving goal shifting and rule activation with performance costs
- Butterfly defect - learners fluttering across information on screens, touching or not touching hyperlinks without plan or awareness of value
- Learning styles hypothesis - belief requiring crossover interaction where type A learners learn best with method A while type B learners learn best with method B
- Meshing hypothesis - preferential model assuming instruction should match learner's preferred style, with learner "knowing" what is best
- Mathemathantic effects - teaching kills learning when instructional methods match preferred but unproductive learning style (from Latin mathema = learning + thanatos = death)
- Compensation model - instructional approach making up for undesirable effects of particular learning inclinations rather than catering to them

## Theoretical Framework
- Human cognitive architecture - explains limitations on multitasking through central cognitive bottleneck requiring task operators to be interleaved
- Working memory theory - underpins arguments about cognitive resource limitations when switching between tasks requiring conscious processing
- Information processing theory - frames discussion of how task switching creates interference because tasks compete for limited cognitive resources

## Methods
- Critical literature review examining evidence for three educational urban legends
- Synthesis of empirical studies from cognitive psychology, educational psychology, and neuroscience
- Meta-analysis examination (Clark 1982) of learner preference for instructional methods
- Review of learning styles research across 71 different styles (Coffield et al. 2004)

## Main Arguments
- Digital native claims lack empirical foundation: Prensky coined the term by rationalizing observed phenomena rather than conducting research, even misattributing neuroscience quotes to wrong researcher
- University students lack deep technology knowledge: multiple international studies show students have basic office skills, email, texting, and Facebook but limited understanding of enhanced functionality or learning applications
- Young people's technology engagement is unspectacular: Selwyn's thorough review contradicts popular portrayals, while Bennett et al. characterize debate as "academic form of moral panic" with mismatch between claims and evidence
- Multitasking is impossible for conscious cognitive tasks: humans can only switch between tasks, not perform them simultaneously, with switching creating central cognitive bottleneck at neural level
- Task switching impairs performance and learning across contexts: from graduate students' reading comprehension time (1.66x longer with instant messaging) to medical errors from interruptions to pilot errors and pharmacy mistakes
- Heavy media multitaskers perform worse at task switching: Ophir et al. found they are more susceptible to irrelevant interference and reduced ability to filter out distractions
- Learning styles cannot be reliably measured: self-report instruments show low reliability (.60s-.70s) and weak validity, with preferred learning mode being poor predictor of effective learning
- Preferred learning ≠ effective learning: Clark's meta-analysis found learner preference typically uncorrelated or negatively correlated to learning outcomes—like children preferring candy over fruit
- 71 different learning styles create impossible combinatorics: with 271 possible combinations (more than people on Earth), individualizing instruction becomes totally impractical
- Crossover interactions rarely occur: Pashler et al. review found very few studies showing required evidence for learning-styles hypothesis where different learners benefit from matched methods
- Self-directed learning risks: giving learners control over selection and sequencing of learning content often leads to suboptimal choices based on incomplete metacognitive awareness

## Limitations & Critiques
- Authors adopt polemical tone using "urban legend" framing that may alienate educators who have implemented challenged approaches in good faith
- Selective literature review: while comprehensive for cognitive psychology evidence, underrepresents constructivist and experiential learning traditions that inform challenged practices
- Digital natives critique may be dated: article published 2013 before widespread educational technology integration—claims about student technology incompetence may not hold for 2020s cohorts
- Dismisses potential benefits of learner agency: focus on cognitive limitations neglects motivational and engagement benefits when learners exercise choice and control
- Learning styles criticism conflates weak measurement with invalid construct: poor reliability of instruments doesn't necessarily invalidate existence of individual differences in learning preferences
- Overgeneralizes from laboratory studies: multitasking research often uses artificial dual-task paradigms that may not reflect authentic multimedia learning environments
- Compensation model underspecified: authors advocate teaching against students' natural inclinations but provide limited guidance on how to implement compensatory instruction effectively
- Missing nuance on developmentally appropriate control: blanket rejection of learner control doesn't differentiate between young children and adult learners with more metacognitive sophistication
- Authors don't address technology's rapidly evolving nature: critiques of 2008-2012 student capabilities may not account for how AI tools fundamentally change information access and learning requirements
- Economic and access issues ignored: assumes all students have equal technology exposure, potentially conflating access inequities with competence deficits
