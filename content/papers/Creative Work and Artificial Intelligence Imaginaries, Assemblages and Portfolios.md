# Creative Work and Artificial Intelligence: Imaginaries, Assemblages and Portfolios


## Summary

## Summary
This paper examines how creative workers (designers, artists, writers, musicians) construct imaginaries — collective narratives and expectations — about AI and how these imaginaries shape creative practice, professional identity, and portfolio decisions. Through qualitative research with creative practitioners, the authors find that AI imaginaries are diverse and contested: some workers envision AI as liberating creative potential; others see it as a threat to creative livelihood and distinctive human expression. The paper analyzes how these imaginaries influence actual AI adoption and the emergent assemblages of human creativity and AI capability.

## Key Concepts
- Sociotechnical imaginaries (Jasanoff) applied to AI
- Creative practice and AI assemblages
- Portfolio strategies in the age of AI
- Creative labor identity and AI
- Contested narratives about AI's creative potential

## Theoretical Framework
Sociotechnical imaginaries (Jasanoff & Kim); assemblage theory (DeLanda); critical AI studies; draws on sociology of technology and creative industries research.

## Methods
Qualitative interviews with creative workers (designers, artists, writers) across disciplines; analysis of portfolio strategies and AI disclosure practices; thematic analysis of AI imaginary narratives.

## Main Arguments
- Creative workers hold diverse and often contradictory imaginaries about AI that shape their adoption behaviors more than actual AI capabilities
- AI assemblages in creative work are emergent and unstable: what AI "is" in creative practice depends on how practitioners configure relationships between human skills, AI tools, and client contexts
- Portfolio presentation strategies around AI are becoming a site of professional identity work: when and how to disclose AI use signals professional values
- The diversity of creative AI imaginaries argues against uniform policy approaches to AI in creative industries

## Limitations & Critiques
Qualitative sampling may not represent the full diversity of creative workers; the imaginaries framework may overemphasize discourse at the expense of material practice conditions; rapidly evolving tools may shift imaginary narratives faster than research can document.
