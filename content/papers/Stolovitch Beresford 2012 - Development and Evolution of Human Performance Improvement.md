# The Development and Evolution of Human Performance Improvement

## Summary
This chapter traces the origins, development, and evolution of Human Performance Improvement (HPI) and Human Performance Technology (HPT). The authors define HPI as a professional field centered on achieving valued accomplishments through people in workplace settings, with performance defined as the ratio of valued accomplishment to costly behavior. They explain how HPI emerged from the confluence of human capital economics, systems thinking, organizational complexity, and dissatisfaction with training-only solutions.

The chapter provides a historical account beginning with apprenticeships, through the audiovisual movement and instructional systems design, to the birth of HPT through the work of Thomas Gilbert (who developed the Behavior Engineering Model and mathetics), Joe Harless (who created the performance improvement process), and Robert Mager and Peter Pipe (who wrote "Analyzing Performance Problems"). The authors describe the role of professional societies like ISPI and ASTD in advancing the field and conclude that HPI represents a natural evolution toward systematic alignment of human capital management with organizational goals.

## Key Concepts
- **Human Performance Improvement (HPI)**: Achieving increasingly successful accomplishments through people that are valued by all stakeholders
- **Performance**: The valued accomplishment derived from costly behavior; P = A/B where worthy performance has accomplishment value exceeding behavior cost
- **Behavior Engineering Model (BEM)**: Gilbert's six-cell model examining environmental factors (information, resources, incentives) and individual factors (knowledge, capacity, motives)
- **Human Capital**: The knowledge, skills, and capabilities of workers that yield higher rates of return than physical capital

## Theoretical Framework
HPT draws from multiple knowledge sources including cybernetics, behavioral psychology, communications theory, information theory, systems theory, management science, cognitive sciences, and neuroscience. The field is empirical, guided by experience-based principles that allow for innovation while maintaining systematic practice. Key assumptions include that human performance follows predictable laws, performance can be predicted and controlled, and HPT must rely on both practical experience and scientific research.

## Methods
This is a historical and conceptual chapter reviewing the development of HPI/HPT through analysis of key publications, models, and professional organizations from the 1960s through the 2000s.

## Main Arguments
- HPI has a straightforward vision: achieve increasingly successful accomplishments through people that are valued by all stakeholders
- Performance is the valued accomplishment derived from costly behavior; the goal is to increase the ratio of accomplishment to cost
- Simple solutions for closing performance gaps are almost always ineffective; systemic analysis is required
- HPI emerged when training professionals recognized that many performance problems require non-instructional interventions
- Human capital yields higher rates of return than physical capital, justifying investment in performance improvement
- HPT is closely related to organizational development, human resource development, and organizational effectiveness but maintains a unique focus on measurable performance outcomes

## Limitations & Critiques
- HPT has not yet generated a firm theoretical foundation of its own, relying instead on principles borrowed from related fields
- The field continues to evolve, with definitions and practices still being refined
- Implementation requires practitioners to resist the tendency to jump to training solutions without proper performance analysis
