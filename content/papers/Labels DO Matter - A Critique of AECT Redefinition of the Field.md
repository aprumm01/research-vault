# Labels DO Matter! A Critique of AECT's Redefinition of the Field

## Summary

This article critiques the Association for Educational Communications and Technology's (AECT) 2008 decision to revert to the term "educational technology" from "instructional technology" in their official definition of the field. Published in TechTrends, the authors argue that this change was problematic not because of which term is objectively better, but because of the weak rationale provided for the change and the failure to consider how labels shape professional identity, market positioning, and field coherence.

The authors marshal evidence from three sources—professional job announcements, university program titles, and listserv discussions—to demonstrate that practitioners and institutions have not reached consensus on field labels, with "instructional technology," "instructional design," and "educational technology" all in common use with different connotations. They argue that labels matter because they function as brands that shape how external audiences perceive the profession, because language is constitutive of thought and identity (drawing on Vygotsky and Gee), and because changing labels without thorough justification risks fragmenting an already diffuse field.

Rather than advocating for any particular label, the authors call for AECT to engage in more transparent, participatory, and thoroughly reasoned processes when making definitional changes that affect the entire professional community.

## Key Concepts

- **Labels as Brands**: Professional field labels function like corporate brands, requiring substantial investment in recognition and carrying connotations that attract or repel potential members and employers
- **Language and Identity**: Drawing on Vygotsky (1962, 1978), Gee (1996, 2000), and Lakoff (1987), the authors argue that labels are not neutral descriptors but constitutive of professional identity and community membership
- **The Cocktail Party Question**: The common professional challenge of explaining "What do you do exactly?" reflects ongoing identity confusion in the field
- **Educational Technology vs. Instructional Technology**: Terms with different historical associations—educational technology suggests K-12 settings, while instructional technology connotes broader training and workplace contexts

## Theoretical Framework

The article draws on sociolinguistic theory (Vygotsky, Wertsch, Gee, Bourdieu) to argue that language shapes rather than merely reflects thought and identity. Labels create communities of practice and signal inclusion or exclusion. The authors also invoke branding literature to argue that labels function as organizational assets requiring careful management.

## Methods

The authors conducted a multi-method environmental scan:
1. **Job posting analysis**: Six weeks of postings from Chronicle of Higher Education, HigherEdJobs.com, AECT Job Board, and Indiana University's IT Job Board (N=327 postings), coded for use of "educational technology," "instructional technology," and "instructional design"
2. **Academic program analysis**: Reviewed AECT's Curricula Data of Degree Programs listing 134 U.S. programs, counting label usage in degree and program names
3. **Listserv discourse analysis**: Searched ITFORUM archives for discussions of field definitions and labels to characterize practitioner perspectives

## Main Arguments

- Labels have far-reaching consequences for how external audiences view the profession and for practitioner identity; they are not interchangeable
- Job postings show "instructional technology" used 3x more often than "educational technology," and "instructional design" is the most common job title
- Among 134 academic programs, 51 use "Instructional Technology," 34 use "Educational Technology," and 13 use "Instructional Design" in their names
- Listserv discussions reveal persistent identity confusion and lack of consensus, with participants noting that "educational technology" suggests K-12 while "instructional" implies broader learning contexts
- AECT's book justifying the label change provides insufficient thorough rationale; readers are left to "piece together some kind of grounds for the decision"
- The field has historically changed its name multiple times (Department of Visual Instruction → Audiovisual Instruction → Audiovisual Communications → AECT), suggesting ongoing identity crisis
- Design is equally important to technology in the field; Heinich (1984) argued design should have equal standing, yet "design" appears minimally in official definitions

## Limitations & Critiques

- The authors explicitly do not advocate for any specific label, which may limit the article's practical utility for resolving the naming question
- Job posting and program data provide a snapshot but may not reflect deeper field dynamics or future trajectories
- The critique focuses on process (how the decision was made) more than substance (which label is best), which may frustrate readers seeking definitive guidance
- Does not engage with international perspectives where "educational technology" may have different connotations
- The article was written in 2010; subsequent developments in the field may have shifted the landscape
