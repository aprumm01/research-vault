# The Expert Systems Business: How It Grew and Died (IEEE Annals, 2018)

## Summary
Chronicles the rise and fall of the commercial expert systems market from the 1970s through the early 1990s, tracing the proliferation of companies, products (LISP machines, shells, domain-specific tools), and marketing strategies before the market's collapse. Offers the expert systems business as a cautionary case study for understanding how AI commercialization cycles of hype and disappointment operate.

## Key Concepts
- Expert systems (rule-based AI)
- Inference engines and knowledge bases
- LISP and Prolog languages
- AI winter
- Technology hype cycle

## Theoretical Framework
Business history of technology commercialization; market segmentation analysis distinguishing language vendors, hardware vendors, shell tools, and domain-specific systems.

## Methods
Industry history drawing on professional experience (Harmon edited a leading expert systems newsletter); review of company strategies, products, and market evolution; speculative analysis of collapse causes.

## Main Arguments
- Expert systems originated from Stanford's MYCIN program and proliferated into a complex commercial ecosystem of tools, shells, and domain-specific products
- The market attracted investment but suffered from unrealistic expectations, high development costs, and the "knowledge acquisition bottleneck"
- Collapse in the early 1990s was driven by overpromising, platform wars, and inability to scale beyond narrow domains
- Today's AI developers face structurally similar risks from hype cycles and misaligned commercialization incentives

## Limitations & Critiques
Primarily descriptive/historical with limited causal analysis; speculation on collapse causes is not empirically validated; single industry insider's perspective may overemphasize certain market dynamics; parallels to contemporary deep learning AI are suggestive but not systematically analyzed.
