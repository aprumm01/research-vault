# The Technocratic Momentum after 1945: Teaching Machines

## Summary

Trohler provides a historical analysis of the teaching machines and programmed instruction movement following World War II, examining how post-war anxieties and Cold War pressures shaped educational technology development. The paper reveals ideological continuities between Eastern and Western approaches to education reform, both driven by technocratic visions of efficiency and standardization. Skinner's behaviorist teaching machines are situated within broader political and economic contexts of the era.

## Key Concepts

- **Technocratic momentum**: The self-sustaining drive toward technological solutions in education
- **Programmed instruction**: Behaviorist approach breaking content into small steps with immediate feedback
- **Teaching machines**: Mechanical/electronic devices implementing programmed instruction principles
- **Cold War education reform**: Post-Sputnik urgency to modernize education through technology
- **Ideological convergence**: Surprising similarities between Soviet and American educational technology approaches

## Theoretical Framework

Historical-sociological analysis drawing on:
- History of ideas in education
- Science and technology studies
- Cold War historiography
- Critical analysis of behaviorism in education

## Methods

- Historical document analysis
- Comparative analysis of Eastern/Western educational reforms
- Examination of policy documents, scientific papers, and popular media
- Contextual analysis within post-WWII geopolitical tensions

## Main Arguments

1. **Post-war crisis response**: Teaching machines emerged from anxieties about national survival and international competition
2. **Behaviorist foundations**: Skinner's operant conditioning provided scientific legitimacy for programmed instruction
3. **OECD influence**: International organizations promoted technocratic educational reforms across borders
4. **East-West convergence**: Despite ideological opposition, both superpowers pursued similar technological solutions to education
5. **Teacher displacement anxiety**: Teaching machines raised enduring concerns about technology replacing human educators
6. **Legacy effects**: The movement established patterns of technological solutionism still visible in contemporary EdTech

## Limitations & Critiques

- Focus on US and European contexts limits global perspective
- Could engage more deeply with teacher and student perspectives of the era
- The connection to contemporary AI-driven education could be more explicitly drawn
- Does not fully address why programmed instruction ultimately declined in popularity
