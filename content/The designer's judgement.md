# The designer's judgement

## Summary
Holt (University of Queensland) examines the nature of "judgement" in engineering design, drawing on Sir Geoffrey Vickers' philosophical work on appreciation and judgment. Holt argues that design judgment—the pervasive evaluative act underlying every design decision—operates across three knowledge domains: the formative (aesthetic and cultural fit), the commercial (economic and market considerations), and the instrumental (technical feasibility and function). The paper proposes a framework for understanding design judgment and considers how engineering education can develop it in students.

## Key Concepts
- **Design judgement**: The evaluative act by which designers assess options, directions, and trade-offs at every stage of design
- **Formative domain**: Judgments about aesthetic quality, cultural appropriateness, and social fit
- **Commercial domain**: Judgments about market viability, economic constraints, and client requirements
- **Instrumental domain**: Judgments about technical feasibility, functional performance, and engineering constraints
- **Vickers' appreciative system**: Framework for understanding how individuals and institutions perceive, evaluate, and act; the basis for Holt's design judgment model

## Theoretical Framework
Philosophical analysis drawing on Sir Geoffrey Vickers' theory of appreciation and judgment, applied to engineering design. Vickers argued that professional judgment involves both "reality judgments" (what is the case) and "value judgments" (what matters), operating through an appreciative system built from experience.

## Methods
Theoretical and philosophical analysis; applies Vickers' framework to engineering design contexts and examines implications for design education.

## Main Arguments
1. Good judgment is the core competency of design practice; it cannot be reduced to rule-following or optimization algorithms
2. Design judgment operates simultaneously across formative, commercial, and instrumental domains; excellent designers integrate all three
3. Judgment is teachable—through exposure to design cases, structured reflection, and mentored practice—but current engineering curricula underemphasize it
4. The "game-playing" critique of engineering education (Hamming) reflects the cultivation of narrow technical judgment at the expense of broader design judgment

## Limitations
Philosophical framework based on Vickers may be unfamiliar to engineering education practitioners; operationalizing "appreciative systems" for pedagogical purposes is non-trivial. Focus on engineering design may limit direct applicability to other design disciplines.
