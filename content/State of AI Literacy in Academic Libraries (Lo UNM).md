# State of AI Literacy in Academic Libraries (Lo UNM)

**DOI:** [10.25844/p9rv-vj67](https://doi.org/10.25844/p9rv-vj67)

## Summary
Lo surveys the current state of AI literacy knowledge, skills, and institutional support among academic library professionals, identifying significant gaps between practitioner awareness of AI tools and the competencies required to effectively support AI-mediated research and learning. The study finds wide variation across institutions, with some libraries developing proactive AI literacy programs and others largely unprepared. The paper proposes a tiered AI literacy framework for academic libraries that distinguishes foundational awareness, intermediate competency, and advanced expertise levels.

## Key Concepts
- AI literacy in academic libraries
- Tiered competency framework
- Institutional variation in AI preparedness
- AI in research and scholarly communication
- Library professional development

## Theoretical Framework
Draws on library science professional competency frameworks and AI literacy models (Long and Magerko); adapts these to the specific information professional context; distinguishes AI literacy as a user-facing (helping patrons) and practitioner-facing (professional workflow) challenge.

## Methods
Survey study of academic library professionals across multiple institutions; structured questions on AI tool awareness, current use, training received, and perceived competency gaps; descriptive and comparative analysis across institution types.

## Main Arguments
- Most academic librarians have foundational AI awareness but lack intermediate and advanced competencies needed to critically evaluate AI tools and support complex research applications
- Institutional support for AI literacy development is highly variable — larger research libraries have more resources; smaller institutions are largely left to self-directed learning
- AI literacy in academic libraries must address the unique epistemic challenges of LLMs for information work: hallucination, citation fabrication, and training data biases undermine traditional information quality frameworks
- A tiered competency framework enables institutions to set realistic development targets and design appropriate training programs

## Limitations
- Survey methodology captures self-reported competency; actual skill levels may differ
- Rapidly changing AI tool landscape means findings about specific tools may date quickly
