# Understanding the Past, Present, and Future of Design Fictions

## Metadata

- **Year:** 2020

## Overview
This CHI 2020 workshop summary paper maps the landscape of [[design fiction]] within HCI — its diverse forms, contested definitions, historical roots, and methodological status. Rather than imposing a single definition, the organizers surface open questions about what design fictions are, where they came from, and how they should be evaluated. The paper is an agenda-setting document for the CHI community, proposing evaluation guidelines and inviting reflection on the role of speculative practice in interaction design research.

## Research Overview
- **Research question:** What is the state of design fiction as a practice, method, and community within HCI — and what shared frameworks, if any, should guide it?
- **Methodology:** Workshop-based synthesis; community input solicited through position papers and examples; reflection on historical development, current diversity, and future directions
- **Context:** CHI 2020 (originally Honolulu, held virtually); HCI and interaction design community; workshop led by multi-institutional team including IU Bloomington researchers

## Theories of Knowledge
- **Epistemological stance:** Pluralist and reflexive; the paper explicitly resists epistemic closure — it invites "reports, analyses, and examples" without pre-defining what counts
- **Key frameworks:**
  - [[Design Fiction]] — diverse lineages: Dunne & Raby's critical design, Sterling's coinage, speculative fiction traditions
  - [[Speculative Design]] — Dunne & Raby; designs that interrogate, not just solve
  - [[Participatory Design]] — Boal's Theatre of the Oppressed as antecedent; drama and scenario methods
  - [[Critical Design]] — design as cultural critique; challenges dominant assumptions
  - [[HCI Methods]] — scenarios, personas, probes, future studies as predecessor/overlapping methods
- **Knowledge construction:** Design fictions construct knowledge through artifact-making; the artifact itself is a form of argument about possible futures, not a proposal for a specific one

## Central Arguments
1. Design fiction is increasingly important in HCI but is used in inconsistently named, practiced, and evaluated ways — this diversity is both a strength and a source of confusion.
2. The origins of design fiction are contested: literary, critical design, and HCI traditions all claim ownership; the paper surfaces rather than resolves this.
3. Design fictions serve multiple functions: communicating within teams, provoking publics, inviting co-design, making political arguments — not all of these can be evaluated by the same criteria.
4. The field is ready for both a descriptive account of current diversity and a set of evaluation guidelines for reviewers of design fiction submissions.

## Evidence
- Taxonomy of design fiction categories (Table 1): stories, pastiche scenarios, imaginary abstracts, futuristic autobiographies, artifacts, probes, dramas, and others
- Historical antecedents identified: Swift's *Gulliver's Travels*, Shelley's *Frankenstein*, *1001 Arabian Nights* — probing what properly "counts" as design fiction
- HCI-specific antecedents: scenarios and personas (over a decade of use), probes, future studies, participatory theatrical practices
- Unresolved questions surfaced: How do we evaluate design fictions? What distinguishes them from predecessor methods? How much is design fiction novel vs. continuous with prior speculative practices?

> "Design fictions are increasingly important and prevalent within HCI, though they are created through diverse practices and approaches and take many diverse forms." (Muller et al., 2020, p. 1)

## Conclusion
The workshop produces a descriptive orientation to design fiction's current state and a proposed set of evaluation guidelines — acknowledging that a field cannot be evaluated without first agreeing on what it is. The paper serves as both a map of existing practice and an invitation to collective self-definition.

## Bias Check
- Workshop paper — reflective and agenda-setting rather than empirical; findings are community-generated, not controlled
- Primarily Western/Anglophone design fiction tradition; non-Western speculative practices underrepresented
- Multi-author team with strong HCI backgrounds may underweight design fiction's lineage in art, architecture, and literary fiction communities

## Discussion Questions
1. How does [[design fiction]] differ from [[speculative design]] and [[critical design]] — are these distinctions meaningful or territorial?
2. What evaluation criteria are appropriate for design fictions — and who should set them?
3. How might [[design fiction]] be used specifically in AI research — to surface not-to-design implications (cf. [[Showkat_2022_ValueSystemNLP]])?
4. Can design fiction be [[participatory]] in a meaningful sense — or does it inevitably reflect the designer's imaginative horizon?
5. What is the relationship between design fiction and [[futures studies]] / foresight methods used in policy and organizational contexts?
