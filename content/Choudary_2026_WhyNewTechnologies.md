## Overview
This HBR practitioner essay argues that incumbent organizations consistently fail to leverage new technologies — including AI — not because of execution failures, skills gaps, or middle management inertia, but because they misunderstand what transformation actually requires. The core claim: incumbents use AI to optimize existing work structures rather than to restructure the *coordination architecture* of work itself. The Figma/Adobe and Shein/fashion industry cases are used to illustrate how smaller challengers win by redesigning how work is organized, not just how efficiently it runs.

## Research Overview
- **Research question:** Why do incumbents invest heavily in new technologies yet consistently lose competitive advantage to smaller challengers?
- **Methodology:** Practitioner essay; comparative case analysis (Figma vs. Adobe; Shein vs. traditional fashion houses); no empirical data collection; argument-driven with illustrative evidence
- **Context:** Harvard Business Review, AI and Machine Learning section; audience is organizational leaders and strategists; author is a C-level AI/platform strategy advisor, senior Berkeley fellow, and Thinkers50 Strategy Award winner (2025)

## Theories of Knowledge
- **Epistemological stance:** Practitioner-strategic; pragmatist — knowledge is tested by whether it produces competitive advantage, not by theoretical coherence
- **Key frameworks:**
  - [[Coordination Architecture]] — the central concept; how work is divided, sequenced, and coordinated across teams; distinct from workflow efficiency
  - [[Platform Strategy]] — Choudary's prior work on network effects and platform design (see *Reshuffle*)
  - [[AI Adoption]] — framed not as a tooling problem but as an organizational design problem
  - [[Incumbent Disadvantage]] — echoes [[Christensen]]'s [[disruptive innovation]] framework but reframes the mechanism
- **Knowledge construction:** Knowledge comes from observing organizational behavior across industries; the practitioner standpoint privileges pattern recognition over controlled inquiry

## Central Arguments
1. Incumbents mistake tool adoption for transformation — layering AI on top of existing workflows produces efficiency gains, not competitive advantage.
2. The real opportunity is in restructuring the *unit of work* and the coordination architecture — how work is broken into pieces, sequenced, and recombined. AI makes this restructuring newly possible at scale.
3. When incumbents fail at AI transformation, they blame execution (skills gaps, training lag, middle management). The actual cause is architectural — they are optimizing the wrong level of the system.
4. New coordination architectures foreclose some strategies while enabling entirely new ones — this is a strategic, not operational, shift.

## Evidence
- **Figma vs. Adobe:** Adobe successfully transitioned to subscription (cloud) model — conventional wisdom calls this a model of transformation. But Figma built a *shared* design environment that restructured team collaboration and decision-making, creating a strategic threat Adobe's cloud transition did not address. Adobe's $20 billion acquisition attempt of Figma reflects how existential this architectural gap became.
- **Shein vs. traditional fashion houses:** Shein redesigned the unit of production — rapid micro-batch manufacturing tied directly to real-time demand signals — while incumbents used AI to optimize within existing seasonal production architectures.

> "Incumbents fail because they haven't recognized that new technologies enable new ways of coordinating work." (Choudary, 2026)

> "The bigger opportunity lies elsewhere... restructure the organization of work — how it's divided, sequenced, and coordinated across teams." (Choudary, 2026)

## Conclusion
AI's competitive advantage lies not in making existing work faster but in enabling new [[coordination architectures]] that make certain incumbent strategies structurally obsolete. Organizations that recognize this shift can use AI to create strategic positions that are difficult to replicate by incumbents optimizing within existing structures.

## Bias Check
- HBR practitioner essay — not peer-reviewed; no systematic data; argument structured to persuade, not to build cumulative evidence
- Cases are selected to illustrate the argument — no counter-cases considered (incumbents who successfully restructured coordination architecture)
- The concept of "coordination architecture" is not formally defined or operationalized, making empirical testing difficult
- Choudary's background in platform strategy and AI consulting may bias toward structural/systems-level explanations over cultural or political ones

## Discussion Questions
1. How does [[coordination architecture]] relate to [[organizational design theory]] and [[sociotechnical systems]] frameworks?
2. Is this argument a restatement of [[disruptive innovation]] (Christensen) in AI terms — or does it identify a genuinely different mechanism?
3. What prevents incumbents from restructuring coordination architecture even when they understand the argument? What organizational forces resist?
4. How does this apply to educational institutions adopting AI — are universities incumbents failing to restructure the unit of learning?
5. What is the relationship between [[coordination architecture]] and [[power]] — who controls the architecture controls the work?
