## Summary
Nokhiz, Ruwanpathirana, and Nissenbaum propose a framework that combines systems thinking with the economic concept of externalities to address the unintended social consequences of optimization-based AI decision-making. Noting that traditional economic frameworks describe externalities but fail to address their normative implications or feedback loops, the paper argues for incorporating systems thinking to understand interconnected stakeholder effects, temporal dynamics, and feedback. Three subpar optimization practices are analyzed — ignorance (missing affected parties), error (wrong model), and short-term goal prioritization — with the framework showing how each can be characterized and corrected.

## Key Concepts
- Social externalities of optimization
- Systems thinking (interconnections, feedback loops, emergence)
- Economic externalities (costs/benefits to third parties)
- Subpar optimization practices (ignorance, error, short-termism)
- Stakeholder characterization in AI systems

## Theoretical Framework
Synthesizes Pigouvian externality theory from economics with systems thinking (Meadows, Senge); extends both by attending to normative questions (who is affected, by how much, and what obligations follow) that pure economic or systems analysis leaves underspecified.

## Methods
Conceptual framework paper; develops the combined systems-externalities framework; illustrates it through three case types of subpar optimization; applies it to AI/ML decision-making contexts.

## Main Arguments
- Labeling all problematic AI outcomes as "unintended consequences" is too coarse — distinguishing types of causes (ignorance vs. error vs. short-termism) is necessary for targeted solutions
- External parties affected by optimization are systematically excluded from the optimization process — systems thinking reveals feedback loops through which their exclusion degrades system performance for everyone
- Economic externality frameworks provide powerful quantification tools but lack normative teeth; systems thinking adds the "when" and "how" of incorporating affected parties into redesigned optimization
- Joint application of both frameworks enables more comprehensive characterization of optimization failures and more principled reform proposals

## Limitations
- Framework is primarily conceptual; operationalizing stakeholder identification and systems boundary drawing in real AI deployments remains challenging
- The paper focuses on optimization-based AI; newer generative AI systems present different externality dynamics
