# Navigating More-Than-Human Perspectives in HCI Education (Nilsson et al.)


## Abstract

**DOI:** [10.1145/3742901.3742907](https://doi.org/10.1145/3742901.3742907)
This paper follows up on the challenges we encountered when we initially began teaching more-than-human perspectives in human-computer interaction (HCI) and technology design. Since then, we have developed a collection of teaching activities and conducted pilots with nearly 800 students, exploring how these perspectives can be taught in various settings. Although we think that our teaching activities address some of the initial challenges, several challenges are still open, and new ones have emerged such as the gap between what we are teaching and the industry, and the feelings of hopelessness that students may feel when confronted with the notion that technology may not fix all problems. These challenges can be used as an entry point for discussions and the identification of possible and impossible future paths for teaching more-than-human design.

## Summary
Nilsson et al. examine how HCI educators can incorporate more-than-human perspectives — posthumanist, multispecies, and ecological framings — into interaction design curricula that have historically centered human experience and needs. The paper argues that as AI, the Internet of Things, and ecological crisis reshape what HCI addresses, students must develop capacity to design with and for non-human actors, entangled systems, and uncertain futures. The authors present pedagogical approaches and student responses, finding that more-than-human framings productively destabilize students' assumptions about design's subject matter.

## Key Concepts
- More-than-human design perspectives
- Posthumanism in HCI
- Multispecies design
- Ecological design futures
- Decentering the human in interaction design education

## Theoretical Framework
Draws on posthumanist theory (Haraway, Barad), more-than-human geography, and speculative design; challenges HCI's foundational anthropocentrism and invites students to consider non-human agents, materials, and ecological systems as design stakeholders.

## Methods
Pedagogical action research; design studio courses incorporating more-than-human briefs and frameworks; thematic analysis of student project outcomes and reflections; educator reflection on pedagogical challenges.

## Main Arguments
- HCI education's dominant human-centered paradigm is insufficient for contemporary design challenges that inherently involve non-human actors (AI systems, ecosystems, microbiomes, infrastructure)
- More-than-human perspectives destabilize students' design assumptions productively — uncertainty about who/what to design for drives more reflective and ethically attentive design thinking
- Posthumanist framings do not abandon human welfare but recontextualize it within broader webs of relation — a necessary move for sustainable, equitable design
- Educators must model intellectual flexibility and genuine openness to non-anthropocentric perspectives rather than treating them as exotic add-ons

## Limitations
- Pedagogical approaches developed in a Scandinavian higher education context with specific cultural receptivity to ecological and posthumanist thinking
- Assessment challenges: more-than-human design work resists conventional evaluation criteria centered on user needs and usability
