# EVALUATING

## Summary
Kirkpatrick's *Evaluating Training Programs: The Four Levels* presents the foundational framework for evaluating workplace training effectiveness across four levels: (1) Reaction—participant satisfaction; (2) Learning—knowledge and skill acquisition; (3) Behavior—on-the-job transfer; (4) Results—organizational outcomes. The book advocates moving beyond Level 1 (smile sheets) to demonstrate training's business value. The excerpted chapter covers reasons for evaluation: establishing credibility, justifying investment, and demonstrating value to organizational stakeholders.

## Key Concepts
- **Level 1 (Reaction)**: Participant satisfaction ratings collected immediately after training
- **Level 2 (Learning)**: Pre/post knowledge and skill assessment
- **Level 3 (Behavior)**: Observation or supervisor ratings of on-the-job behavior change after training
- **Level 4 (Results)**: Organizational metrics (productivity, quality, costs, turnover) attributed to training
- **Training credibility**: The organizational case for training as a value-adding function

## Theoretical Framework
Practical evaluation framework developed from Kirkpatrick's doctoral research and decades of training practice. Not grounded in a specific learning theory but in organizational pragmatics—demonstrating that training produces measurable organizational value.

## Methods
Prescriptive framework; based on practitioner experience and case illustrations rather than controlled research. Each level is described with practical guidance for data collection and use.

## Main Arguments
1. Training evaluation is essential for establishing and maintaining organizational credibility for L&D functions
2. Most organizations evaluate only at Level 1 (reaction), missing the most important evidence about effectiveness
3. Moving to Level 4 (results) requires patience, rigor, and collaboration with business stakeholders to isolate training's contribution
4. The four levels are hierarchically related: positive reactions do not guarantee learning; learning does not guarantee behavior change

## Limitations
Extensively criticized for conflating evaluation levels with causal relationships; positive correlations across levels do not establish that training caused the outcomes. Level 4 evaluation is notoriously difficult to attribute to training specifically amid other organizational influences.
