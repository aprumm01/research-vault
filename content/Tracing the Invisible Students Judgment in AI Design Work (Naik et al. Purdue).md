# Tracing the Invisible: Students Judgment in AI Design Work (Naik et al. Purdue)


## Abstract

**DOI:** [10.1145/3698061.3734399](https://doi.org/10.1145/3698061.3734399)
As generative AI tools become integrated into design workflows, students increasingly engage with these tools not just as aids, but as collaborators. This study analyzes reflections from 33 student teams in an HCI design course to examine the kinds of judgments students make when using AI tools. We found both established forms of design judgment (e.g., instrumental, appreciative, quality) and emergent types: agency-distribution judgment and reliability judgment. These new forms capture how students negotiate creative responsibility with AI and assess the trustworthiness of its outputs. Our findings suggest that generative AI introduces new layers of complexity into design reasoning, prompting students to reflect not only on what AI produces, but also on how and when to rely on it. By foregrounding these judgments, we offer a conceptual lens for understanding how students engage in co-creative sensemaking with AI in design contexts.

## Summary
Naik et al. investigate how design students develop and exercise judgment when working with AI tools in design contexts — specifically examining the "invisible" cognitive and evaluative work that students perform when assessing AI-generated outputs, navigating uncertainty, and deciding when to accept, modify, or reject AI contributions. The study identifies gaps between student self-assessment of their AI-mediated design process and the actual quality of their design decisions, with implications for how design education should scaffold AI-assisted studio work.

## Key Concepts
- Instrumental judgment in AI-assisted design
- Invisible cognitive work in human-AI collaboration
- Design decision-making under uncertainty
- Student self-assessment and metacognition
- AI literacy in design education

## Theoretical Framework
Draws on design cognition research, Gray et al.'s instrumental judgment framework, and HCI literature on AI-assisted creative work; frames judgment as a developmental capacity that must be explicitly cultivated in studio settings.

## Methods
Qualitative study with design students at Purdue; protocol analysis and interview methods to surface judgment processes during AI-assisted design tasks; comparative analysis between novice and more experienced students.

## Main Arguments
- Students working with generative AI often treat AI outputs as authoritative rather than as one design input requiring critical evaluation
- The evaluative work of judging AI outputs is "invisible" — it is not scaffolded or assessed in most curricula, so students develop it idiosyncratically
- Effective design judgment in AI contexts requires domain knowledge, reflective capacity, and explicit experience with AI failure modes
- Educators must make the invisible judgment work visible through critique structures and explicit reflection prompts

## Limitations
- Single institutional study; design judgment development may vary significantly across disciplines and program structures
- AI tool capabilities change rapidly, potentially outpacing findings about specific judgment challenges
