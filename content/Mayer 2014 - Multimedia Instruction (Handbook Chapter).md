# Mayer 2014 - Multimedia Instruction (Handbook Chapter)

**DOI:** [10.1007/978-1-4614-3185-5_31](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4614-3185-5_31)

## Summary
Mayer's 2014 handbook chapter synthesizes three decades of research on multimedia instruction, presenting multimedia learning as instruction combining words (printed or spoken text) and pictures (graphics, animation, video). Drawing on the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML), Mayer reviews evidence for twelve evidence-based principles of multimedia design and discusses boundary conditions under which each principle holds. The chapter bridges basic cognitive science and applied instructional design, arguing that learning from words and pictures together can exceed learning from words alone when the design aligns with the learner's cognitive architecture.

## Key Concepts
- **Multimedia instruction**: Any instruction combining words and pictures in any presentation medium
- **Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML)**: Dual-channel (auditory/visual), limited-capacity, active-processing model of how people learn from words and pictures
- **Twelve principles**: Coherence, signaling, redundancy, spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity, segmenting, pre-training, modality, multimedia, personalization, voice, and image principles
- **Boundary conditions**: Principles that hold for novices may not hold for experts; low-complexity material may not require segmenting
- **Generative processing**: Active cognitive effort to build a coherent mental representation from words and pictures

## Theoretical Framework
Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning, integrating Baddeley's dual-channel working memory model, Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory, and Mayer's prior multimedia learning research. Frames learning as sense-making activity constrained by cognitive architecture.

## Methods
Review of experimental studies testing each of the twelve principles, drawing on Mayer's laboratory and dozens of replications by independent researchers. Meta-analytic evidence where available.

## Main Arguments
1. Multimedia learning is supported by robust experimental evidence when design aligns with cognitive architecture
2. The twelve principles identify both productive (what to include) and counterproductive (what to avoid) design choices
3. Individual differences (prior knowledge, spatial ability) moderate multimedia effects; principles are not universal
4. Generative learning—actively organizing and integrating—is more productive than passive reception of multimedia

## Limitations
Predominantly laboratory research with short, bounded learning episodes; ecological validity for complex, sustained learning is less established. Most research tests constrained multimedia materials rather than authentic e-learning environments.
