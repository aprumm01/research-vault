# The Cultures of Contemporary Instructional Design Scholarship, Part One: Deve...

## Summary
Willis (2011, *Educational Technology*) surveys the major intellectual traditions shaping contemporary instructional design scholarship, focusing on approaches grounded in behavioral and cognitive science foundations. The paper identifies how ID scholarship has evolved from its behaviorist roots through cognitive information processing approaches, and examines the theoretical assumptions, research methods, and practical applications associated with each tradition. Willis argues that ID scholarship reflects multiple distinct cultures that share vocabulary but hold fundamentally different assumptions about knowledge, learning, and design.

## Key Concepts
- **Behavioral foundations of ID**: Programmed instruction, task analysis, behavioral objectives, mastery learning—the ID field's origins in Skinnerian behaviorism
- **Cognitive science foundations**: Information processing theory, schema theory, cognitive load theory, and worked examples applied to instructional design
- **ID as scholarship vs. ID as practice**: The distinction between academic research on instructional design and the practical design work of practitioners
- **Multiple cultures**: Willis's argument that "instructional design" encompasses distinct scholarly communities with different epistemological commitments
- **Behavioral-cognitive synthesis**: How mainstream ID absorbed cognitive science while retaining behavioral design elements (objectives, assessment, systematic process)

## Theoretical Framework
Kuhnian analysis of paradigms and scholarly cultures applied to the ID field; Willis argues the field contains multiple paradigms in tension rather than a single unified framework.

## Methods
Analytical literature review and historical synthesis; examines foundational texts, theoretical commitments, and research methods associated with behavioral and cognitive ID traditions.

## Main Arguments
1. ID scholarship is not a unified field but a collection of scholarly cultures with different epistemological foundations and research traditions
2. The behavioral and cognitive traditions share a commitment to systematic, scientific approaches to instructional design but differ in their models of learning
3. Cognitive science has substantially displaced behavioral psychology in academic ID, but behavioral elements persist in practice
4. Understanding the cultural diversity of ID scholarship is prerequisite to productive dialogue across traditions

## Limitations
Part One of a series; this installment covers only behavioral and cognitive traditions, not constructivist, postmodern, or critical traditions. Willis's "cultures" framework may oversimplify complex theoretical relationships within traditions.
