# Exploring Resistance and Other Oppositional Responses to AI

## Metadata
- **Authors:** Eric P.S. Baumer, Inha Cha, Vera Khovanskaya, Rosemary Steup, Janet Vertesi, Richmond Y. Wong
- **Year:** 2025
- **DOI/URL:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3715070.3748295
- **Source PDF:** [[Exploring Resistance and Other Oppositional Responses to AI.pdf]]

## Overview
This workshop paper (CSCW Companion 2025) gathers researchers and practitioners who study and/or engage in opposition to the proliferation of AI technologies. The organizers adopt an inclusive conceptualization of AI to assemble diverse participants and perspectives. The workshop challenges the sociotechnical imaginary that AI adoption is inevitable, creating space for a range of oppositional stances from resistance to avoidance to outright refusal.

## Research Overview
- **Research question:** What forms of opposition to AI exist, how are they enacted, and how can researchers and practitioners collectively organize to exercise meaningful agency over AI adoption?
- **Methodology:** Workshop-based — participatory, interactive activities; submissions describe work studying or engaging in oppositional practices
- **Context:** Interdisciplinary, spanning organizational contexts, higher education, professional software development, and creative fields; CSCW Companion 2025, Bergen, Norway

## Theories of Knowledge
- **Epistemological stance:** Critical theory and STS (Science and Technology Studies) tradition
- **Key frameworks:**
  - [[Suchman]]'s critique of the "thingness" of AI — warning against presuming a singular ontological agent
  - [[Narayanan and Kapoor]]'s three-type AI typology: prediction, generation, content moderation
  - [[Crawford]]'s materialist/critical perspective on AI as infrastructure
  - [[AI Solutionism]] — Morozov's concept challenged throughout
- **Knowledge construction:** Opposition and resistance are treated as empirical phenomena worthy of systematic study, not as anomalies to explain away

## Central Arguments
1. Not everyone is equally enthusiastic about AI, yet sociotechnical imaginaries frame AI adoption as inevitable — this framing forecloses meaningful choice.
2. The "thingness" of AI must be resisted: no single definition should dominate; AI is constructed socially and politically.
3. Opposition takes many forms beyond resistance: avoidance, irritation, protest, friction, dissidence, subterfuge, alterity, destruction — all are legitimate objects of study.
4. Resistance itself may inadvertently reify current conceptions of AI by accepting its terms of engagement.

## Evidence
- Case evidence drawn from professional journalists, scientific researchers, and screen writers/entertainment unions who have developed guidance constraining AI use
- OpenAI's stated stance that superintelligent AI is "inevitable" and the only way to control it is to be its creator — used as a primary example of inevitability framing
- Workshop outputs: a living annotated bibliography across domains; a practical guide with context-sensitive tactics for challenging AI's perceived inevitability

> "Although AI is a human-created phenomenon, no individual or group seems to have the power to say 'Yes' or 'No' to AI." (Baumer et al., 2025, p. 1)

## Conclusion
The workshop aims to shift AI discourse from inevitability toward collective agency. By assembling scholars across domains, it catalyzes connections and produces actionable resources — an annotated bibliography and a guide for oppositional tactics — to help individuals and communities exercise genuine choice about AI.

## Bias Check
- Workshop paper — not empirical; the organizers' framing is explicitly critical and skeptical of AI adoption, which may limit perspectives that view AI positively
- Small scope (workshop summary); findings are prospective, not based on completed research
- Lack of diversity in named examples (primarily US/Western professional contexts)

## Discussion Questions
1. Is resistance to AI always productive, or can it calcify existing power structures by accepting the terms AI sets?
2. How does the concept of [[non-use]] differ from resistance — what does it mean to simply opt out?
3. In what professional contexts is collective oppositional action most feasible?
4. How do we study resistance without romantizicing it or framing it as inherently progressive?
5. What role does [[design fiction]] play in imagining AI alternatives — does it reinforce or expand the space of possibilities?
