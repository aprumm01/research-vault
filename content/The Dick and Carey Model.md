# The Dick and Carey Model:

## Summary
Walter Dick's "The Dick and Carey Model: Will It Survive the Decade?" reviews the Dick and Carey systematic instructional design model's evolution over 20 years, assessing its resilience in the face of constructivist criticism, alternative ID textbooks, and declining academic interest in instructional design. Dick traces how the model adapted to incorporate constructivist elements in its 1996 edition while maintaining its systematic process orientation. He evaluates the model's future prospects in light of technology changes, emerging tools, and shifting epistemological commitments in the field.

## Key Concepts
- **Dick and Carey Model**: A linear, systems-based instructional design model with stages: needs assessment, instructional analysis, learner analysis, performance objectives, assessment, instructional strategy, instructional materials, formative evaluation, revise instruction, summative evaluation
- **Constructivist adaptation**: The 1996 edition's incorporation of constructivist principles while preserving systematic process
- **Objectivist vs. constructivist models**: The epistemological debate underlying competing approaches to ID practice
- **Long-term impact assessment**: Dick's evaluation of the model's continued relevance for both academic programs and industry practice
- **Alternative ID texts**: The emergence of competing instructional design frameworks that challenge the Dick and Carey model's dominance

## Theoretical Framework
Reflective practitioner analysis; Dick evaluates his own model's strengths and limitations through the lens of field developments and theoretical debates of the 1990s.

## Methods
Analytical review and retrospective assessment by the model's principal author; draws on review of editions, field developments, and constructivist critiques.

## Main Arguments
1. The Dick and Carey model has demonstrated durability because its systematic process is compatible with diverse theoretical orientations
2. Constructivist criticism misunderstands the model as prescribing objectivist outcomes when it prescribes only a design process
3. The 1996 edition's incorporation of constructivist elements represents a genuine adaptation rather than mere surface modification
4. The model's future depends on whether academic programs continue to teach systematic ID and whether practitioners see value in process-oriented design

## Limitations
Author's perspective naturally inclines toward defense of the model; independent evaluations may reach different conclusions about the model's adaptability. The paper was written in the 1990s; subsequent developments (e-learning, mobile, AI) test the model's flexibility further.
